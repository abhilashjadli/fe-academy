## 2. Technique catalogue

### Galton board with per-bounce ±1 arrows summed, then mass dropping
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt/galton_board.py` (`GaltonBoard`, `BiggerGaltonBoard`, `NotIdenticallyDistributed`)
- Topic illustrated there: CLT as "a sum of many small independent nudges"
- What moves / what the viewer does: one ball falls step by step; at each peg two faint arrows (+1/−1) appear and the chosen one stays bright; the arrows fly to a corner and add up to the bucket label. Then Pascal-triangle fractions appear on the pegs one row at a time via `TransformFromCopy` from the parents (the (n−1,k−1)+(n−1,k) split is shown, then merged). Finally 250–800 balls drop with `LaggedStart` and `stack_ratio` shrinks so the pile stays on screen; a Gaussian curve is stretched to the pile's height. `NotIdenticallyDistributed` draws a little fan of arrows on each peg to show the per-bounce distribution.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: that a bucket is literally a sum, that binomial coefficients are *paths*, and that the bell shape is an emergent pile, not a formula.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2 — the whole chapter's spine; C2 — the same board *is* the binomial tree (replace ±1 by up/down factors, buckets by terminal prices); B1 — random walk as the ball's x-position; A1 — "normal arises from many small shocks".
- Notes on execution: the single-ball step-through before the flurry is what makes it teach; the sum-in-the-corner is the key move. Shrink stack height rather than clipping. Playing peg sound was used for rhythm; on the web, a click per bounce is cheap and effective.

### Dice-sum simulation with speed ramp and self-rescaling histogram
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt/dice_sims.py` (`DiceSimulation.run_one_sum`, `scale_brick_height`), variants `SimulationWithUShapedDistribution`, `SimulationWithExpDistribution15Dice`
- Topic illustrated there: sampling distribution of a sum, for any starting distribution
- What moves / what the viewer does: the source distribution is a small bar chart top-left; N dice appear one by one with the chosen bar flashing; "Sum = 23" counts up; a brick flies into bucket 23 on the big axis. Three slow samples, ten at 0.5 s, 200 instant, then thousands with `still_frame=True`. When bricks reach the top, all bricks are stretched to 10 % height in one animation.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: that a histogram is a pile of individual outcomes, and that the shape stabilises as the count grows, regardless of the dice's own shape.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2, A1 (start from a skewed/fat-tailed source and watch the sum normalise — or not, for Cauchy-like tails), E1 (P&L histogram being built from scenarios), E2 (Monte Carlo convergence: the empirical mean settling).
- Notes on execution: the speed ramp is essential — slow enough to understand one sample, fast enough to see the law. Expose "source distribution" as editable bars (see Limiting distributions below).

### Exact sum distribution from a product grid sorted into columns
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt/main.py` (`DiceSumDistributions`, "Grid of dice to bar chart")
- Topic illustrated there: distribution of two dice, exactly
- What moves / what the viewer does: a 6×6 grid of dice pairs; each anti-diagonal (same sum) is highlighted, then the squares slide into a column over the sum's bar, so the bar chart is built out of the cells themselves; labels "k/36" appear with the numerator counting the cells.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: counting outcomes = measuring probability; the triangle shape of a 2-dice sum is obvious once cells are stacked.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2 (counting → binomial), C2 (two-step tree outcomes), A2 (joint distribution table; independence as product of marginals).
- Notes on execution: keep the cells as the *same objects* when they move; the eye must track them.

### Realign, rescale, overlay: showing the limit shape
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt/main.py` (`DiceSumDistributions` → `realign_distributions`, `rescale_distributions`, "Map bell curve over bars")
- Topic illustrated there: sums of n dice converge after centring and scaling
- What moves / what the viewer does: a vertical stack of n = 1…7 distributions with mean lines and ±σ lines; the camera pans down the stack; then all axes fade, the bars shift so the means align, then stretch so the σ's match, and copies of one bell curve are dropped onto each.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: that "converges to normal" means "after subtracting μN and dividing by σ√N", because you watch the subtraction and division happen.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2, A1, B3 (√t scaling of Brownian increments; self-similarity), C2 (tree → lognormal as steps shrink).
- Notes on execution: `LimitingDistributions` (same file) is the interactive version: a top "Random variable X" bar chart that can be *changed* (random, U-shaped, exponential) while the bottom plot shows the standardised sum for a chosen N; N is a `ValueTracker` stepped 2→50. This two-panel "change the input, watch the invariant" is the strongest CLT explorable in the repo.

### Formula build-up locked to graph morph (e^x → e^{−x} → e^{−x²} → N(μ,σ))
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt/main.py` (`BuildUpGaussian`)
- Topic illustrated there: where every symbol of the normal density comes from
- What moves / what the viewer does: `TransformMatchingTex` morphs the formula while `Transform`/`bind_graph_to_func` morphs the graph in the same `play`: e^x, flip to e^{−x} (glow dots run down the decay), e^{−|x|} (jagged, flashed at the kink), e^{−x²}; a red constant c with a slider display; "e is not special" by swapping the base; shaded area with a,b trackers (area = √π); then 1/√π, σ, μ enter one at a time.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: each constant's *job* — c sets width, 1/√(2π) sets area, σ, μ are rescale/shift — because only one thing changes at a time.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2/A1 (normal density), C3 (N(d1), N(d2) as areas), A1 (lognormal density by the same build-up: apply exp to x), 0.1 (same pattern for adding Taylor terms).
- Notes on execution: the "area under the curve between a and b" with a draggable pair of bounds and a live decimal (`RuleOfThumb` in the same file, 68–95–99.7) is reusable on its own for VaR quantiles (E1).

### Variance as (probability-weighted) squares on the axis
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt/main.py` (`MeanAndStandardDeviation`, "Show squares")
- Topic illustrated there: Var(X) = Σ p(x)(x−μ)²
- What moves / what the viewer does: for each bar, a square whose side is the distance from the bar to the mean line is drawn, then squashed vertically by the probability; toggling between a low- and high-variance distribution redraws them live (`always_redraw`).
- What it makes visible that text cannot: why outliers dominate variance (area grows with the square) and what "weighted by probability" does to the picture.
- Our chapters that could use it: A1 (variance, kurtosis as fourth-power version), A5 (why squared returns are the volatility estimator), E1 (ES vs VaR weighting).
- Notes on execution: do the "toggle distribution" version, not the static picture.

### Variance adds: two distributions, their convolution, σ annotations

### Samples pushed through a function to a second axis, with a live mean marker
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt_proof/main.py` (`DirectMGFInterpretation`)
- Topic illustrated there: E[e^{tX}] as the mean of transformed samples
- What moves / what the viewer does: 30 sample dots on the x-axis, vertical lines up to the graph of e^{tx}, horizontal lines to the y-axis where red dots land; they fly to a single tip marking their average; then t oscillates (`t_tracker` driven by sin(time)) and the whole picture re-renders.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: that an expectation of a function of X is "map the sample, then average", and how the weighting changes with t.
- Our chapters that could use it: B5 (change of measure: weighting samples by e^{tx}/M(t) is an Esscher tilt — the same picture with the dots' *opacity* as the new weights is Girsanov in miniature), A1 (moments, MGF), E2 (importance sampling), C1 (expected payoff = push terminal prices through the payoff and average).
- Notes on execution: keep the dots as the same objects across t changes; the viewer should watch them crawl.

### Taylor series added term by term against the graph, with the formula prefix boxed
- Source: `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2023/clt_proof/main.py` (`ExpandCGF`, "Approximations") and `/home/claude/3b1b/videos/_2021/matrix_exp.py` (`IntroduceTheComputation`, "Taylor series example": terms of e² as a bar chart)
- Topic illustrated there: log(1+x) ≈ x − x²/2 + …; e² = Σ 2^n/n!
- What moves / what the viewer does: a rectangle around the formula grows to include one more term while the yellow approximation curve is swapped for the next-order polynomial against the blue true graph (13 orders). In the 2021 version each term 2^n/n! grows as a bar from the baseline with its value counting up, so the bars' total height visibly converges.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: where the approximation is good (near the expansion point) and how it spreads outward; that the terms shrink after n = 2 because n! wins.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.1 — this is the chapter; C4 (delta–gamma as the first two Taylor terms of the option price in S; show the error curve), B4 (Itô: why the second-order term survives), D1 (P&L explained by Taylor terms).
- Notes on execution: show the *error* (true minus approximation) as a shaded band, not only the curves; add a draggable expansion point.

## 3. Top 10 ideas for our chapters

1. **0.2 — Galton board with per-bounce ±1 arrows summed in the corner, Pascal fractions appearing row by row, then the mass drop and bell overlay** (`clt/galton_board.py`). Reuse the *same component* for C2 by relabelling arrows as up/down factors.
2. **0.2/A1 — "Change the input distribution, watch the standardised sum stay normal"**: two-panel explorable with editable source bars and an N slider (`clt/main.py` `LimitingDistributions`), with the dice-sum simulation speed ramp (`clt/dice_sims.py`) as the opening.
3. **0.1 — Taylor terms added one at a time against the graph, formula prefix boxed, error band shaded, draggable expansion point** (`clt_proof/main.py` `ExpandCGF`; `matrix_exp.py` term bars). Then reuse for C4 delta–gamma and B4.
4. **A2 — Unit circle → correlation ellipse under the covariance map with eigenlines fixed** (`quick_eigen.py`), plus the 3-D joint-density surface with a diagonal slice (`convolutions2/diagonal_slices.py`) and the sample cloud that fades-not-deletes (`convolutions2/continuous.py`).
5. **A4 — Two linked planes: drag AR roots in/out of the unit circle, coefficients and a simulated series update** (`quintic/roots_and_coefs.py`) — the unit-root chapter in one explorable. Same pattern for D3 (Heston params ↔ smile).
6. **E2/B5 — Bertrand's three samplers side by side with running fractions** (`bertrands_paradox.py`): "the estimate converges to what the measure says". Extend with the e^{tx} sample re-weighting picture (`clt_proof/main.py` `DirectMGFInterpretation`) for Girsanov.
7. **F1 — Finite-difference stencil as a kernel walking the (S,t) grid with the output filling in, zoom-to-kernel** (`convolutions/discrete.py` `ImageConvolution`); plus continuous flip-and-slide for Feynman–Kac "price = payoff ⊛ kernel" (`convolutions2/continuous.py`).
8. **E1/E2/G3 — Histogram growing while the specimen (day's P&L vs VaR; path; fold) streams past in the corner, with a running mean** (`wordle/scenes.py` `ShowScoreDistribution`).
9. **B3/D1 — Zig-zag refinement that converges in position but not in length** (`visual_proofs/lies.py`) for quadratic variation and discrete hedging error; and the live-traced spring for OU mean reversion (`optics_puzzles/driven_harmonic_oscillator.py`).
10. **Break-the-model pattern — repeat an operation and display the shrinking slack until it hits zero** (`borwein/main.py` `MovingAverages`): template for put–call bounds, stationarity |φ| < 1, smile arbitrage bounds, radius of convergence.
