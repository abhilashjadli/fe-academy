## 2. Technique catalogue

### T01 Recursive splitting of a proportion bar (the "brick row") [build-up]
- Source: `_2018/eop/reusables/brick_row.py` (`BrickRow`, `SplitRectsInBrickWall`), `_2018/eop/chapter1/brick_row_scene.py`, `_2018/eop/chapter1/entire_brick_wall.py` (`EntireBrickWall`)
- Topic illustrated there: distribution of #tails after n coin flips.
- What moves / what the viewer does: a unit-width bar is the whole sample space. Each flip draws dashed lines splitting every brick in half (one half heads-coloured, one tails), then adjacent same-count bricks merge by colour into bricks of width C(n,k)/2^n. Rows are stacked downward for n = 0..20 to make a "brick wall"; one brick is highlighted with its probability as a percentage; a final 9-second exponential camera zoom into the corner of the wall.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: probability is *width*; binomial coefficients are what you get when you keep halving and re-merging; the whole wall is Pascal's triangle drawn to scale, so the thinning of the tails is physically visible.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2 — build the binomial exactly this way before the Galton board; C2 — the binomial tree's terminal distribution as a bar that splits per step (widths = risk-neutral probabilities, not ½); B1 — random walk marginal at step n.
- Notes on execution: keep the bar width fixed and let bricks get thin — the viewer should feel the squeeze. Colour-grade bricks by k (`graded_color`) so the merge step is legible. Merging is done by replacing the row with a freshly built one (not by animating individual bricks), which is cheap and reads fine.

### T02 Bar rotates into a histogram, then rescales as n grows [transformation/morph → zoom/continuum-limit]
- Source: `_2018/eop/chapter1/morph_brick_row_into_histogram.py` (`GenericMorphBrickRowIntoHistogram`, `MorphBrickRowIntoHistogram3/20/100/500`)
- Topic illustrated there: binomial → bell curve.
- What moves / what the viewer does: bricks space out, each rotates 90°, drops onto a baseline as a bar; an outline polygon is drawn over the bars; y-guides labelled k/8 appear; covering rects fade in one by one while a "total area = 0.125 → 1.000" decimal counts up. For n = 20, 100, 500 the chart is then stretched in x and y about its base so it fills the frame, with axis labels shifting along.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: a histogram is literally the sample-space bar rotated; total area 1 is conserved through the rotation; "convergence to the normal" is a rescaling, not a new object.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2 (CLT), A1 (why the normal is the default; later contrast with fat tails by doing the same rotation for a heavy-tailed step), C2 (binomial → lognormal terminal distribution under rescaling), E2 (histogram of Monte Carlo outcomes building as paths finish).
- Notes on execution: the stretch-about-point trick (bars and outline stretched by the same factor, labels shifted by (scale−1)·x) is the whole effect; do not redraw. Show the area counter — it is the thing learners miss.

### T03 Doubling-and-shifting stacks build Pascal's triangle into a bell [build-up]
- Source: `_2018/eop/combinations.py` (`StacksApproachBellCurve`, `BuildUpFromStart`, `BuildFiveFromFour`)
- Topic illustrated there: C(n,k), why the binomial is bell-shaped.
- What moves / what the viewer does: a row of bars with integer labels is copied; copy shifts half a step right, original half a step left; copies drop onto the originals and stack, labels add (1,3,3,1 → 1,4,6,4,1); bars are recoloured on a gradient and rescaled to fit; repeated 30 times.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: the recursion C(n,k)=C(n−1,k−1)+C(n−1,k) *is* a convolution with {½,½}; the bell appears from nothing but shift-and-add.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2; C2 — the binomial lattice is exactly "shift by u or d and add probability mass", and with p≠½ the shift-and-add becomes weighted; B1 — random walk distribution evolving; A4 — MA(q) as convolution of white noise.
- Notes on execution: label-width guard (`min_scale_val`) matters when numbers get long; after ~10 iterations switch labels off and let shape carry it.

### T04 Recombining lattice with a highlighted sample path; rescaled lattice of fixed width [geometric-reinterpretation]
- Source: `_2018/eop/pascal.py` (`PascalNetScene`, `RescaledPascalNetScene`), `_2018/eop/reusables/coin_flip_tree.py` (`CoinFlipTree`)

- Notes on execution: colouring edges by position gives free visual encoding of "where mass is"; keep stroke thin and let overlap do the density.

### T05 Hierarchical aggregation of a simulation (coins → 100-blocks → 10,000-blocks) [sampling/simulation + zoom/continuum-limit]
- Source: `_2018/eop/chapter1/million_flips.py` (`MillionFlips`)
- Topic illustrated there: law of large numbers, 1,000,000 flips.
- What moves / what the viewer does: 100 coins appear one by one in a 10×10 grid with "# flips / # heads" counters incrementing; the grid shrinks into one square whose fill colour encodes the heads proportion; 100 such squares fill a new grid (each adds 100 flips to the counters); those shrink into one square, and so on. Colour variance visibly drops at each level (proportions drawn from N(0.5, 0.05) then N(0.5, 0.005)).
- What it makes visible that text cannot: 1/√n shrinkage as fading colour contrast across three scales; the counters keep the viewer anchored to actual numbers.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2 (LLN/CLT), E2 — Monte Carlo standard error shrinking as 1/√N, blocks = batches, colour = batch mean; A5 — realised variance estimates over longer windows; E1 — backtesting hit rates.
- Notes on execution: do not actually simulate 10⁶ draws; draw block proportions from the correct normal. Keep the "zoom-out" as a scale-down-into-one-cell animation — it is the entire argument.

### T08 Unit-square area model: prior splits one axis, conditionals split the other; posterior rectangles reshape [geometric-reinterpretation]
- Source: `_2018/eop/chapter1/area_model_bayes.py` (`IllustrateAreaModelBayes`), `_2018/eop/bayes.py` (`UpdatePokerPrior`, `GeneralizeBayesRule`, `MusicExample` on `SampleSpaceScene`), `_2018/eop/bayes_footnote.py` (`TryUnitSquareVisual`), `_2018/eop/independence.py` (`MeaningOfIndependence`, `AccurateProductRule`)
- Topic illustrated there: independence, conditional probability, Bayes' rule, the medical-test paradox.
- What moves / what the viewer does: the unit square is divided horizontally in ratio P(A):P(not A); each strip is then divided vertically by P(B|A) and P(B|not A) with braces/labels; the "A and B" rectangle grows/shrinks as P(B|A) changes and the formula terms copy themselves out of the braces and into P(B|A)=P(A and B)/P(A); for Bayes the two "evidence" rectangles are pulled out and re-laid side by side so the posterior is visibly one rectangle's share of the pair. The poker scene lets the prior and each conditional be "tweaked" with the posterior updating. The footnote scene also shows the failure mode: with a 0.001 prior the strip is too thin to see ("Hard to see").
- What it makes visible that text cannot: independence = the vertical cut is at the same place in both strips; Bayes = comparing two rectangle areas, not a formula; the base-rate fallacy is a *thin strip*.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.2; A2 — conditional distributions and dependence; B5 — change of measure as re-weighting the areas of outcomes (P vs Q rectangles over the same sample space; Radon–Nikodym = height ratio); G1 — Bayesian view of regularisation; E1 — P(loss > VaR | regime).
- Notes on execution: `SampleSpace.divide_horizontally/vertically` with braces is the reusable API. Letting formula tokens fly out of the diagram's labels into the equation is the key move (ReplacementTransform of label copies). When a probability is tiny, switch to T09.

### T09 Grid of 1,000 individuals/quizzes split by conditionals (sorting, shifting, fading subsets) [sampling/simulation + comparison]
- Source: `_2018/eop/independence.py` (`ThousandPossibleQuizzes`, `CorrelationsWith35Percent`), `_2018/eop/bayes_footnote.py` (`ShowRestrictedSpace`)
- Topic illustrated there: independence vs correlation; 1-in-1000 disease with a 1 % false-positive rate.
- What moves / what the viewer does: a 25×40 grid of tiny icons. Split 1: top 80 % shift up and recolour their first question green, bottom 20 % red, braces "800 / 200". Split 2 (independent): each group splits left/right in the same ratio; (correlated): the ratio differs across groups, and the "all three wrong" corner ends up visibly larger than 0.35³ would give. In the disease scene the 1 sick icon and 10 false positives are highlighted, everyone with a negative test fades out, leaving 11 icons of which 1 is sick.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: a conditional is a *subset*; correlation is a ratio that changes from one subset to the next; posterior = count among survivors.
- Our chapters that could use it: A2 — dependence and tail dependence (icons = days, colour by "both assets fell"); E1 — historical VaR: 1,000 scenario days sorted by loss, worst 10 highlighted; H1 — worst-of baskets: fraction of scenarios where any one asset breaches; G1 — confusion matrix as a grid; G3 — leakage (train/test subsets).
- Notes on execution: `sort` the subgroup by x before shifting so the split reads as a clean cut; keep icons identical so only colour and position carry meaning.

### T16 Function as a map from one number line to another; zoom until it is a pure stretch (= derivative) [transformation/morph + zoom/continuum-limit]
- Source: `_2018/alt_calc.py` (`NumberlineTransformationScene`, `IntroduceTransformationView`, `TalkThroughXSquaredExample`, `ZoomInOnXSquaredNearOne/Three/OneFourth/Zero`, `ZoomInMoreAndMoreToZero`, `XSquaredForNegativeInput`)

- What moves / what the viewer does: two parallel number lines (Inputs above, Outputs below); sample dots on the input line, arrows to their images, then the dots slide down to their images (ghosts remain). A `ZoomedScene` window follows a chosen input; inside it, local tick marks (0.9, 1.0, 1.1) and a dense dot cloud map to output ticks and the viewer sees a uniform stretch by 2; near 0 the stretch factor goes to 0 after repeated 10×/100×/1000× zooms; for negative input the local map flips.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: a derivative is a local magnification factor; non-linearity is visible as uneven dot density; zooming is how "local linearity" is earned.
- Our chapters that could use it: 0.1 — Taylor: first zoom shows stretch (1st order), unzoom slightly shows bending (2nd order); B4 — Itô: the zoomed stretch picture *fails* for a Brownian input because √dt wiggle survives the zoom, motivating the ½f″ term; C4 — delta as local stretch of payoff-vs-S; F2 — forward-mode AD as propagating the stretch factor; A1 — lognormal as exp stretching the line unevenly.
- Notes on execution: ghost dots at the inputs are essential; zoom window with its own mini number line (`get_local_coordinates`) is the reusable component. In a web build: a draggable magnifier.

### T17 Repeated application of a map: convergence to a stable fixed point, flight from an unstable one; cobweb diagram [animate-the-object]
- Source: `_2018/alt_calc.py` (`AnalyzeFunctionWithTransformations`, `StabilityAndInstability`, `RepeatedApplicationGraphically`, `ShowRepeatedApplication`, `NumericalPlayOnNumberLineFromOne`)
- Topic illustrated there: x → 1 + 1/x converges to φ; −1/φ is an unstable fixed point; |f′| < 1 decides.
- What moves / what the viewer does: dense dots on the input line map to the output line, the output line slides up to become the new input, repeat 10×; dots pile up at φ. Zoomed view near each fixed point shows arrows pointing in (|f′|<1, "Stable") or out (|f′|>1, "Unstable"). Separately a cobweb: dashed vertical to the graph, horizontal to y=x, repeat, with a pi creature reacting.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: stability is literally local contraction; the decimal sequence (2.718→1.368→1.731→…→φ) and the geometry are the same thing.
- Our chapters that could use it: A4 — AR(1) as a contraction map (|φ|<1 stationary, φ=1 unit root = no contraction, |φ|>1 explosive) with cobweb + noise; B3 — mean reversion (OU drift pulling dots toward μ); F2 — Newton/fixed-point calibration iterations (implied vol by Newton as a cobweb); C3 — implied vol solver convergence; G1 — gradient descent as repeated map.
- Notes on execution: the "slide the output line up to become the input" trick keeps the iteration continuous. For AR(1), add jitter to the dots each step and the stationary distribution appears for free.

### T20 Local probe with a live read-out: circle of arrows around a point, divergence decimal; tiny-step dot-product view [zoom/continuum-limit + other]
