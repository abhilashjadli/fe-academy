## 2. Technique catalogue

### T1. Live finite-difference evolution of a graph with a colour-mapped physical object
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part2/heat_equation.py` — `BringTwoRodsTogether.update_graph`, `ShowEvolvingTempGraphWithArrows`, `color_rod_by_graph`
- Topic illustrated there: 1D heat equation; two rods at 90° and 10° touch and equilibrate.
- What moves / what the viewer does: The temperature curve is advanced each frame by an explicit scheme (`y_change = alpha * second_deriv * dt`, 500 sub-steps per frame, Neumann boundary). A rod of 20 squares beneath the axes is recoloured from the curve every frame; a clock counts seconds. Small vertical arrows at 20 x-positions have length ∝ the local second derivative, so the curve "shows where it is about to move".
- What it makes visible that text cannot: That ∂T/∂t is literally the curvature; concave-down parts sink, concave-up parts rise, straight parts sit still; the object and its graph are the same thing.
- Our chapters that could use it: **F1** (explicit FD scheme is the animation loop itself; pair with instability when Δt too big) · **C4** (theta as "curvature × ½σ²S²": animate V(S) relaxing under the BS PDE with gamma arrows) · **B6** (density diffusing; Feynman-Kac) · **B3** (transition density spreading). Tag: **animate-the-object**.
- Notes on execution: Use the real numerical scheme as the renderer — nothing is faked, so the viewer's intuition is calibrated against the true dynamics. Keep sub-stepping so the visible Δt can be large while the scheme stays stable. The colour rod is what makes a graph feel physical; our analogue is a colour-mapped strip of option values along the S axis.

### T2. Discretise → neighbour rule → difference quotient → continuum limit
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part2/heat_equation.py` — `DiscreteSetup.discretize/show_nieghbor_rule/focus_on_three_points/write_second_difference`, `TransitionToContinuousCase.show_temperature_difference`, `DiscreteGraphStillImage{1,Fourth,Tenth,Hundredth}`; `_2019/diffyq/part3/discrete_case.py`
- Topic illustrated there: Deriving ∂²T/∂x² from "each point moves toward the average of its neighbours".
- What moves / what the viewer does: The smooth curve is replaced by dots at integer x with dashed drops to rod pieces. Three dots are isolated; the middle one gets an updater `d2.shift(0.25*(T1 − 2T2 + T3) dt)` with a velocity vector coloured by sign, so it visibly drifts toward the neighbour average as the neighbours are dragged. Horizontal lines mark T1, T3 and their average. Then `dx_tracker` shrinks 1 → 0.01 while a ΔT/Δx triangle collapses onto the tangent line; stills at step 1, ¼, 1/10, 1/100 show the dots becoming a curve.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: The second derivative as "(neighbour average − me)", and the limit Δx→0 as a continuous shrinking rather than a definition.
- Our chapters that could use it: **F1** (central differences; the explicit scheme is the neighbour rule; show stability by letting Δt/Δx² exceed ½ and watching it explode) · **C2** (binomial tree as the discrete rule; Δt→0 convergence to BS) · **0.1** (difference quotients → derivative) · **B3** (random walk → Brownian motion as the same limit). Tag: **zoom/continuum-limit**.
- Notes on execution: A single tracker controlling Δx with everything else `always_redraw`n is the whole trick. Show the coarse version first and let the learner feel the rule before taking the limit.

### T3. Stacking time slices into a 3D surface, with a sweeping slice plane
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part2/heat_equation.py` — `TalkThrough1DHeatGraph.show_surface`; `_2019/diffyq/part3/temperature_graphs.py` — `OceanOfPossibilities.show_solution`, `SineWaveScaledByExp.show_decay_surface`, `TemperatureGraphScene.get_const_time_plane`
- Topic illustrated there: T(x, t) as a function of two inputs.
- What moves / what the viewer does: The 2D graph is rotated to stand in a 3D frame; copies of the curve at t = 0, 0.5, 1, … are `TransformFromCopy`-laid behind it along a new t axis; a translucent `ParametricSurface` fills in; a white slicing plane and the rod slide along t for 10 s with the camera orbiting. The input plane is emphasised separately ("2d input space"). Boundary lines on the surface edges are drawn as `ParametricCurve`s and the surface is deformed live by sliding Fourier amplitudes.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: That a PDE solution is a surface, a "snapshot" is a slice, boundary conditions are the surface's edges, and the time evolution is a plane sweeping through.
- Our chapters that could use it: **C3/C4** (V(S,t) surface; delta/gamma are slopes along S, theta along t; sweep t to expiry and watch the kink appear) · **D2** (implied-vol surface built from smile slices at each maturity) · **F1** (the FD grid is exactly this surface sampled) · **D1** (P&L surface). Tag: **geometric-reinterpretation**.
- Notes on execution: Build the surface by transforming copies of the 2D curve so the learner never loses the link to the graph they already understand. Keep surface fill low opacity and draw slice curves with full stroke.

### T4. ∂x vs ∂t nudges on a surface as shrinking tangent triangles
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part2/heat_equation.py` — `ShowDelTermsAsTinyNudges.get_line_group/show_del_x/show_del_t`, `ContrastXChangesToTChanges`
- Topic illustrated there: Meaning of partial derivatives.
- What moves / what the viewer does: On the 3D surface, a right-angled triangle (dx leg, dT leg, hypotenuse) is `always_redraw`n from `x_tracker` and `dx_tracker`; the tracker shrinks 0.5 → 0.01 and the ∂ symbols resize with the legs. Same along t with the camera turned 80°.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: Which direction each partial moves in, on the same object.
- Our chapters that could use it: **C4** (delta/theta as two nudges on V(S,t)) · **B4** (Itô: the dt leg vs the dW leg, and why (dW)² ≠ 0) · **C5** (cross partials vanna/charm as nudging twice). Tag: **zoom/continuum-limit**.
- Notes on execution: Always keep the symbol glued to its leg via updater so the algebra and geometry stay fused while shrinking.

### T5. Many-particle Brownian box with a traced path, re-plotted as a price chart
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part2/staging.py` — `BrownianMotion.update_particles/get_traced_path`, `BlackScholes.add_graph`
- Topic illustrated there: Brownian motion as a PDE example; Black-Scholes cameo.
- What moves / what the viewer does: 100 small elastic particles (mass 1) and one large one (mass 10–100) collide in a box (momentum-conserving update per frame); the big particle's path is traced in blue. In `BlackScholes`, the particles are hidden and a chart draws y-coordinate-of-the-big-particle against time under the BS equation — "a stock chart that is literally Brownian".
- What it makes visible that text cannot: That the jittery path is an emergent effect of many invisible small shocks; and the honest mapping "price = one coordinate of a buffeted particle".
- Our chapters that could use it: **B3** (open the chapter with this; then replace collisions with Gaussian increments and show the same statistics) · **B1** (random walk origin) · **A4** (random walk vs mean reversion: add a spring to the big particle) · **D3** (jump diffusion: occasionally a large particle hits). Tag: **sampling/simulation**.
- Notes on execution: The "re-plot a coordinate as a time series" move is cheap and powerful; keep both views on screen with a dashed link so the viewer sees that the chart is the particle.

### T6. Linearity: basis shapes with live scalar dials and a weighted-sum panel
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part4/three_d_graphs.py` — `ShowLinearity.change_scalars`, `CombineSeveralSolutions.show_infinite_family/show_sum/show_time_passing`, `CycleThroughManyLinearCombinations`; `_2019/diffyq/part3/temperature_graphs.py` — `BreakDownAFunction`
- Topic illustrated there: Superposition of heat-equation solutions; Fourier decomposition.
- What moves / what the viewer does: Five small 3D axes each hold one basis surface cos(kx)e^{−αk²t} with a yellow "× 0.81" `DecimalNumber` under it driven by a `ValueTracker`; a big axes below shows the sum, `always_redraw`n from the same trackers. Scalars are animated to new values; copies of the top graphs fly down into the sum; then a shared time tracker sweeps slice planes on all six at once. `BreakDownAFunction` runs it backwards: an arbitrary function fans out into its cosine terms with "+" signs and check marks propagating back up.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: That a sum of solutions is a solution, with the weights as the only knobs; and that "decompose, solve each, recombine" is a visual pipeline.
- Our chapters that could use it: **H2** (every structured product = weighted sum of vanilla payoffs: top row = call/put/digital/barrier payoff panels with dials, bottom = the product; this is the chapter's core illustration) · **E3/A2** (PCA on yield curves: level/slope/curvature components with dials summing to a curve) · **C1** (put-call parity and payoff building) · **G2** (autoencoder/PCA reconstruction). Tag: **build-up**.
- Notes on execution: One tracker per component, everything derived; animate the dials rather than the result so the learner attributes change to the weight.

### T7. Partial sums added one term at a time, with the previous sum ghosted
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part3/staging.py` — `FourierSeriesIllustraiton`; `_2019/diffyq/part4/staging.py` — `ShowInfiniteSum.show_many_inputs_in_parallel/follow_single_inputs`
- Topic illustrated there: Fourier series converging to a step function.
- What moves / what the viewer does: Each new sine term is drawn in its own colour, the matching term in the formula is boxed, then the term `ReplacementTransform`s into the new partial sum while the old partial sum fades to a thin ghost. Separately, 21 dots at fixed x-values move vertically as `n_tracker` runs 0 → 100 partial sums, each dot converging to its limit; then one input is followed with braces under each term showing its numeric contribution.
- What it makes visible that text cannot: Convergence as a *process* per input point, including Gibbs overshoot near jumps.
- Our chapters that could use it: **0.1** (Taylor series: add terms one at a time, ghost the previous polynomial, show dots at several x converging or diverging outside the radius) · **E2** (Monte Carlo running average converging, 1/√N) · **C2** (binomial price converging to BS as n grows, with the odd/even oscillation). Tag: **build-up**.
- Notes on execution: Ghosting the previous state is what lets the eye measure the increment. Colour-code term ↔ curve ↔ formula identically.

### T8. Series terms as tip-to-tail vectors in the complex plane
- Source: `_2020/ldm.py` — `ExpMeaning.get_vectors`; `_2019/diffyq/part2/fourier_series.py` — `FourierCirclesScene.get_rotating_vectors/update_vector/add_path_fader`; `_2019/diffyq/part4/fourier_series_scenes.py` — `AddVectorsOneByOne`; `_2020/18S191/dft.py`
- Topic illustrated there: exp(iθ) = Σ(iθ)ⁿ/n!; Fourier series as rotating arrows.
- What moves / what the viewer does: 20 arrows, the n-th of length θⁿ/n!, laid head to tail from the origin, all `always_redraw`n from `theta_tracker`; as θ grows to 2π the chain curls and its tip stays on the unit circle. ### T9. Integral as centre of mass of sampled outputs, refined
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part4/fourier_series_scenes.py` — `IntegralTrick.show_center_of_mass/get_sample_dots`; `_2019/diffyq/part4/staging.py` — `NumericalIntegration`

### T13. Live-drawn simulated graph against a dashed analytical prediction
- Source: `_2019/diffyq/part1/pendulum.py` — `ThetaVsTAxes.get_live_drawn_graph`, `LowAnglePendulum`, `MultiplePendulumsOverlayed`, `IntroducePendulum.tweak_length_and_gravity`

- What moves / what the viewer does: The simulated pendulum appends (t, θ) each frame to a VMobject; a dashed θ₀cos(√(g/L)t) is pre-drawn.
