**Working title:** *Three jobs with no formula*
**Standfirst:** Three things a desk does every morning that no formula does for it. It decides, on simulated paths, when an option should be exercised — by fitting a curve through the paths themselves. It turns a model's knobs until its prices match the screen's — and finds a long valley of settings that match equally well. And it gets every sensitivity of a price in one pass, not by bumping each input and repricing, but by differentiating the pricing program itself, number by number. Each is arithmetic you already own — least squares from A3, paths from E2, the chain rule — pointed at a job that used to need a formula.
**Assumes:** E2 (paths, error bars, the same seed), F1 (the American rule on a grid, its 4.28), D3 (three stories fitted to one smile), C4 (delta, vega, theta), A3 (least squares). Each restated where used.

## Close (short)
**So what are the three jobs?** — Longstaff–Schwartz: at each date, regress what waiting paid on where the paths were, exercise where exercising beats the fit; a low-biased estimate in the limit, optimistic at small samples, as good as its basis. Calibration: least squares between the model's prices and the screen's, with a valley where one expiry cannot tell the knobs apart; you calibrate because you hedge with the instruments you fit (Wilmott), and you pick a point in the valley for reasons the smile does not give. Automatic differentiation: carry derivatives through the program — forward mode one pass per input, reverse mode (the adjoint, Giles & Glasserman 2006) one pass for all; exact, where a bump has a best step and a floor; on Monte Carlo, differentiate the path (and never bump with fresh random numbers). One formula at most, under Figure 3: the chain rule as a product along the tape. Collapsed: the LSM algorithm in six lines and its two biases; Andersen's boundary parametrisation; the basis-function question (Glasserman Table 8.1); the dual-number rules; the pathwise delta e^{−rT}1{S_T > K}S_T/F and why it fails for a digital; the optimal bump h* (Glasserman Table 7.1). "Where it is used": every exotics desk prices Bermudans and callables by LSM and risks them with adjoints; every model on the site was calibrated the way Figure 2 shows; Huge–Savine's differential machine learning is the tape fed to a network. Last line, no formula; pull to G1 ("a regression that chooses its own basis — and the day it fits the noise").

## Why this shape
- Each figure is one artefact the reader can audit: eight paths, one grid truth, one real smile, one real call.
- The textbook examples are the two that have checkable truths (Hull's tables; F1's grid); everything else is the site's own data.
- The failure modes are on the page (small-n optimism, the valley, the bump's floor, fresh-seed noise), not in caveats.
- Lightness: one formula; the adjoint is credited and described, not implemented.
