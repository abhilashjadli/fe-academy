**Working title:** *A line through the bad days*
**Standfirst:** In August 2007 a bank's finance chief explained a week of losses with "25-standard-deviation moves, several days in a row". Under the bell curve a seven-standard-deviation day comes once in three billion years; his came on Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. The arithmetic was fine. The curve was wrong. This page is about the one risk number every desk reports — how much you lose on 99 days in 100 — how it is drawn through a pile of past days, what it leaves out, and the only honest test of it: counting how often the next year crossed the line. A town does not build its flood wall for the worst flood imaginable; it builds it for the hundred-year flood, and then watches the high-water marks.

## Close (short)
**So what is VaR?** — a line through a pile of past days at the 99th-worst position, reported in rupees; a number for the person who cannot read a thousand Greeks. Three ways to draw the line: use the days themselves (historical), fit a bell and read 2.33 widths (parametric), or simulate tomorrows and sort them (Monte Carlo — Hull's six steps in one paragraph, pointed to E2). Expected Shortfall is the average of what lies beyond the line, and it is the measure that adds up. Backtesting is the only test: count the crossings. One formula at most, under the bell toggle: VaR = 2.33 σ, ES = 2.67 σ. Collapsed: the √N scaling and where it fails (10-day historical ₹7.41 lakh against √10 × ₹2.65 lakh = ₹8.39 lakh); Kupiec's count test; Cornish–Fisher on Raj's book (skew −2.9 → ₹8.0 lakh against the exact ₹7.35 lakh); Wilmott's "which volatility" (slope from implied, move from realised); EVT in two sentences with McNeil's 1987 number; sub-additivity and coherence named. "Where it is used": every risk report (the "VaR 99 % 1d" column beside the desk's P&L and the line "exceptions YTD"), Basel capital (three times the 10-day 99 %), position limits. Last line, no formula; pull to E2 ("sample, reprice, sort — and how wide the error bar on a worst day is").
