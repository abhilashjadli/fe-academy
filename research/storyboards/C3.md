**Working title:** *The one dial the screen does not set*
**Standfirst:** Five things decide what a call costs. Four of them are printed on the screen: the index, the strike, the date, the interest. The fifth is not printed anywhere — and yet the screen says 249.15, which means somebody turned it. This page hands you that dial, lets you find the setting that prints 249.15, opens the machine to show why it prints what it prints, and then turns the dial on every strike at once until the market's own opinion appears as a curve.
**Assumes:** you can read a table, you know what an average is and what interest is. C1's row (call 249.15, put 249.60 at 22,600) and C1's forward (22,603) are restated in the first figure, so the page stands alone.
**Characters:** Raj, once, in Figure 3 — the bookmaker from B5 who sells the call and lays off the bet. Nobody else.

## Close (short)
**So what is Black–Scholes?** — the price of a call is the average payoff over a bell-shaped cloud of futures centred on the forward, shrunk by interest; the famous line is that average written out, two areas under the cloud and a discount; the only input the screen does not fix is the width of the cloud, and "implied volatility" is turning that one dial until the formula agrees with the screen. One formula at most, the one from Figure 2. Collapsed: Hull's 42/40 example in full (d₁ 0.7693, d₂ 0.6278, 4.76 / 0.81); the limits (wobble → 0 gives max(F − K, 0) discounted, ₹2.99 here; index → ∞ gives F − K discounted); the dividend/currency/futures variants as a three-line table; why the PDE and the cloud agree (B6 in one line). "Where it is used": every quote in vol units is this dial; a vol surface is Figure 4's strip for every expiry (D2); the slope 0.51 is C4's delta; Raj's ticking is D1's whole day. Last line, no formula; pull to C4 ("the three glyphs in Raj's sentence have names, and a trader watches all three move at once").

## Why this shape
- The dial comes first and unexplained because that is how the number is met on a desk: a trader quotes "15.9" before anyone writes N(d₁). Doing it in round 1 makes the formula an explanation of something the reader has already done.
- The cloud is C2's thousand-step board at the limit (C2 said so), so the "expectation" derivation is performed by counting lit dots; the hedging derivation is told by Raj in four panels — performed would overlap D1.
- The game at the end pays twice: the skew appears from the reader's own hands, and the screen's own IV column — which a fresher would have trusted — is shown to be a model output with a wrong input, fixed with one dial. Nothing on the page is simulated; every price is from the 23 Oct screen.
- Lightness: one formula, written once, after the reader has built every piece of it; the PDE appears only in words; the collapsed panel holds everything else.
