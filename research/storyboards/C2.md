**Working title:** *Pick any probability*
**Standfirst:** A stock is 20. In three months it will be 22 or 18 — you choose how likely. A ticket pays if it is 22. Set the odds to anything you like and I will tell you what the ticket is worth without looking at your dial. The trick works every time, it is the whole of option pricing, and by the end of this page you will have done it on a thousand-step board.
**Assumes:** you know what an average is and what interest is. That is all.
**Characters:** none. The board is the stage.

## Close (short)
**So what is the binomial model?** — a board on which the price can only go to two places per step, so a ticket's payoff can be copied exactly with some shares and some borrowed cash, and the ticket must cost what the copy costs; the chance of going up never enters. The copy's cost happens to equal an average under one particular weight — the one that makes the stock a fair bet at the interest rate — and on a thousand-step board that average becomes the Black–Scholes number. One formula at most: price = discounted average of the payoff under that weight. Collapsed: Δ, the weight p, why u = e^{σ√δt} matches the wobble, the n-step sum with 0.2's path counts as weights, the odd/even averaging trick. The chapter's own last line, no formula; pull to C3 ("the formula has two derivations, and the smooth curve at six months is one of them").
