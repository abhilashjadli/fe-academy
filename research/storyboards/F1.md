**Working title:** *Every point moves toward its neighbours*
**Standfirst:** Predict how a cup of tea cools by assuming it loses a fixed share of its excess heat every minute, and you will be right — at one-minute checks. Check once an hour and apply the hourly share in one go, and your prediction overshoots the room and rings. That is the whole of this page. The Black–Scholes equation says how the value of an option, drawn as a curve over the index, relaxes as you walk back from expiry: every point moves toward the average of its neighbours, faster where the index is higher. A computer does exactly that, one neighbour at a time, and gets the formula's 249.15 without the formula. Take too long a step and it gets a billion rupees. There is a second way of stepping that cannot overshoot, a third that is accurate to the square of the step — and one extra line that prices the option the formula cannot.
**Assumes:** B6 (the equation as a heat rod), C3 (the real call at 249.15), C2 (stepping back from expiry), C4 (the bend is the second difference). Each restated where used.

## Close (short)
**So what is a finite-difference solver?** — the equation, obeyed one neighbour at a time on a grid. Explicit: tomorrow from today's neighbours, cheap, and unstable past a step that shrinks with the square of the grid spacing. Implicit: today's neighbours solved together, never unstable, first order in the step. Crank–Nicolson: the average of the two, second order on a smooth payoff, and apt to ring at a corner unless started gently. American exercise: the same grid plus "never below the payoff", and the line where that rule binds is the exercise boundary. One formula at most, under Figure 0: V_t + ½σ²F²V_FF − rV = 0, in words "the clock pays for the bend, less the carry". Collapsed: the three stencils, the stability condition δt ≤ δF²/(σ²F²_max), the tridiagonal solve in one paragraph, Hull's trinomial reading (the weights are probabilities), the kink and Rannacher's half-steps, the ln S grid, and why a wide grid starves an 11-day option (1.4 nodes per standard deviation on 0–45,200, 18 rupees low; 6 on the 101 dots shown and 50 on the 800-node grid of 18,000–28,000). "Where it is used": every pricing library's PDE engine (barriers and Americans on stocks run on it daily; Heston and local vol on a two-dimensional grid); the reason Glasserman says Monte Carlo is "not competitive" in one dimension; chapter H1's barriers are this grid with one more boundary condition. Last line, no formula; pull to F2 ("when the grid is impossible — many assets, a path-dependent rule — the American rule has to be done on paths").
