**Working title:** *How sure are you?*
**Standfirst:** In chapter C3 you grew a cloud of two thousand index levels for 3 November and read the price of a call off it — 249, the average of what the call paid in each dot. Nobody called it Monte Carlo. Grow the cloud again with different random numbers and the price is 256; again, 241. This page is about that wobble: why a random average carries an error bar you can compute from the same draws, why halving it costs four times the dots, the two tricks that shrink it for free, and the two jobs — a payoff that depends on the whole road, a book with two risks that move together — for which throwing random tomorrows is the only method there is. (Stanislaw Ulam invented it in 1946 wondering how often solitaire comes out; the name is the casino.)
**Assumes:** C3 (the cloud and the dial), B5 (the average is taken under the weight that makes the forward fair), A2 (correlation is a shear), D1 (Raj's book), E1 (VaR is a rank in a pile). Each restated where used.

## Close (short)
**So what is Monte Carlo?** — compute an average by drawing random tomorrows, and compute how wrong you probably are from the same draws. The error falls like one over the square root of the draws in one dimension or a thousand, which is why it loses to a grid for one variable and wins for many. The tricks are ways of throwing fewer, better darts — a table (one row each, what it attacks): antithetic (the odd part of the payoff), control variate (the part that moves with something you can price exactly), stratified and quasi-random (the gaps — Hull's Sobol' picture as the one still), importance sampling (the rare region), moment matching (the sample's own mean and width). One formula at most, under Figure 1: SE = s/√n. Collapsed: the exact log step vs Euler, and why D3's breathing wobble needed Milstein; common random numbers for Greeks; the twelve-uniforms normal and its missing tails; Cholesky for n assets; Hull's six-step Monte Carlo VaR. "Where it is used": every structured-products desk's overnight batch (ten thousand paths a trade and a standard-error column nobody reads), E1's VaR when the book is nonlinear, F's portfolio of defaults, H's barriers and autocallables which have no other pricer. Last line, no formula; pull to E3 ("the matrix Cholesky factorised has axes of its own — and a yield curve has three").
