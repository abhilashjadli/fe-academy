# A5 Volatility: EWMA, ARCH/GARCH, MLE fitting, forecasting and evaluation

_R1 deep-research note, 2026-10-23. Options for discussion, not decisions. Companion catalogue entries: Part C §A5; Part B B7, B28, B56, B59, B62, B63, B74, B75, B81._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

Volatility is not something you can read off a chart, it is a guess about how big tomorrow's move could be — and the best guess leans on recent big moves, remembers them for a couple of weeks, and slowly drifts back to normal, so a volatility model is nothing more than a rule for how fast to forget.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (the hook candidates — 3, each one paragraph: a puzzle, a story with stakes, an everyday object; which is strongest and why)

**Puzzle — "One of these is real. The other is the same numbers shuffled."** Two return charts side by side: the daily returns of an index, and the *same* returns in random order. Same histogram, same mean, same standard deviation, same fat tails — every summary statistic a fresher knows is identical. Yet anyone can tell them apart in two seconds: the real one has storms (1929–33, 1987, 2008 bunch together), the shuffled one is uniform fuzz. The thing the eye sees and the statistics miss is volatility clustering — Mandelbrot's sentence, "large changes tend to be followed by large changes, of either sign, and small changes tend to be followed by small changes" (Wikipedia, opened). The puzzle isolates the phenomenon with nothing but a shuffle button.

**Story with stakes — the limit that was set on a calm month.** A risk manager sets a desk's position limit from the last year's average volatility: 0.6 % a day. A bad week arrives: −2 %, +1.8 %, −3 %. The yearly average barely moves (three days out of 250). An EWMA with λ = 0.94 has already doubled its estimate by the third day; a GARCH(1,1) says "and it will take about 17 days to fade back" (Hull's yen data: α + β = 0.9602, half-life ln 0.5/ln 0.9602 ≈ 17 days, p. 383). The limit was wrong for the week that mattered, and the fresher is the one asked "what is the vol right now?" The stakes are a VaR (E1) and an option price (C3) that both take one number called σ.

**Everyday object — the weather forecaster's umbrella.** Nobody forecasts tomorrow's rain from the yearly average; after a stormy day you pack an umbrella, and after a dry fortnight you stop. Storms come in spells. A forecaster who weights yesterday heavily and last month lightly is doing EWMA; one who also knows "this town gets about 100 rainy days a year, spells end" is doing GARCH. Both are rules for forgetting.

**Strongest: the shuffle puzzle as the opener, the umbrella as the running metaphor, the limit story as the stakes before the fitting section.** The puzzle is a one-button reveal that makes the whole chapter's object visible before a single formula; the umbrella explains λ and ω in plain words; the story explains why anyone pays for the right λ.

**Common misconceptions (from forums, StackExchange, textbooks' own warnings)**

- "Volatility is the standard deviation of the returns." It is a latent quantity; the sample sd is one estimator with equal weights (Hull p. 373; Tsay p. 98 "not directly observable").
- "Returns are independent, so there's nothing to model." They are *uncorrelated but dependent* — Tsay Fig. 3.1 — the ACF of r² is the fingerprint.
- "A big σₜ means tomorrow will be a big move." It means a big move is more *likely* (Tsay p. 103).
- "EWMA and GARCH are different models." EWMA is GARCH(1,1) with ω = 0 and α + β = 1 (Hull p. 376; O'Connell); it has no long-run level and its forecast is flat (Hull p. 383).
- "α + β close to 1 is a fit problem." It is the normal finding (Tsay: 0.97 on S&P monthly, p. 119; Hull: 0.96 on yen, p. 383); it means long memory, and at exactly 1 the unconditional variance stops existing (IGARCH, Tsay p. 122).
- "The forecast was wrong because realised r² was far from σ²." A single squared return is a one-sample estimate of a variance — "not an adequate measure" (Tsay p. 121); evaluate against a 25-day realised average (Hull p. 375) or standardised-residual autocorrelations (Hull Table 17.2).
- "MLE finds *the* answer." The likelihood ridge is flat along α + β; Excel Solver "is liable to provide a local rather than global maximum" (Hull p. 379 fn. 10); variance targeting barely changes the objective (22,063.5274 vs 22,063.5763, p. 380).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept A — "Real or shuffled?" (guess-then-reveal + annotated real artefact)

*Mode:* guess-then-reveal, then an annotated real chart (B60; Tsay Fig. 1.2/3.1 as the template).

1. Two daily-return panels, unlabelled. "One is a real index. One is the same numbers shuffled. Click the real one." Summary tiles under both are identical (mean, sd, min, max, kurtosis).
2. Reveal. The real one is annotated: the 1929–33 spell, Oct 1987, autumn 2008, March 2020 as shaded "storms". Caption: Mandelbrot's sentence.
3. The fingerprint: under each panel the ACF of r (both ≈ 0) and the ACF of r² (real: positive bars for 20+ lags; shuffled: nothing). "Uncorrelated, but not independent."
4. One more shuffle button: the learner shuffles as often as they like; the clusters never come back. Caption: "the order *is* the information."
5. The honest line: "we never see volatility; we see returns." A dashed unknown σₜ line is drawn *behind* the real returns and labelled "what every model in this chapter is guessing".

*Makes visible:* clustering as the only thing the shuffle destroys; the r² ACF as its signature; volatility as latent. *Cost:* low — one real return series (S&P daily, freely available), a shuffle, an ACF. *Risk:* none for comprehension; the risk is anticlimax if the storms are not annotated with dates a fresher has heard of.

### Concept B — "The leaky bucket" (physical simulation / scrubbable animation)

*Mode:* a physical mechanism animated beside the return bars, with one dial per section (B7 kernel comb as the second panel; B62 one-knob-two-consequences).

1. A bucket of water. Its level is today's variance estimate. Each day a cup is poured in: the cup's size is yesterday's squared return. A hole in the bottom leaks a fixed *fraction* of the level every day. Caption: "level today = (1 − leak) × level yesterday + (leak) × cup". That is EWMA with λ = 1 − leak (Hull eq. 17.7).
2. Dial: the hole size. Small hole (λ = 0.97): the level barely notices one big cup — smooth, slow. Big hole (λ = 0.80): the level jumps and drains — jumpy, fast. Beside it the weight comb (1−λ)λᵏ redraws (the 3b1b kernel, printed once, then run) with the half-life marked: 11.2 days at 0.94, 22.8 at 0.97. The acadifi numbers appear as a check: one −2.3 % cup takes 1.80 % to 1.835 % at λ = 0.94 and to 1.91 % at 0.80.
3. "Play the storm": the real 2008 returns are poured day by day; the bucket rises over a week and drains over a month. The equal-weight bucket (a 250-cup average) is shown as a ghost that barely moves — the limit-setter's mistake.
4. GARCH = one more pipe. A thin tap drips a constant ω each day. Now the level has a *home*: when nothing is poured, it settles where the drip balances the leak: ω/(1 − α − β). Hull's yen: V_L = 0.00004422 → 0.665 %/day (p. 380). The three dials now read: drip ω, cup fraction α, keep fraction β. The weight comb now decays at rate β and sits on a floor (Hull p. 377).
5. "Turn off the drip" → EWMA again, no home; "set α + β = 1" → the level never settles (IGARCH). "Let α + β > 1" → the bucket overflows (Hull p. 383: "mean fleeing").
6. Forecast as draining: freeze the pouring, let the bucket run forward. The level glides toward V_L along Hull's Fig. 17.2 curve; the readout at 10 days (0.74 %) and 100 days (0.67 %) from the current 0.77 % (Hull p. 383). Averaging the glide over an option's life gives the term structure: 12.03 % (10 d) … 10.65 % (500 d) (Table 17.3, p. 384).

*Makes visible:* λ, α, β, ω as pipe sizes; the long-run level as a balance of drip and leak; the forecast as draining; EWMA/GARCH/IGARCH as three settings of one bucket. *Cost:* medium — a bucket animation, the EWMA/GARCH recursion, the comb, one real series. *Risk:* the bucket is a metaphor for a linear recursion on *variance*, and the on-screen level must be labelled "variance" with a √ tile beside it labelled "volatility", or freshers will read the bucket as the price. Water mixing is not a fraction-leak; the caption must say "a fraction leaks, not an amount".

### Concept C — "Be the fitter" (game with a score, then the optimiser)

*Mode:* a solve-by-hand game with per-day likelihood bars and a score, then "+1 step" of the computer's optimiser (B74, B75, B81).

1. Fixed data: 250 days of returns as bars (|r| shown as grey spikes). Three dials ω, α, β. The σₜ band is drawn over the spikes and moves as the dials move.
2. The score: under each day a small bar, −ln vᵢ − uᵢ²/vᵢ (Hull eq. 17.12) — green when the band fits that day, red when the band is far too wide (wasteful) or too narrow (a spike pokes out). The score is the sum of the bars. Hull's Table 17.1 rows are the check: day 3 u = −0.004242, v = 0.00004355 → 9.6283; day 4 u = 0.009037, v = 0.00004198 → 8.1329.
3. The learner plays for a minute. Best score sticks. A "why is this day red?" hover shows the arithmetic (B81). Discovery the game forces: widening the band fixes the spikes but costs every calm day; the balance *is* the fit.
4. "Let the computer try": +1 step at a time on an (α, β) contour map with the dot sliding uphill (B75); the monitor shows the score rising each step; the ridge is visibly flat along α + β ≈ const. Hull's optimum appears: ω = 0.00000176, α = 0.0626, β = 0.8976, score 22,063.5763 (p. 379–380); the learner's best is shown beside it.
5. Two honest beats: "variance targeting" pins V_L to the sample variance and the score barely moves (22,063.5274, p. 380); "EWMA only" has one dial (λ = 0.9686, score 21,995.8377, p. 380) — fewer dials, lower score, and a sentence on when that is fine.
6. "Did it work?": the standardised residuals uᵢ/σᵢ replace the returns; their r² ACF is flat (Hull Table 17.2: Ljung–Box 123 → 8.2) — the model has eaten the clustering. Then the forecast is drawn forward against the next 60 days' 25-day realised vol (B59, Hull's own evaluation rule).

*Makes visible:* likelihood as a sum of per-day scores on fixed data; the optimiser as doing what the hands did; the flat ridge; evaluation as "did the clustering go away". *Cost:* medium–high — GARCH likelihood, a contour map, an optimiser with a step button, Hull's data or a close simulated stand-in. *Risk:* three dials at once is the sponsor's "rocket science" trigger; start with only λ (one dial, EWMA), unlock ω and β after the first score. The log-likelihood number (22,063) means nothing to a fresher — show the score as "points above the equal-weight baseline".

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right (formulas, assumptions) and reference numbers for tests, with their source (book + page)

**Returns and the latent variance.** uᵢ = (Sᵢ − Sᵢ₋₁)/Sᵢ₋₁ (Hull eq. 17.2, p. 373), mean assumed zero; equal-weight estimator σₙ² = (1/m) Σ uₙ₋ᵢ² (eq. 17.3). σₜ² = Var(rₜ | Fₜ₋₁) (Tsay eq. 3.2, p. 100).

**EWMA.** σₙ² = λσₙ₋₁² + (1 − λ)uₙ₋₁² (Hull eq. 17.7, p. 374); weights (1 − λ)λᵏ summing to 1; half-life ln 0.5/ln λ. Tests: Hull Example 17.1 (p. 375): λ = 0.90, σₙ₋₁ = 1 %, uₙ₋₁ = 2 % → σₙ² = 0.9·0.0001 + 0.1·0.0004 = 0.00013, σₙ = 1.14 %. acadifi (opened): λ = 0.94, 0.000324, −2.3 % → 0.000337 (1.835 %); λ = 0.80 → ≈ 1.91 %. Half-life at λ = 0.94: 11.20 days; at 0.97: 22.76; effective window 1/(1 − λ) = 16.7 at 0.94. RiskMetrics λ = 0.94 daily (Hull p. 375), 0.97 monthly (acadifi).

**ARCH(1).** aₜ = σₜεₜ, σₜ² = α₀ + α₁aₜ₋₁², α₀ > 0, 0 ≤ α₁ < 1; Var(aₜ) = α₀/(1 − α₁); kurtosis 3(1 − α₁²)/(1 − 3α₁²) > 3, needs α₁² < 1/3 (Tsay pp. 104–105). Test: Tsay eq. 3.11 (Intel monthly, p. 111): σₜ² = 0.0126 + 0.3526aₜ₋₁² → unconditional sd √(0.0126/0.6474) = 0.1394 (text prints 0.1394 using 0.352).

**GARCH(1,1).** σₙ² = ω + αuₙ₋₁² + βσₙ₋₁² = γV_L + αu² + βσ², γ + α + β = 1, V_L = ω/(1 − α − β), stable iff α + β < 1 (Hull eqs. 17.8–17.9, p. 376; Tsay eq. 3.15, p. 114). Weight on uₙ₋ᵢ² is αβ^{i−1} (Hull p. 377). Tests: Hull Example 17.2 (pp. 376–377): ω = 0.000002, α = 0.13, β = 0.86 → γ = 0.01, V_L = 0.0002 (1.4 %/day); with σₙ₋₁ = 1.6 % and uₙ₋₁ = 1 %: σₙ² = 0.000002 + 0.13·0.0001 + 0.86·0.000256 = 0.00023516, σₙ = 1.53 %. O'Connell (opened): ω = 0.000002, α = 0.08, β = 0.90, σ² = 0.0004, r = −3 % → 0.000434 (2.08 %); V_L = 0.0001; half-life ln 0.5/ln 0.98 = 34.3 days. Tsay eq. 3.18 (S&P monthly excess returns, p. 118): σₜ² = 0.000086 + 0.1216aₜ₋₁² + 0.8511σₜ₋₁²; unconditional variance 0.000086/(1 − 0.8511 − 0.1216) = 0.00315 (book prints 0.00314; p. 119 prints α̂₁ + β̂₁ = 0.9772 where the coefficients sum to 0.9727 — flag both as book rounding/misprints; the engine uses the coefficients).

**Maximum likelihood.** Per-day term −ln vᵢ − uᵢ²/vᵢ, maximise the sum (Hull eq. 17.12, p. 379); equivalently Tsay's −Σ[½ ln σₜ² + ½ aₜ²/σₜ²] (p. 107); start v₃ = u₂² or the sample variance (Hull p. 379; Tsay p. 116). Tests (Hull Table 17.1, p. 380): day 3: u = −0.004242, v = 0.00004355, term 9.6283; day 4: 0.009037, 0.00004198, 8.1329; day 5: 0.002687, 0.00004455, 9.8568; day 6: 0.011101, 0.00004220, 7.1529; day 2421: 0.003374, 0.00007626, 9.3321; day 2422: −0.017309, 0.00007092, 5.3294; day 2423: 0.000144, 0.00008417, 9.3824. Optimum ω = 0.00000176, α = 0.0626, β = 0.8976, Σ = 22,063.5763; V_L = 0.00000176/0.0398 = 0.00004422, √ = 0.665 %/day. Variance targeting: sample variance 0.00004341 (0.659 %), α = 0.0607, β = 0.8990, Σ = 22,063.5274. EWMA fit: λ = 0.9686, Σ = 21,995.8377 (all p. 380). The yen data file is not in the repo — these are reproduced only if the sponsor supplies it; otherwise the per-day term formula is tested on the listed (u, v) pairs, and the optimiser is tested on a simulated GARCH series with known parameters (recover within ±0.03 at T = 2,000).

**Forecasting.** E[σₙ₊ₖ²] = V_L + (α + β)ᵏ(σₙ² − V_L) (Hull eq. 17.13, p. 383; Tsay eq. 3.16 closed form, p. 115). Test: α + β = 0.9602, V_L = 0.00004422, σₙ² = 0.00006 (0.77 %): k = 10 → 0.00005473 (0.74 %); k = 100 → 0.00004449 (0.67 %) (Hull p. 383). Term structure: average of the k-day forecasts over N days, annualised ×√252 (eq. 17.14): N = 10 → average 0.00005745 → 12.03 %; Table 17.3: 10/30/50/100/500 days → 12.03/11.61/11.35/11.01/10.65 % (p. 384). After a 1 % rise in instantaneous vol (12.30 → 13.30 %, daily 0.84 %, 0.00007016): 10-day average 0.00006597 → 12.89 %; Table 17.4 increases 0.86/0.64/0.48/0.28/0.06 % (p. 385).
