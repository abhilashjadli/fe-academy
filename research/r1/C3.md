# C3 Black–Scholes in detail: lognormal stock, the formula, both derivations, implied volatility

_Phase R1 deep research note (sprint of 2026-10-22). Options for discussion with the sponsor, not decisions. Prerequisites assumed: C1 (payoffs, parity), C2 (binomial tree, risk-neutral q), 0.2 (normal), A1 (lognormal) — but the chapter re-draws the lognormal in its first figure so it stands alone._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

The price of a call is the average payoff over a bell-shaped cloud of possible future prices, shrunk by interest — and the famous formula is nothing more than that average written out, with two areas under the bell as its only moving parts; "implied volatility" is just turning the one dial you cannot observe until the formula agrees with the screen.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: "two numbers everyone agrees on, one nobody does."** Open with a real-looking quote line: stock 42, strike 40, six months, rate 10 %, and a call trading at 4.76. Ask: of the five things that set this price, which one is not printed anywhere? Strike and expiry are in the contract, spot and rate are on the screen; the fifth — how much the stock will wobble — is a guess. Natenberg's framing is exact: "Volatility is the one input into a theoretical pricing model which cannot be directly observed" (Natenberg p. 67). The puzzle pays off twice: first when the formula is shown to need only those five inputs, and again when implied vol is revealed as the market's answer to the question. Strong because it makes the invisible input the hero before any mathematics.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: the insurer who lays off bets.** A bookmaker who has sold a bet does not pray; he balances his book so that he is indifferent to the outcome. Natenberg opens his pricing chapter with the roulette table: the house's "theoretical value" of a bet is the probability-weighted payoff, and "in the long run, with the odds on his side, a trader can be fairly certain of making a profit" (p. 36, 69). Black–Scholes is the bookmaker's trick done continuously: sell the option, hold the right amount of stock, and the random part cancels. The stakes beat: Hull's institution that sold a call for $300,000 and faces a cost anywhere "from zero to over $1,000,000" if it does nothing (Hull p. 300). This hook motivates the hedging derivation, which is otherwise the hardest sell.

**Hook C — the everyday object: the Galton board / pinball maze.** Natenberg's Ch. 4 opens with a pinball maze — a ball drops through nails, 50/50 left or right at each, into troughs at the bottom (p. 51, Fig. 4-1). The troughs fill into a bell. Put a strike line between two troughs and the option's value is "every ball that lands to the right, times how far right it landed". The fresher has already met this object in 0.2 and the tree in C2, so the board becomes the lognormal with no new mathematics. Easiest of the three, but it is a re-use rather than a hook.

**Strongest: A, with B as the mid-chapter story.** A puts the mystery input first (easy to understand, genuinely interesting), and B gives the derivation a human reason to exist. C is used as the first figure, not as the hook.

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Book chapters read

- **Hull OFOD 5e, Ch. 12 "The Black–Scholes model", §12.1–12.11, book p. 234–251 (PDF 249–266).** Does well: the lognormal built from the SDE with Example 12.1 (S₀ = 40, μ = 16 %, σ = 20 %, 6 months → 95 % band 32.55–56.56, p. 235); §12.5 "Concepts underlying the differential equation" — the riskless portfolio is "a long position in 0.4 share, a short position in one call option", with the warning that it "is riskless for only a very short period of time … it must be adjusted, or rebalanced, frequently" (p. 241–242, Fig. 12.2); the seven assumptions listed cleanly (p. 242); risk-neutral valuation as a three-step recipe (assume μ = r, compute expected payoff, discount) with the honest caveat that it is "merely an artificial device" and that "it is fortunate that μ happens to drop out" (p. 245); Example 12.7, the worked S = 42 case (p. 248–249); the σ → 0 and S → ∞ limits (p. 248); §12.11 implied vol by bisection, c = 1.875, S₀ = 21, K = 20, r = 0.1, T = 0.25 → "try 0.20 … 1.76, too low … 0.30 … 2.10, too high … 0.25 … too high … implied volatility is 0.235" (p. 250–251), plus the warning that deep ITM/OTM implied vols "tend to be unreliable". Does badly for a beginner: the derivation (§12.6) is pure algebra with Itô assumed; N(d₂) is never interpreted in this chapter; no picture of the formula.
- **Natenberg (1994), Ch. 3 "Introduction to theoretical pricing models", p. 35–49.** The best plain-English on *what a price is*: the roulette bet whose "theoretical value" is 36/38 of a dollar; the five-price stock model (80, 90, 100, 110, 120 each 20 %) where the 100 call is worth (10 + 20)/5 = 6 (p. 39–41); the candle-in-a-dark-room metaphor for a model: it lights the room imperfectly but you can still find the furniture; the explicit list of inputs (exercise price, time, underlying price, interest, volatility) with the remark that "Of all the inputs required for option evaluation, volatility is the most difficult for traders to understand" (p. 49). Ch. 4 "Volatility", p. 51–79: the pinball maze (Fig. 4-1), "the underlying price as the mean of a distribution" — "When we enter the present price of an underlying instrument we are actually entering the mean of a normal distribution curve" and the mean is the *forward* (p. 60); the compounding tables (+12 % → $1,127.50, −12 % → $886.92) that make the lognormal skew a consequence of continuous compounding (p. 62–63, Fig. 4-7); the four assumptions in trader's words (p. 64); implied vol as "running the theoretical pricing model backwards" with Fig. 4-9's Knowns/Unknowns table (futures 98.50, 105 call, 3 months, r = 8 %, σ = 16 % → 0.96; market 1.34 → implied 18.5 %) (p. 72–73); "It is as if the marketplace were continuously polling all participants to come up with a consensus volatility" (p. 74); the weather-forecast analogy — historical (July in Chicago is hot), forecast (the radio), implied ("everyone outside is wearing a coat and carrying an umbrella") (p. 75). Does badly: everything is prose; no formula at all.
- **Wilmott, Paul Wilmott on Quantitative Finance, Ch. 5 "The Black–Scholes model" (book p. 91–99, PDF 112–120), Ch. 6 §6.3 (p. 102–103), Ch. 7 §7.1–7.2, 7.9 (p. 109–120, 130–132).** Does well: the two-line derivation Π = V − ΔS, dΠ = dV − Δ dS, "Notice that Δ has not changed during the time step; we have not anticipated the change in S" (p. 92), choose Δ = ∂V/∂S and "the randomness is reduced to zero" (p. 93), then no-arbitrage dΠ = rΠ dt; and the clearest sentence anywhere on μ: "The Black–Scholes equation contains all the obvious variables … but there is no mention of the drift rate μ. Why is this? Any dependence on the drift dropped out at the same time as we eliminated the dS component of the portfolio … if you and I agree on the volatility of an asset we will agree on the value of its derivatives *even if we have differing estimates of the drift*" (p. 94). §6.3 reads the PDE term by term as "reaction–convection–diffusion", with Fig. 6.1 showing the call payoff evolved under diffusion only, convection only, reaction only (p. 102–103) — the one picture of the PDE's parts I found in any book. §7.2 turns the PDE into the heat equation by three substitutions and gets the formula from the Green's function; §7.9 gives the Newton–Raphson implied-vol loop in VB with vega as the slope (p. 130). The assumptions list is honest about each one ("Delta hedging is done continuously: This is definitely impossible", p. 96). Does badly: no numbers; the Green's-function route is too much for a fresher.
- **Gatheral, The Volatility Surface, Preface p. xxiii and Ch. 1 opening p. 1–3.** One sentence to steal for the implied-vol section: "actual option prices rarely if ever conform to the predictions of the formula because the idealized assumptions required for it to hold don't apply in the real world. Consequently, implied volatility (the volatility input to the Black-Scholes formula that generates the market price) in general depends on the strike and the expiration of the option" (p. xxiii). Ch. 1 motivates stochastic vol with the 1987 crash and Fig. 1.2 (77 years of SPX daily log returns vs the normal, x-axis "extended to the left to accommodate" −22.9 %). This is the D2/D3 preview in two lines.

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

1. https://gregorygundersen.com/blog/2024/09/28/black-scholes — Gundersen, "An intuitive explanation of Black–Scholes". Skips the PDE; starts from lognormal returns; splits the formula into "two expected payments, one for the stock and one for the strike, each weighted by the probability of exercise"; Figure 4 is a truncated lognormal whose stock term is "its mean weighted by exercise probability". Why it works: it says N(d₂) is "just the probability that the call is exercised" and tells you not to interpret N(d₁) alone — read S·N(d₁) as one unit. Also: "assuming no arbitrage is analogous to assuming no wind or air resistance."
2. https://moontowermeta.com/a-visual-appreciation-for-black-scholes-delta/ — Moontower. One picture: the terminal density with the strike X; N(d₂) is "the probability mass to the right of X", S·N(d₁) "the sumproduct of all stock prices above X weighted by their probabilities". Then: borrow X·P(ITM) by selling a bond, the gap between the shares you can afford and the shares you need "is what the call option must be worth!" Why it works: the formula becomes a funding story.
3. https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/black-scholes-formula-explained — "d₂ is the risk-neutral probability of exercise", d₁ "is d₂ shifted up by one standard deviation", Black–Scholes as "the Rosetta Stone of options markets … the universal translator" for quoting prices in vol. Worked numbers S = 100, K = 105, T = 0.5, r = 5 %, σ = 20 % → d₁ ≈ −0.0975, d₂ ≈ −0.2389, call ≈ 4.58, put ≈ 6.99.
4. https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/implied-volatility-smile-numerical-methods-python — Brenndoerfer. Price is strictly increasing in σ ("This uniqueness guarantees that implied volatility is well-defined"); Newton update σₙ₊₁ = σₙ − (C_BS(σₙ) − C_mkt)/Vega with the tangent-line figure from σ₀ = 0.80 to 23.27 % in three steps; bisection ≈ 50 iterations vs Newton ≈ 4. Test case S = 100, K = 105, T = 0.5, r = 5 %, price 5.50 → 23.27 %.
5. https://forum.wilmott.com/viewtopic.php?p=176518 and https://forum.bionicturtle.com/threads/black-scholes-the-cumulative-normal-distribution-function-parameters-d1-and-d2.165 — practitioner threads. Harper: "N(d1) is not a probability, it is delta"; N(d₂) "concerns expiring in the money, not touching the strike at any point"; Clopinette: both are ITM probabilities under different numeraires, "implying probabilities from prices makes little sense unless you make up your mind on a particular numeraire." 6. https://risknet.de/elibrary/paper/the-illusions-of-dynamic-replication — Derman & Taleb. ### Common misconceptions

- "N(d₁) is the probability of finishing in the money." It is the delta; N(d₂) is the (risk-neutral) exercise probability; S·N(d₁) is a price-weighted area (Wilmott forum, bionicturtle, Moontower).
- "N(d₂) is the real-world probability." It is the probability with μ replaced by r; Hull p. 245 insists risk neutrality is "an artificial device"; Natenberg p. 102 says delta "is only an approximation of the probability since interest rate considerations … may distort this interpretation".
- "Why does the exercise probability fall when volatility rises?" (Wedler's question on the Wilmott thread) — for an ITM call, more vol pushes mass across the strike *both ways* and the lognormal median falls (−½σ²T). The lognormal figure answers it.
- "Implied vol is a forecast / is the market's mistake." Natenberg: it is a consensus *price* expressed in vol units, and "the implied volatility depends on the theoretical pricing model being used" (p. 73); stale inputs matter (same 1.34 price with the future at 99.25 implies 17.3 %, not 18.5 %).
- "The expected return μ must matter." Wilmott p. 94 and Hull p. 245: it drops out because it is cancelled by the hedge / already in S.
- "Higher strike with the same distance from spot should cost the same as the lower-strike put." Natenberg p. 63–64: under the lognormal "the 110 call will always have a greater value than the 90 put".
- "Delta hedging is riskless in practice." Hull p. 242 and Wilmott p. 96: riskless only instantaneously; rebalancing is the whole of C4/D1.

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "Guess the price" (mode: guess-then-reveal game, then symbol-into-picture derivation)

Beats:
1. The pinball board from 0.2 drops 500 balls into troughs labelled with prices 30…60 (Natenberg Fig. 4-1 with a price axis; spacing multiplicative so the troughs are the lognormal). A strike line at 40 is draggable.
2. Prompt: "A call pays what lands right of the line, minus 40. Guess its price." Slider for the guess; the learner commits (B60).
3. Reveal: every ball right of the line lights, its payoff drawn as a vertical bar; the bars' mean appears as a levelled line; then it shrinks by e^{−rT} with a bracket "interest for six months". The number 4.76 lands next to the guess.
4. Now the formula builds from the picture (B83 / `CreateFormulaFromDiagram`): the shaded fraction right of the line flies into **N(d₂)**; the strike flies into **K**; the bracket into **e^{−rT}**; the price-weighted average of the lit balls flies into **S·N(d₁)**. Each symbol keeps the colour of the piece of picture it came from for the rest of the chapter.
5. Drag S, σ, T, K: the board re-shapes (σ√T widens the cloud, (r − ½σ²)T shifts its centre — the lognormal from Hull eq. 12.3) and both areas and the price re-flow live, with d₁, d₂ printed as "how many widths the strike is from the centre".
6. Break-it beats: σ → 0 (cloud collapses to a spike at S·e^{rT}; price → max(S − Ke^{−rT}, 0), Hull p. 248); S → ∞ (both areas → 1, price → S − Ke^{−rT}).
7. The "two areas, one price" sentence is only now written as the formula.

Makes visible: that the formula *is* the picture; what N(d₂) is; why N(d₁) ≠ N(d₂). Costs: the lognormal pinball component (reuse from 0.2 with a log-spaced axis), a 1,500-ball sampler, flying-symbol choreography. Risk: the learner reads N(d₂) as a real-world probability — the caption must say "in the pretend world where the stock drifts at r", and the next concept explains why that world is the right one.

### Concept 2 — "The bookmaker who lays off bets" (mode: drawn-character story in four panels, with one live panel)

Beats:
1. Panel 1: a bookmaker (Natenberg's roulette house) has sold one call. A thought-bubble shows the payoff ramp. "If the stock goes up I lose. How much?" A small slope triangle on the price curve: 0.4 (Hull Fig. 12.2).
2. Panel 2: she buys 0.4 shares. Two bars — the option's loss and the shares' gain — for an up-tick and a down-tick; they cancel. Caption from Wilmott: "we have not anticipated the change in S."
3. Panel 3 (live): a toggle "tick the clock". Each tick the stock moves, the curve's slope changes, and she must buy or sell a sliver of shares (Hull p. 242: "0.4 today … 0.5 in two weeks … an extra 0.1 share"). The portfolio value line is flat; the bank account line grows at r. Caption: "A flat line that must grow like a bank account — that one sentence is the Black–Scholes equation."
4. Panel 4: the PDE written in words under the picture, each term a glyph: "time decay + ½·wobble²·bend + carry = interest" (links C4). Then the twist: "She never once asked whether the stock tends to go up. That is why μ is not in the formula." (Wilmott p. 94.)
5. Coda: Hull's institution that sold for $300,000 and could lose $1,000,000 naked — "this is why every desk has a hedger" (Hull p. 300).

Makes visible: why there is a hedging derivation at all, why μ vanishes, and that the PDE is a bookkeeping identity. Costs: four drawn panels plus one small live simulation (discrete rebalancing engine is shared with C4/D1). Risk: low — but it does not produce the formula, so it cannot stand alone.

### Concept 3 — "An annotated real quote, solved live" (mode: annotated real artefact + the reader solves it by hand)

Beats:
1. A reproduction of a dealer sheet in the style of Natenberg's Fig. 4-11 (gold futures 358.30, 10 weeks, r = 5.5 %, strikes 330–390 with settlement prices and implied vols 13.86–15.93 %) or his Fig. 6-24 (Deutschemark options, 22 May 1992, "Implied Volatility" column). Hover any cell → tooltip explains the column.
2. Pick the 360 call at 7.80. The five inputs appear in Natenberg's Knowns/Unknowns table (Fig. 4-9), with the volatility cell blank.
3. The learner turns a single σ dial (B74). The model price slides along a monotone curve; a gap bar shows |model − 7.80|. The snap at 13.89 % is the moment of equality.
4. "What the computer does instead of your hand": Newton (B75) — from σ₀ = 0.80 the tangent (vega) takes three steps to the root (Brenndoerfer); beside it, bisection halving a bracket (Hull p. 250–251: 0.20 → 0.30 → 0.25 → 0.235). An iteration counter.
5. Now solve every strike at once: the implied vols plot against strike and are *not* flat — a smile (Gatheral's one sentence). Caption: "the formula is wrong in a way the market has learned to speak; D2 is about that language."
6. The stale-input beat: change the underlying to 99.25 with the same option price and the implied vol moves from 18.5 % to 17.3 % (Natenberg p. 73) — "an implied vol is only as good as the inputs it was implied from."

Makes visible: that implied vol is a root, not a forecast; monotonicity; why the smile exists. Costs: low — one dial, one root-finder, a static table. Risk: a fresher may think the smile is a bug in the solver; the caption must pre-empt it.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, and reference numbers

Model (Hull Ch. 12; Wilmott Ch. 5): dS = μS dt + σS dW; ln S_T ~ N(ln S₀ + (μ − ½σ²)T, σ²T) (Hull eq. 12.3, p. 235); E[S_T] = S₀e^{μT} (12.4). Under pricing, μ → r.

Formula (Hull eq. 12.20–12.21, p. 246): c = S₀N(d₁) − Ke^{−rT}N(d₂); p = Ke^{−rT}N(−d₂) − S₀N(−d₁); d₁ = [ln(S₀/K) + (r + ½σ²)T]/(σ√T); d₂ = d₁ − σ√T. Risk-neutral expectation form: c = e^{−rT}E^Q[max(S_T − K, 0)]; N(d₂) = Q(S_T > K); S₀N(d₁) = e^{−rT}E^Q[S_T·1{S_T>K}].

PDE (Hull eq. 12.15; Wilmott eq. 5.6): ∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²∂²V/∂S² + rS∂V/∂S − rV = 0, final condition V(S,T) = payoff. Variants (Wilmott p. 97–98; Hull Ch. 13): dividend yield q → (r − q)S∂V/∂S and S₀e^{−qT} in the formula; currency q = r_f; futures q = r (Black 76). Assumptions (Hull p. 242): constant μ, σ; short selling; no costs/taxes; no dividends; no arbitrage; continuous trading; constant r.

Limits (Hull p. 248): σ → 0 ⇒ c → max(S₀ − Ke^{−rT}, 0); S₀ → ∞ ⇒ c → S₀ − Ke^{−rT}, p → 0.

Implied vol: solve c_BS(σ) = c_mkt; c_BS strictly increasing in σ (vega > 0) so the root is unique when c_mkt ∈ (max(S − Ke^{−rT},0), S). Newton: σₙ₊₁ = σₙ − (c_BS(σₙ) − c_mkt)/vega(σₙ), vega = S₀√T·N′(d₁) (Wilmott p. 130).
