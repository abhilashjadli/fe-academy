# C5 Greeks II: vanna, volga (vomma), charm, veta, speed, zomma, color — and how a desk reads them

_Phase R1 deep research note (sprint of 2026-10-22/23). Options for discussion with the sponsor, not decisions. Prerequisites assumed: C3 (the formula, implied vol as the one dial), C4 (delta = slope, gamma = curvature, theta = sinking, vega = the vol dial's slope). The catalogue flags this chapter as "nothing good found" anywhere; this note invents, grounded in Natenberg's own delta-vs-vol and delta-vs-time figures, which turn out to be vanna and charm drawn in 1994 without the names._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

Every one of these strange-named numbers is the same simple question asked twice — "if I nudge this, how much does *that* move?" — and the only two worth carrying in your head are "my delta drifts when vol moves" (vanna) and "my delta drifts when the clock moves" (charm), because those are the two ways a hedge you balanced this morning is quietly wrong by lunchtime.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the story with stakes: "all he did was change his mind."** Natenberg p. 110: "A trader who sells four calls, each with a delta of 25, and buys one underlying contract, might believe that he is delta neutral. But in order to arrive at a call delta of 25 the trader is required to feed some volatility into a theoretical pricing model. If he subsequently decides that his original volatility assumption was too low, and he now raises it … the delta of the call might be 35, and instead of being delta neutral, the trader is now 40 deltas short. *All he did to unbalance his delta position was to change his assumptions about market conditions.*" Nothing traded, nothing moved — and the book is now 40 deltas off. That is vanna in one paragraph, and it lands because the fresher already knows from C4 what "40 deltas short" costs. (Our answer key: a 110-strike call, 60 days, delta 0.25 at σ = 31.8 %; raise σ by 10 points and delta is 0.317 — Natenberg's "about 35" is a round number, the direction and size are right.) **Strongest hook**: it is a story, it has a loss, and it needs no new symbol.

**Hook B — the puzzle: "why did my delta change over the weekend?"** Friday close: a book hedged flat. Monday open, before any print: the deltas are different. The underlying did not move, vol did not move; only the calendar did. The chapter's answer is charm, Natenberg's Figures 6-11/6-12 (call and put delta vs time to expiration, p. 108–109): "as we decrease the amount of time to expiration … all option deltas move away from 50. An option which is in-the-money will move further into-the-money, and an option which is out-of-the-money will move further out-of-the-money." hypercall's phrasing is the fresher version: "Charm is why delta hedging is not 'set and forget'" and "Charm accumulates over weekends and accelerates near expiry" (docs.hypercall.xyz, Lesson 8). Good second hook because it is a mystery with a one-line resolution.

**Hook C — the everyday object: the map of slopes.** A hiker's map has contour lines (height). A better map also prints the slope at each point (delta), and a still better one prints how the slope changes as you walk north (gamma) or east (vanna). The higher Greeks are just more columns in the same legend. Easy, but it is an explanation rather than a hook; it becomes the frame for the sign-map figure in §4.

**Strongest: A as the opener, B as the mid-chapter turn, C as the organising image.** The sponsor's brief ("do not make it look like rocket science") is served by the fact that A and B contain no formula at all.

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Book chapters read

- **Natenberg (1994), Ch. 6 "Option values and changing market conditions", p. 103–118 (PDF 114–129).** The surprise of this sprint: Natenberg never names vanna or charm but draws both. Figures 6-11 to 6-14 are call/put delta vs time to expiration and vs volatility at S = 100 for strikes 90/100/110 (p. 108–110). His reading: "As we increase the time to expiration or increase volatility, all options tend to become more at-the-money, with call deltas approaching 50 and put deltas approaching −50. Conversely, as we decrease the amount of time to expiration or decrease volatility, all option deltas move away from 50 … Options which are at-the-money and have deltas close to 50 tend to maintain the same delta characteristics regardless of changes in time or volatility" (p. 106). That paragraph *is* the sign map: vanna and charm vanish at the money and have opposite signs either side. Figures 6-9/6-10 (gamma vs time, gamma vs vol: the ATM gamma "can increase dramatically as expiration approaches, or as we decrease our volatility assumption", p. 106) are color and zomma without the names. The "sells four calls … 40 deltas short" story (p. 110) is Hook A. Does badly for a beginner: nothing is named, so the reader cannot look anything up afterwards; no sign rule is stated.
- **Wilmott, Paul Wilmott on Quantitative Finance, Ch. 7 §7.3–7.8, book p. 121–129 (PDF 142–150).** Gives the boxed formulae for delta, gamma, theta, **speed** (§7.6, p. 126: "The speed of an option is the rate of change of the gamma with respect to the stock price … Traders use the gamma to estimate how much they will have to rehedge by if the stock moves … But that's only an approximation. The delta may change by more or less than this, especially if the stock moves by a larger amount, or the option is close to the strike and expiration. Hence the use of speed in a higher-order Taylor series expansion") and vega. Then the footnote on p. 127 we should quote on the page: "The second derivative with respect to σ has been called 'vomma' and the second-order derivative with respect to the asset and the volatility has been called 'kabanga'. I doubt that they represent what their fans think they represent, and I'm going to make no further mention of them." Also p. 128 on the limits of vega: "It is only really meaningful for options having single-signed gamma everywhere." Does well: speed is motivated as the correction to the gamma estimate of a rehedge — exactly our "why a desk cares". Does badly: dismisses the vol cross-Greeks, which a desk in 2026 does use (vanna-volga is a pricing method in FX).
- **Hull OFOD 5e, Ch. 14 §14.10 "Hedging in practice", p. 319 (PDF 334) and §14.11 "Scenario analysis", Table 14.5 (p. 320).** §14.10: "traders usually zero out delta at least once a day … a zero gamma and a zero vega are less easy to achieve … In most cases, gamma and vega are monitored. When they get too large … corrective action is taken." Table 14.5 is a 3 × 7 grid of P&L (exchange rate 0.94–1.06 across, vol 8/10/12 % down) — a real artefact: a desk already looks at a spot × vol grid, and the *curvature* of that grid across its diagonal is vanna and volga. Hull does not say so; we will. Does badly: no higher Greeks named at all (5e, 2002).
- **Hull Ch. 15 §15.5 "Greek letters", p. 337 (PDF 352).** Under sticky delta the delta is ∂c/∂S + (∂c/∂σ)(∂σ/∂S) — a vega-times-smile-slope correction to delta. We mention it in one line as "the D2 chapter adds a term here" and leave the content to D2.
- **Gatheral, The Volatility Surface, Ch. 1 p. 4–7 (PDF 35–38).** The stochastic-vol hedging portfolio Π = V − ΔS − Δ₁V₁ with the cross term ρηβS ∂²V/∂v∂S in dΠ (p. 5): when vol itself moves, a cross-derivative in (S, v) appears in the P&L. One sentence in our chapter: "the vanna term is the price of the fact that vol and spot move together". Too heavy to show; cite only.

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

1. https://nopeitslily.substack.com/p/intuitive-understanding-of-the-higher — "Intuitive understanding of the higher-order Greeks". Frames all of them as "slope of the slope" via y = x² (first derivative 2x, second derivative 2), uses delta ≈ probability of finishing in the money to get the vanna sign ("with more volatility, we expect delta to go up" for an OTM option), and gives the honest warning "Vanna is hard especially for newer traders to grasp, because unlike gamma it's fairly unintuitive." Best line: "Volatility is the true tempo of what determines the option's time value (not wall time!)" — i.e. vanna and charm are cousins: less vol and less time both shrink the cloud. Why it works: one picture (the parabola) and one proxy (delta ≈ probability) carry every sign.
2. https://docs.hypercall.xyz/docs/volatility-course/advanced-greeks — the only page found that states signs by moneyness cleanly: vanna "OTM calls: positive … OTM puts: negative … ATM: roughly zero … ITM: the opposite sign of OTM"; volga "ATM is low or zero. OTM wings are high and positive"; charm "OTM calls: negative … ITM calls: positive … ATM: small". Calls volga "the gamma of vega". Also the "shadow gamma" idea (gamma including vol moving with spot) and the Taleb identity vega = σ·T·S²·Γ with the moral "If you manage gamma and vega as separate risks, you're double-counting." Why it works: it is a table, and a fresher can check each cell against the delta-vs-vol picture.
3. https://www.globalcapital.com/article/28mss7ctnpdchq9s8uuzn/asia/the-hidden-uses-of-vanna — "The hidden uses of vanna". The desk view: vanna "summarizes vega skew" (positive vanna ⇒ net long calls / short puts), "proxies charm" ("falling vol is treated like shrinking time to expiry"), and the double-trouble scenario: a long-call/short-put, delta-hedged, vega-neutral book when vol rises and spot falls — "This is where you have double trouble." Best line: "Vanna is certainly a risk metric best viewed on a price-slide risk matrix" — which is Hull's Table 14.5 with the diagonal read.
4. https://www.macroption.com/higher-order-greeks/ — definitions and aliases only (charm = "delta decay or DdeltaDtime", color = "gamma decay", veta = "vega decay", vanna = "DvegaDspot or DdeltaDvol", zomma = "DgammaDvol", ultima). No signs, no intuition — useful as the glossary we print, and as evidence that the web stops at naming.
5. https://moontowermeta.com/dynamic-hedging-option-p-l-decomposition/ — Moontower. After delta, vega, gamma, theta are subtracted from a real option's daily P&L "the error term … [is attributed] to minor Greeks: vanna, volga, and charm", and across a year of daily data the residual stays "under 0.4 cents". Why it matters for us: the honest scale. For a vanilla, the higher Greeks are the *residual*; they matter for books (many options, wings, near expiry), not for one at-the-money call. Our chapter should say so, loudly, to stay un-rocket-science.
6. Searched and not useful: "Moontower vanna" returns an Austin landmark; the Seeking Alpha "Greek engine … 0DTE" piece and daytrading.com/vanna were not opened (paywall / low quality signals in the snippet) — (not opened).

### Common misconceptions

- "Vanna is positive for calls and negative for puts." (globalcapital states it this way.) Wrong by moneyness: vanna has the same sign for a call and a put at the same strike (both are −e^{−qT}N′(d₁)d₂/σ), and it changes sign at d₂ = 0, roughly at the forward. The call/put statement is only true as a book-level rule of thumb for out-of-the-money options. Our sign map fixes this visually.
- "Volga is always positive" — it is zero *and changes sign* in a narrow band around the money (sign = sign(d₁·d₂); at S = K = 100, T = 1, σ = 20 % the 105 strike has volga −1.98 while 100 has +9.85 and 120 has +72.9). Say: "small and fiddly near the money, large and positive in the wings".
- "Charm is the same thing as theta." It is the time-drift of *delta*, not of value; Natenberg's Figures 6-11/6-12 show it is zero at the money (delta "tends to maintain the same delta … regardless of changes in time").
- "Higher Greeks are a precision tool." Wilmott's footnote and Moontower's 0.4-cent residual both say the opposite for a single vanilla; they matter for aggregates and near expiry. The chapter must give the fresher permission not to memorise speed/zomma/color.
- Sign conventions for charm differ by source (∂Δ/∂t vs ∂Δ/∂τ, τ = T − t) — the engine must state its own (we take calendar time t, so an OTM call's charm is negative: delta decays).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "The sign-map poster" (annotated static figure, illustrated text)

**Mode:** illustrated text with one strong static figure: a 2 × 3 grid of coloured planes over spot (x) × time-to-expiry (y), one plane per Greek (vanna, volga, charm; speed, zomma, color underneath, smaller), hue = sign (two colours only), lightness = magnitude, a dashed ATM/forward ridge, and a drawn trader standing on each plane with a one-line speech bubble.

**Storyboard (6 beats):**
1. The legend first: "Hue = which way. Darker = how much. The dashed line is at-the-money." No symbols yet.
2. Vanna plane. Left of the ridge (low strikes / spot above strike) one colour, right the other; both fade to white at the ridge and in the far wings; the darkest patches are a few months out, 5–10 % either side of the ridge. The trader's bubble: "Vol up → my OTM call's delta goes up, my ITM call's delta goes down." Side panel: Natenberg's Figure 6-13 redrawn (delta vs vol for 90/100/110) — the 110 line rises, the 90 line falls, the 100 line is flat. "That picture is this plane, read along one row."
3. Charm plane. Same two regions, mirror-coloured relative to vanna, but the dark patches crowd toward expiry (bottom of the plane) and are nearly white a year out. Bubble: "Nothing moved. It's Monday. My OTM delta shrank." Side panel: Natenberg's Figure 6-11.
4. Volga plane. White along the ridge, dark and single-coloured in both wings, with a faint sliver of the opposite colour hugging the ridge (so the fresher sees the sign flip exists and that it is small). Bubble: "My wings love a vol spike more than the middle does."
5. The second row, smaller, captioned not narrated: speed ("gamma's slope"), zomma ("gamma's vol-sensitivity"), color ("gamma's decay") — each with Wilmott's one sentence. A footer: Wilmott's "I doubt that they represent what their fans think they represent" quote, and Moontower's "under 0.4 cents" residual, so the reader knows how much weight to give them.
6. The desk panel: Hull's Table 14.5 (exchange rate × vol grid) reproduced with the diagonal highlighted and the words "a desk already has this grid; vanna is how fast the numbers change along the diagonal, volga how fast they change down a column".

**Makes visible that text cannot:** that vanna and charm are *mirror images* with the same zero line, that charm lives near expiry and vanna in mid-dates, that volga is a wing phenomenon. **Cost:** low — six heat maps from the engine plus illustration; no interaction. **Risk:** a colour map without numbers can feel like weather; mitigated by the side panels redrawn from Natenberg's curves and by printing three actual values (K = 95/100/105) on each plane.

### Concept 2 — "What happens to my delta when vol jumps?" (guess-then-reveal)

**Mode:** guess-then-reveal (catalogue B60), three rounds.

**Storyboard (7 beats):**
1. Round 1: a drawn position card — "You sold 4 calls, strike 110, 60 days, delta 25 each, and bought 1 future. Vol goes from 32 % to 42 %. Are you now long, short, or flat deltas?" Three buttons. The reader's pick is recorded.
2. Reveal: the delta-vs-vol curve for the 110 strike slides in; the marker moves from 0.25 to ≈ 0.32; the card updates "−4 × 32 + 100 = −28 deltas: you are short". Natenberg's sentence appears verbatim ("All he did to unbalance his delta position was to change his assumptions").
3. Round 2: "Same book, but the calls are strike 90 (in the money, delta 80). Vol jumps. Long, short or flat?" Reveal: delta *falls* toward 50 → the book is long. The two reveals side by side are the vanna sign flip.
4. Round 3: "Same 110 calls. Nothing happens for a week; vol unchanged. Long, short or flat?" Reveal: the delta-vs-time curve; delta slides down; short. "This one is charm. Notice it's the same direction as a vol *fall* — less time and less vol both shrink the cloud."
5. A tally: the reader's three picks vs the truth, with the sentence "If you got round 2 wrong you are in good company — the sign flips at the money."
6. Only now the words: vanna = ∂Δ/∂σ, charm = ∂Δ/∂t, with the three curves pinned.
7. Hand-off: "The poster below shows every strike and every date at once."

**Makes visible:** the reader's own wrong prior (most freshers guess "long" in round 2). **Cost:** low–medium (three BS curves, state). **Risk:** the "deltas per contract" bookkeeping (Natenberg's ×100 convention) can distract; keep everything in "deltas" with the convention stated once.

### Concept 3 — "Nudge twice on the surface" (one animated probe, illustrated text around it)

**Mode:** scrubbable animation / probe explorable (catalogue B66), the heat-equation nudge scene run on the delta surface.

**Storyboard (6 beats):**
1. The delta surface Δ(S, σ) at fixed T drawn in 3D with a synced 2D slice (catalogue rule: always pair); the ATM ridge dashed.
2. Place the probe at (S = 100, σ = 20 %). First nudge along S: a green ∂S leg, the ∂Δ rise, the tangent; the readout "that slope is gamma — you know this".
3. Second nudge along σ: a yellow ∂σ leg, the rise, "that slope is vanna". The nudge shrinks from 5 vol points to 0.1 as in the 3b1b scene.
4. Drag the probe across the ridge: the yellow rise flips from up to down; the sign change is seen, not stated.
5. Swap the surface to Δ(S, τ) with one toggle; repeat; the yellow leg is now charm.
6. Swap to vega(S, σ); the S-nudge is vanna again ("same number, other door": ∂vega/∂S = ∂Δ/∂σ), the σ-nudge is volga.

**Makes visible:** that vanna is *one* number reachable by two routes, and that the cross-partial is literally "nudge twice". **Cost:** medium–high (3D + probe + three surfaces). **Risk:** highest of the three — 3D surfaces with a moving probe are the classic "looks like rocket science" picture, and the catalogue already notes drwong's 3D Greeks are the weakest-explained tool on his site. Only with the 2D slice and slow probe.

## 5. My recommendation

**Concept 2 then Concept 1, as a sequence** — the guess-then-reveal opens the chapter and the poster closes it. Judged by the brief: *easy* — the reveal uses only delta-vs-vol and delta-vs-time curves the fresher met in C4, and the poster is a legend-first colour map with Natenberg's curves as the side panels; *interesting* — the three rounds carry a story with a loss (Hook A) and a mystery (Hook B), and round 2 reliably catches the reader out; *interactive* — exactly as much as teaches (three buttons, three reveals) and no more. Concept 3 is parked as an optional "see it on the surface" link for readers who want it; the sponsor's own guidance ("mostly illustrated text with one strong figure") matches this choice, and the one strong figure is the sign-map poster. Speed, zomma, color and veta are named, drawn small, and explicitly de-emphasised with Wilmott's footnote.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

Notation: BS with continuous yield q, τ = time to expiry, d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r − q + ½σ²)τ]/(σ√τ), d₂ = d₁ − σ√τ, N′ the standard normal density. Vega per unit σ (not per vol point) unless stated. Sources: Wilmott Ch. 7 boxed tables (delta/gamma/vega/speed, p. 122–128); vanna/volga/charm/zomma/color/veta are standard BS cross-partials (Haug's formula collection is the usual reference — (not opened); each is verified below against central finite differences of our own BS price in the Python key, which is the test the engine must pass).

- Vanna = ∂Δ/∂σ = ∂vega/∂S = −e^{−qτ} N′(d₁) d₂/σ. **Sign = −sign(d₂)**: negative when the option is in the money by more than the ½σ²τ drift (d₂ > 0), positive out of the money, zero where d₂ = 0.
- Volga (vomma) = ∂²V/∂σ² = vega · d₁d₂/σ. **Sign = sign(d₁d₂)**: positive in both wings, negative in the thin band where d₁ > 0 > d₂.
- Charm (call, calendar-time convention ∂Δ/∂t) = −[−q e^{−qτ}N(d₁) + e^{−qτ}N′(d₁)(2(r−q)τ − d₂σ√τ)/(2τσ√τ)]; put charm = call charm + q e^{−qτ} (equal when q = 0). For an OTM call charm < 0 (delta decays), for an ITM call > 0.
- Speed = ∂Γ/∂S = −(Γ/S)(1 + d₁/(σ√τ)) (Wilmott p. 126).
- Zomma = ∂Γ/∂σ = Γ(d₁d₂ − 1)/σ. Color = ∂Γ/∂t (calendar) = −e^{−qτ}N′(d₁)/(2Sτσ√τ) · [2qτ + 1 + (2(r−q)τ − d₂σ√τ)d₁/(σ√τ)]. Veta = ∂vega/∂τ = −S e^{−qτ}N′(d₁)√τ [q + (r−q)d₁/(σ√τ) − (1 + d₁d₂)/(2τ)].
- The Taylor frame that motivates speed (Wilmott p. 126): dΔ ≈ Γ dS + ½ Speed dS² + vanna dσ + charm dt. One line, no more.
