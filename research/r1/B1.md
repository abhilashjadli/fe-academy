## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

Randomness can have rules you can picture: a drunk's next step depends only on where he is standing now, a crowd of drunks spreads out like the square root of time, a weather chain forgets where it started and settles into its long-run habits, and buses that arrive "at a constant rate" still arrive in clumps.

## 2. Why a fresher should care — three hook candidates

**(a) The puzzle: the drunk and the lamppost.** A drunk leaves the lamppost and takes 100 steps, each left or right with a coin. Where is he? "At the lamppost, on average" is true and useless: the typical distance is 10 steps, and after 400 steps it is 20, not 40. Quanta's version (Mutalik, "A drunkard's walk in Manhattan") gives the one-line reason a fresher can hold: from 5 m out, the most *neutral* step is sideways, and it lands him √26 m away — so distance grows like √n. "The more the drunkard walks, the farther he gets from his starting point." The stakes: this √t is the same √t that will set the width of every option price distribution from C2 on, and the same reason Hull says volatility is "1.0 per square root of years" (p. 218).

**(b) The story with stakes: "it's been falling all week, it's due".** Hull's opening (p. 216): IBM is at $100 now. Does it matter for your forecast that it came up from $80 or down from $120? A Markov process says no — "the past history of the variable and the way that the present has emerged from the past are irrelevant". The stakes are the firm's models: Wilmott's cartoon says it in one line, "MARKOV MEANS NO MEMORY BEYOND THE PRESENT. A PROPERTY OF MOST FINANCE MODELS" (p. 73). Hull's argument for why it holds is a nice two-sentence story: if a pattern "always gave a 65% chance of subsequent steep price rises", everyone would buy on the pattern and the rise would happen immediately (p. 217). The colleague who says "it's due for a bounce" is proposing a non-Markov world.

**(c) The everyday object: the bus stop.** Buses come every 10 minutes on average and you arrive at a random time. How long do you wait? Everyone says 5 minutes. If buses arrived as a Poisson process, the answer is 10 (jakevdp, "The Waiting Time Paradox"): long gaps are the ones you are more likely to land in, and the process is memoryless — "the expected time to the next arrival is always exactly 10 minutes", however long you have already waited. The same page then checks Seattle bus data and finds real buses are *not* Poisson (waits were about half the interval plus a minute or two), which is a rare chance to show a model and its failure in one figure.

**Which is strongest.** For the chapter's *opening*, (a): it is a direct continuation of 0.2 (the Galton board turned sideways is this walk — chapter 0.2 already built F10 for exactly this) and it gives the one number the fresher must keep (√t). But the most *instructive* ten seconds in the whole chapter is setosa's trick for the Markov section: two strips of rainy/sunny days, one real and one generated by a fair coin, "which one is the real weather?" — most people pick the coin-flip strip because it "looks more random", and the reveal (real weather is sticky: 0.9 chance of staying) teaches persistence before any matrix exists. I recommend (a) to open, the fake-weather guess for the Markov act, and the bus stop for the Poisson act; the three topics are three acts and each gets its own hook.

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Book chapters read

- **Hull, OFOD 5e, ch. 11 §11.1–11.2, pp. 216–221 (PDF 231–236).** Does well for a beginner: the opening reassurance — "Many people feel that continuous-time stochastic processes are so complicated that they should be left entirely to 'rocket scientists'. This is not so." (p. 216) — the IBM-at-$100 statement of the Markov property, and the cleanest explanation of √t anywhere: a variable at 10 whose one-year change is φ(0, 1) has a two-year change φ(0, √2) and a six-month change φ(0, √0.5), because "the variance of the changes in successive time periods are additive. The standard deviations of the changes in successive time periods are not additive" (p. 217). Example 11.1 (z = 25; after five years, sd √5 = 2.236) and Example 11.2 (cash position 50, drift 20/yr, variance rate 900/yr → after one year N(70, 30), after six months N(60, 21.21), p. 221) are good test values. Does badly: no discrete chain, no Poisson, no picture of many paths; jumps to continuous time on page two.
- **Shreve, SCF II, §3.2 "Scaled random walks", pp. 83–89 (PDF 99–105).** Does well: everything is counted from coin tosses. X_j = ±1, M_k = ΣX_j (3.2.1–2); independent increments with variance ℓ − k (3.2.4); the four-line martingale proof (3.2.5) that B2 will reuse; quadratic variation [M, M]_k = k "computed path-by-path" (3.2.6, p. 85–86); the scaled walk W^(n)(t) = M_{nt}/√n (3.2.7) with Fig. 3.2.2 (400 tosses, step 1/10, up to t = 4); the worked increment "W^(100)(0.70) − W^(100)(0.20) is the sum of 50 independent random variables, each ±1/10, so the variance is 50 · 1/100 = 0.50" (p. 87); and Fig. 3.2.3, the histogram of W^(100)(0.25) against the normal with peak 0.798 (p. 89). Does badly: σ-algebra language from the first page; one path, never a crowd.
- **Shreve, §11.2 "Poisson process", pp. 462–468 (PDF 478–484).** Does well: builds N(t) the way a bus stop does — from waiting times. Exponential τ with Eτ = 1/λ and P{τ > t} = e^{−λt} (11.2.2); memorylessness, "after waiting s time units, the probability that we will have to wait an additional t time units is the same as the probability of having to wait t time units when we were starting at time 0" (11.2.3, p. 463); arrival times S_n = Στ_k, N(t) counts arrivals, Fig. 11.2.1 is the staircase; Lemma 11.2.2 P{N(t) = k} = (λt)^k e^{−λt}/k!; Theorem 11.2.3 stationary independent increments; mean = variance = λ(t − s) (11.2.9–10, "the variance is the same as the mean"); Theorem 11.2.4 the compensated process N(t) − λt is a martingale, with Fig. 11.2.2 the sawtooth. Does badly: the gamma-density induction and the series manipulations are noise for us; no clumping intuition.
- **Shreve, §2.3 Definition 2.3.6, p. 74 and §2.4 p. 77.** The continuous-time Markov definition E[f(X(t)) | F(s)] = g(X(s)) and the summary sentence worth quoting: "the estimate of f(X(t)) made at time s depends only on the process value X(s) at time s and not on the path of the process before time s."
- **Wilmott on QF, ch. 4 §4.2–4.6, pp. 71–75 (PDF 92–96).** Does well: one running experiment — "Toss a coin. Every time you throw a head I give you $1, every time you throw a tail you give me $1… the sequence was THHTHT, and we finished even" (p. 71) — carries the random walk, the Markov property, the martingale property and quadratic variation on two pages. Two sentences a fresher needs: "the random walk has no memory beyond where it is now" and, immediately, "Note that it doesn't have to be the case that the expected value of the random variable S_i is the same as the previous value" (p. 73) — Markov is not martingale. Also the blackjack-vs-roulette aside (a shoe of cards has memory; a wheel does not). §4.6 (p. 74) rescales: six tosses in time t with bet √(t/6), then n tosses with bet √(t/n), quadratic variation t throughout — the bridge to B3. Does badly: no chain with states, no Poisson.

### Common misconceptions

- **Markov means independent.** No: the sticky weather chain is Markov and strongly dependent; Markov says the *dependence runs through the present only*. (setosa; Wilmott p. 73.)
- **Markov means martingale, or the reverse.** Wilmott p. 73 denies the first explicitly; Shreve §2.4 defines them separately. A biased walk is Markov and not a martingale; B2 needs this clean.
- **"Random looks evenly mixed."** The coin-flip weather strip looks "more random" than real weather; Poisson arrivals look clumpy. Both are the same misperception of randomness as evenness.
- **"It's due."** The gambler's fallacy is a claim that the walk has memory. Wilmott's blackjack remark shows where memory genuinely exists (a shoe of cards) and where it does not (a wheel).
- **Expected position zero means "it stays near zero".** Mean 0, typical distance √t, and the fraction of walkers within ±√t after 100 steps is 0.73 (exact; §6).
- **Standard deviations add.** They do not; variances do (Hull p. 217–218). This is the whole reason for √t.
- **The stationary distribution is "where the chain ends up".** It is a distribution, not a state; and the periodic chain [[0, 1], [1, 0]] never settles yet spends half its time in each state (QuantEcon).
- **"Constant rate" means evenly spaced.** A perfectly even timetable is the opposite extreme; Poisson is maximal clumping for a given rate (jakevdp's data sits between).
- **Expected wait is half the gap.** Only for a timetable; for Poisson it is the full gap (inspection paradox).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three different modes

### Concept A — "A crowd of drunks" (physical simulation with a speed ramp)

Mode: physical simulation / experiment, the 0.2 engine reused. Beats:

1. A lamppost and one drunk on a street drawn as a number line. Click *step*: he moves ±1; a running sum in the corner (`show_corner_sum`). Five clicks by hand.
2. *Auto*: 100 steps at speed; the drunk ends up at, say, +8. "Where would he be next time?" — reset, run again: −12.
3. *1,000 drunks*: each starts at the lamppost, same rules; grey count bars above the street (`Diffusion1D`). The bars are a bell after 30 steps.
4. The ⟨x²⟩ odometer in the corner climbs linearly with the step counter while the bars widen slowly: a bracket on the bars reads "typical distance √t" — at t = 100 it is 10, at 400 it is 20. A time slider lets the reader scrub.
5. *Predict then run*: "after 400 steps, what fraction of drunks are within 20 of the lamppost?" Reader guesses; the bars answer (≈ 0.73 for ±√t including the boundary; see §6).
6. Rotate the street to vertical and run time left-to-right: the crowd is a fan of paths; the bars are the terminal histogram; "this is the sideways Galton board from 0.2".
7. One sentence under it: "the next step does not look at the path — only at where he stands". A toggle *give him memory* (a step is more likely to repeat the last one): the crowd spreads faster than √t, the Markov sentence fails visibly.

What it makes visible that text cannot: one walker is erratic, a crowd is a bell, and the bell's width grows with the *square root* of the counter, read from an instrument rather than a formula. Cost: low — the Galton engine, bars and odometer exist in 0.2; new work is the memory toggle and the rotation. Risk: the fresher takes "mean 0" as "stays at 0"; the odometer and the predict-then-run beat are there to prevent it.

### Concept B — "Spot the fake weather, then build the machine" (guess-then-reveal → manual-to-automatic explorable)

Mode: guess-then-reveal that turns into an explorable with the manual → automatic arc (B9/B76; setosa + QuantEcon). Beats:

1. Two 60-day strips of R/S squares, one from real daily data (or a seeded sticky chain presented as "recorded"), one from a fair coin. "One of these is real. Which?" Vote; reveal; show the count: the real strip has runs averaging 10 days, the coin's runs average 2.
2. A two-state diagram R ⇄ S with four arrows. The reader *drives* it: click to roll, the token hops, the strip grows at the bottom — six hops by hand. The arrow thicknesses are the probabilities; the matrix appears beside the diagram *as counts from the strip* (how many R→R, R→S …), then as fractions. Rows sum to 1; edit a row so it does not and the matrix turns red (setosa).
3. *Auto*: 1,000 hops. Two instruments: the running fraction of days in R (a line settling at 0.5) and, beside it, the *law* ψ_t = ψ_0 Pᵗ as two bars that move each step from (1, 0) through (0.9, 0.1), (0.82, 0.18), (0.756, 0.244) … to (0.5, 0.5). The caption separates the two: "the bars are what you would bet before looking; the line is what actually happened".
4. Drag P(stay in R) from 0.9 to 0.6 to 0.5: the strip loses its stickiness, the bars reach their resting place faster (the distance to rest shrinks by the second eigenvalue, 0.8 per day at 0.9/0.9, 0.2 per day at 0.6/0.6 — printed, not derived).
5. Twist: make it asymmetric, P = [[0.4, 0.6], [0.2, 0.8]] (QuantEcon). The resting bars are (0.25, 0.75) whatever the start — start from R, start from S, the bars arrive at the same place. "Where a chain settles does not depend on where it starts, if it mixes around."
6. *Break it*: P = [[0, 1], [1, 0]]. The bars never rest (they flip), yet the running fraction still settles at 0.5. One sentence: settling of the *law* and settling of the *time-average* are different things.
7. Optional third act: Hamilton's three regimes (normal / mild / severe recession) with the chain's law as a dot moving inside a triangle (the simplex), three starts spiralling into ψ* = (0.813, 0.163, 0.025). A regime-switching volatility model (A5, D3) is exactly this.
8. Close: "Markov" named only now — the token's next hop uses the arrow it is standing on and nothing else; the strip's history is never consulted.

What it makes visible: persistence as a picture before a number; the matrix as *counts*; a distribution as a point that moves and rests; the difference between the law and the sample path. Cost: medium (two-state version) to high (simplex). Risk: the triangle is unfamiliar — keep two states as the default and make the three-state act a second panel; the "law vs running fraction" distinction can confuse if both instruments are not labelled in plain words.

### Concept C — "The bus stop" (drawn-character comic strip with one live timeline)

Mode: comic strip with a drawn person at a bus stop, whose last panels are an annotated live timeline (B26's glow-dot primitive). Beats:

1. Panel: a sign, "buses every 10 minutes (on average)". The character arrives, thinks "about 5 minutes then".
2. Panel: the reader is handed 12 buses and a 2-hour line: "place them as you'd expect". Most readers space them evenly.
3. Panel: "now let chance place them, at the same rate": each second a lottery ticket with 1-in-600 odds (the `VariableProofOfWork` move: a number re-rolls until it hits); a glow dot flares on the line at each hit. Clumps and gaps appear; three buses in five minutes, then nothing for 25.
4. Panel: zoom out to a week of lines; count buses per hour; the counts' histogram is drawn and the Poisson bars overlaid (0.16 at 5 and 6, 0.0025 at 0, 0.0113 at 12). "Constant rate, clumpy arrivals."
5. Panel: the staircase N(t) climbing one step per dot (Shreve Fig. 11.2.1), and the sawtooth N(t) − 6t that B2 will call a martingale (Fig. 11.2.2), shown but not explained.
6. Panel: the character drops onto the line at a random moment; the gap she lands in is measured; repeat 1,000 times; the mean wait reads 10.0, not 5. The croupier-style caption: "the bus does not know how long you have waited" — memorylessness — and the small print that real buses are not Poisson (jakevdp's Seattle data), so a timetable makes the wait 5.
7. Payoff panel: the same glow dots on a price chart are *jumps* (D3); client orders hitting a desk are arrivals; the rate is the one number you can read off the timeline.

What it makes visible: the gap between "constant rate" and "even spacing", and memorylessness as a physical fact about a lottery. Cost: low–medium — six drawn panels plus a timeline widget with a rate knob. Risk: the waiting-time paradox is so memorable it can hijack the act; keep it to one panel with the honest "real buses are not Poisson" footnote so it does not become a false fact about buses.

## 5. My recommendation

A sequence, because the chapter is three topics: **Concept A as a short opening (reusing 0.2), Concept B as the spine and the one real build, Concept C as a comic with a single interaction.** The modes differ (simulation; guess-then-reveal into an explorable; comic strip), which is what the sponsor asked for.

Judged by the brief: *easy first* — the fake-weather guess and the bus-stop comic ask nothing of the reader but an opinion; the drunks are the 0.2 board they have already seen. *Interesting second* — being wrong about which weather is real, and being wrong about the 5-minute wait, are the two moments that make a fresher continue. *Interactive third* — interaction is spent where it teaches: driving the token by hand before automation (the manual → automatic arc), editing one probability, and placing buses before chance does. If budget forces one cut, drop the simplex (B.7) and keep the two-state chain with its two instruments; if it forces two, make C fully static with a pre-rendered clip of the lottery.

The one picture nobody else gives, and the thing I would protect: in B.3, the *law* (bars) and the *running fraction* (line) settling side by side, then B.6 pulling them apart with the periodic chain.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, and reference numbers

**Random walk** (Shreve §3.2, pp. 83–89). X_j = ±1 with probability ½ each; M_0 = 0, M_k = Σ_{j≤k} X_j. Independent increments over disjoint intervals; E[M_ℓ − M_k] = 0, Var = ℓ − k (3.2.4). Markov; martingale (3.2.5); quadratic variation Σ(M_j − M_{j−1})² = k (3.2.6), path by path. Scaled walk W^(n)(t) = M_{nt}/√n (3.2.7); Var(W^(n)(t) − W^(n)(s)) = t − s (3.2.8); Theorem 3.2.1 (CLT): W^(n)(t) → N(0, t). Hull: variance of a change over T is proportional to T, standard deviation to √T (p. 217–218); Wiener process δz = ε√δt (11.1, p. 218).

Reference numbers (verified 2026-10-22):
- P(M_10 = 2) = C(10, 6)/2¹⁰ = 0.205078; P(M_10 = 0) = 0.246094.
- sd(M_100) = 10; sd(M_400) = 20; P(|M_100| ≤ 10) = 0.728747 (exact; equals 2Φ(1.1) − 1 = 0.7287 with the continuity correction, *not* 0.68).
- Shreve (3.2.11): P{W^(100)(0.25) = 0.1} = 25!/(13! 12!) · 2^{−25} = 5,200,300/33,554,432 = **0.154981**. The book prints 0.1555 and bar height 0.7775 (p. 88); the exact values are 0.1550 and 0.7749. Tests should use the exact value and the note should say the book rounds. The normal peak 2/√(2π) = 0.797885 matches the book's 0.798.
- Hull Example 11.1: sd after five years √5 = 2.23607. Example 11.2: after one year N(70, 30), after six months N(60, 21.2132). Example 11.3 (p. 223): one-week change with S = 100, μ = 0.15, σ = 0.30, δt = 0.0192: mean 0.288, sd 4.1569.
- Memory toggle (A.7): with P(repeat last step) = p the increments are a two-state chain and Var(M_t)/t → (1 + (2p − 1))/(1 − (2p − 1)); at p = 0.75 the long-run ratio is 3 — the engine should print the measured ratio, not this formula.

**Markov chain** (QuantEcon Markov Chains I; Shreve Def. 2.3.6 p. 74). P stochastic: rows sum to 1; ψ_{t+1} = ψ_t P, ψ_{t+m} = ψ_t P^m; stationary ψ* P = ψ*; unique if P (or some power) is everywhere positive; then ψ_0 P^t → ψ* from any ψ_0 (asymptotic stationarity); ergodicity: the fraction of time in x → ψ*(x) along almost every path; the periodic chain shows the two are distinct. Two-state P = [[1−α, α], [β, 1−β]]: ψ*(0) = β/(α + β).

Reference numbers:
- P = [[0.4, 0.6], [0.2, 0.8]]: ψ* = (0.25, 0.75); from (1, 0): (0.4, 0.6), (0.28, 0.72), (0.256, 0.744), (0.2512, 0.7488), (0.25024, 0.74976); second eigenvalue 0.2; QuantEcon's 10⁶-step sample from ψ_0 = (0.1, 0.9) gave mean(X == 0) = 0.249886 (seeded; our test: within ±0.005 of 0.25 over 10⁶ steps).
- Sticky P = [[0.9, 0.1], [0.1, 0.9]]: ψ* = (½, ½); ψ_t(R) from R = ½ + ½ · 0.8ᵗ: 0.9, 0.82, 0.756, 0.66384 (t = 5), 0.553687 (t = 10), 0.505765 (t = 20); mean run length 1/(1 − 0.9) = 10 days vs 2 for the coin.
- Hamilton P (above): ψ* = (0.81280, 0.16256, 0.02464); eigenvalues 1, 0.851574, 0.389426; row 0 of P⁶ = (0.883197, 0.104550, 0.012253).
- Periodic [[0, 1], [1, 0]]: ψ* = (½, ½); ψ_t from (1, 0) alternates (1, 0), (0, 1), …; time-average → ½.
- Validation: a row not summing to 1 (±1e−9) is rejected in red.

**Poisson process** (Shreve §11.2, pp. 462–468). Inter-arrival τ_k i.i.d. exponential, density λe^{−λt}, Eτ = 1/λ, P{τ > t} = e^{−λt}; memoryless P{τ > t + s | τ > s} = e^{−λt} (11.2.3). S_n = Σ_{k≤n} τ_k; N(t) = n for S_n ≤ t < S_{n+1}, right-continuous; P{N(t) = k} = (λt)^k e^{−λt}/k! (11.2.6); increments stationary and independent (Thm 11.2.3); E[N(t) − N(s)] = Var = λ(t − s) (11.2.9–10); N(t) − λt is a martingale (Thm 11.2.4). Discrete lottery: a ticket each second with success 1/600 gives geometric gaps with mean 600 s; the exponential is the fine-grid limit (the C.3 mechanism is honest, not a metaphor).

- P{N(1 h) = k}, k = 0…12: 0.0025, 0.0149, 0.0446, 0.0892, 0.1339, 0.1606, 0.1606, 0.1377, 0.1033, 0.0688, 0.0413, 0.0225, 0.0113.

- Mean wait from a uniformly random arrival time = 1/λ = 10 min (inspection paradox); the C.6 simulation over 1,000 drops must read 10.0 ± 0.6 (sd of the mean ≈ 10/√1000 ≈ 0.32; tolerance 2 sd).
- Superposition: two independent streams at rates 6 and 4 per hour form one stream at 10 per hour (the "more miners" beat).
