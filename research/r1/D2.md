# D2 Implied volatility, the smile and skew, term structure, the surface — and what the smile says about the future

_Phase R1 deep research note (sprint of 2026-10-22/23). Options for discussion with the sponsor, not decisions. Prerequisites assumed: C3 (implied vol as the one dial; put–call parity from C1), A1 (lognormal, fat tails), D1 (the break-even vol of a hedge is the implied vol). The catalogue flags "no existing explorable builds a smile from quotes"; this note builds one from a real 1992 option chain that is printed in Natenberg and reproduces in our solver._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

Implied volatility is just the price of an option translated into a language where every option can be compared with every other, and the moment you translate a whole screen of prices you discover the market does not believe in one number — it pays more for crash insurance than the bell curve says it should — and that disagreement, read strike by strike, is a picture of the future the market actually fears.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: "fifteen strikes, fifteen different volatilities of the same thing."** Natenberg's Figure 18-7 (p. 406): LIFFE Bund December futures at 87.86 on 27 July 1992, 119 days to expiry, interest 0 %, fifteen strikes from 84.50 to 91.50 with the settlement call and put prices and their implied vols: 3.98, 3.82, 3.76, 3.68, 3.62, 3.62, 3.58, 3.58, 3.56, 3.60, 3.62, 3.69, 3.73, 3.81, 3.87. Then the question in his own words: "Can those traders trading the 89 call or put really believe that the December futures contract will have a volatility of 3.60, while those trading the 85 call or put believe the December contract will have a volatility of 3.82? … the December Bund future can have only one volatility over its life … it doesn't make sense for every exercise price to have a different implied volatility" (p. 406). The fresher has the tool to check (C3's root-finder) and the chain is real. **Strongest hook**: it is a contradiction the reader can verify with their own hands, and the resolution — "the marketplace does not think the Black-Scholes model is one hundred percent efficient" (p. 407) — is the chapter.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: 19 October 1987.** Gatheral Fig. 1.1 ("Note the −22.9% return on October 19, 1987!", p. 2) and Hull p. 335: "the pattern in Figure 15.3 for equities has existed only since the stock market crash of October 1987. Prior to October 1987 implied volatilities were much less dependent on strike price. This has led Mark Rubinstein to suggest that one reason for the pattern in Figure 15.3 may be 'crashophobia'. Traders are concerned about the possibility of another crash similar to October 1987, and they price options accordingly." Hull's Table 15.1 gives the evidence a fresher can feel: in 12 currencies over 10 years, daily moves beyond 3 s.d. happened on 1.34 % of days against the lognormal's 0.27 %, beyond 5 s.d. on 0.08 % against 0.00 % (p. 333). Hull's §14.12 (p. 322) closes the loop with the portfolio insurers who "found that they were unable to sell either stocks or index futures fast enough" that Monday — the people who *should* have bought the puts. A real date and a real loss; the skew is the scar. Second-strongest; opens the equity-skew section.

**Hook C — the everyday object: the same word, different prices.** A storm-insurance quote for a house on the coast and one inland is "the same policy" with two premiums; nobody says the insurer has two beliefs about the weather — the two premiums *are* the belief, read by location. The smile is the premium map read by strike; Natenberg's "the marketplace appears to believe there is a better chance for a large price move in the real world than is predicted by the Black-Scholes model" (p. 407) and mathandmarkets' "The shape of the smile tells you what the market is afraid of." Easy, and it becomes the caption of the density figure rather than the opener.

**Strongest: A, with B as the turn into the equity skew and C as the recurring caption.**

### Common misconceptions

- "Calls and puts at the same strike have different implied vols." Hull (15.2) and Natenberg p. 405–406: parity forces them equal for Europeans ("the fact that call and put implied volatilities all line up indicates an efficient market with respect to arbitrage relationships"); Natenberg's own OEX index figures (18-11a/b) show the exception for Americans with early-exercise and arbitrage frictions.
- "Implied vol is the market's forecast of volatility." Partly. Wilmott p. 834–835: "Yes and no … the market also has views on the direction of the underlying, and also responds to supply and demand"; Natenberg p. 407: it is also "what weaknesses the marketplace is implying to the model".
- "The smile means the model is wrong, so throw it away." Hull p. 337: it is "a sophisticated interpolation tool"; Natenberg Fig. 18-9: the skew is simply a sixth input.
- "The smile is a function of strike." It is a function of moneyness *and* time (Natenberg's ln(E/U)/√t; Hull's (1/√T) ln(K/F₀)); plotting against raw strike makes different dates incomparable (Fig. 18-8c vs 18-8d).
- "Interpolate the surface in vol." Gatheral p. 26 and Hull p. 336: interpolate in a way that avoids negative butterflies and calendar spreads (total variance in time; smoothed in vol-space across strike); the "arbitrage-free" flags are part of the builder.
- "A density from the chain integrates to one." metricgate's 0.5888: a sparse, truncated grid leaks probability; the builder must show the mass it cannot see.
- "Term structure upward-sloping means the market expects vol to rise." Hull p. 336 says exactly that *when short-dated vols are historically low*; mathandmarkets' contango/backwardation; but Wilmott's "variances add" means the forward vol between 1m and 3m can be below both — the builder should compute the forward variance.

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "Smile from quotes" builder (explorable with a sweep-traced output)

**Mode:** explorable / the one-dial machine (catalogue B74 + B88 "draw or paint your own input"), fed by a real chain.

**Storyboard (8 beats):**
1. The chain first, as a real artefact: Natenberg's Figure 18-7 re-typeset — "LIFFE, 27 July 1992, Bund Dec futures 87.86, 119 days, rate 0" — fifteen rows of strike / call price / put price, *no vol column*. One prompt: "Which of these is expensive?" (The fresher cannot tell from prices. That is the point.)
2. The machine: C3's root-finder as a box with one dial (σ) and one needle (model price − market price). Press "solve 88 call": the dial turns until the needle reads zero and stops at 3.58; a dot appears on an empty axis of strike vs vol.
3. "Solve all": the dial sweeps strike by strike and the smile is *traced* by the sweep — fifteen dots, minimum at 88–88.5, both wings rising. Natenberg's question appears: "Can the 89 traders believe 3.60 while the 85 traders believe 3.82?"
4. The put column: press "solve puts" — the put dots land on the call dots. The parity line (15.2) appears under them: "they must."
5. Now the reader edits. Drag the 88.50 call price up by 0.05 → its dot jumps, the butterfly flag turns red: "88/88.5/89 butterfly now has negative value — a free lunch; a trader would sell it" (Natenberg p. 407). Drag it back; green. This is the arbitrage-free rule taught as a tripwire, not a theorem.
6. "Draw your own smile" (B88): the reader sketches a curve over the strikes; the machine runs *backwards* and prints the prices the sketch implies; a flat sketch gives the Black–Scholes prices — "one vol, one belief, the bell curve". The sketch is constrained monotone-in-the-wings so every drawing is valid.
7. Re-plot: a toggle switches the x-axis from strike to ln(K/F)/√T and the y-axis to % of ATM (Natenberg 18-8d/e); a second chain (the same market 5 weeks later, future 91.57, from Fig. 18-8c, hand-read) overlays and the two smiles "start to look very much alike".
8. Hand-off: a second tab stacks Hull's Table 15.2 as six smile slices raised one maturity at a time into a surface (B65) with a probe and the synced 2D slice; the 9-month/1.05 interpolation (13.7) is the probe's first stop; a "forward variance 1m→3m" readout shows that variances add.

**Makes visible:** that implied vol is *computed from* prices by a machine the reader has already seen, that the smile is a property of the chain and not of any model's output, and that an arbitrage is a kink you can make with your mouse. **Cost:** medium–high (root-finder, chain table, sketch input, butterfly/calendar checks, the surface tab). **Risk:** Bund vols at 3.6 % look like a typo next to equities; the page must say "bond futures are quiet — the *shape* is what matters" and offer the OEX chain (Fig. 18-11) as the second preset; the sketch tool needs guard rails.

### Concept 3 — from the smile to the density (step-through)

**Mode:** step-through derivation (catalogue B4-style, one click per step), with the butterfly as the physical object.

**Storyboard (7 beats):**
1. Three call tickets on a table: strikes K − δ, K, K + δ. Click: buy one, sell two, buy one. The payoff triangle appears — height δ, base 2δ, centred at K.
2. Click: shrink δ. The triangle narrows and, scaled by 1/δ², becomes a spike at K: "this spread pays one unit if the stock lands near K". Its price is therefore "the discounted probability of landing near K" — the density, in tickets.
3. Click: the three prices from Hull's Table 15.3 at 45/50/55 (7.168, 4.422, 2.277) are substituted into e^{rT}(c₁ + c₃ − 2c₂)/δ² → 0.0243 at 50; then at 40, 45, 55, 60 (0.0387, 0.0372, 0.0243, 0.0304, 0.0308): a *dip* at 50 — the two-humped Fig. 15.5 recovered from seven prices, with the frown smile of Fig. 15.6 beside it.
4. Click: swap the chain for a smooth smile (Hull Fig. 15.1 shape) and the density becomes Fig. 15.2 — "heavier tails *and* a sharper peak"; swap for the equity skew (Fig. 15.3) and it becomes Fig. 15.4 — "heavy left tail, light right". The two-OTM-options argument from Hull p. 332–335 appears as two shaded tails with the matching smile points lit.
5. Click: the total mass readout, 0.59 for a sparse chain (metricgate's number), 0.98 for a dense one: "what the chain cannot see, the density cannot show".
6. Click: a negative value flashes where the butterfly is mispriced (Natenberg's 88.50 call): "a negative probability is a free butterfly".
7. Hand-off to D3: "models (local vol, Heston, SABR) are machines that produce an *entire* smile from a few knobs; here you built one from the tickets."

**Makes visible:** that the second derivative is a *spread you can buy*, and that smile shape ⇔ density shape is a one-step mapping. **Cost:** medium (finite differences, the three-ticket animation, chain presets). **Risk:** "second difference" may still read as calculus; keep the ticket picture on screen throughout and the formula under it.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

**Definitions and identities:**
- Implied vol σ_imp(K, T): the unique σ with C_BS(S, K, r, q, σ, T) = C_mkt, existing iff max(S e^{−qT} − K e^{−rT}, 0) < C_mkt < S e^{−qT} (Wilmott p. 834; C3's solver). For futures-style options with r = 0 (Natenberg's Bund) use Black-76 with F in place of S e^{(r−q)T}.
- Put–call parity ⇒ σ_imp(call) = σ_imp(put) for Europeans (Hull (15.1)–(15.2), p. 330–331).
- Smile coordinates: strike; log-moneyness x = ln(K/F₀); Natenberg's x/√T (Hull p. 336: (1/√T) ln(K/F₀)); Natenberg's y = σ(K)/σ_ATM. The engine must offer all three and state which.
- Term structure: total variance w(T) = σ_imp(T)² T must be non-decreasing in T at fixed moneyness (no calendar arbitrage, Gatheral p. 26); the forward variance between T₁ and T₂ is (σ₂²T₂ − σ₁²T₁)/(T₂ − T₁) (Wilmott (50.1)–(50.2), "variances add up, not standard deviations", p. 837).
- Breeden–Litzenberger: g(K) = e^{rT} ∂²C/∂K² (Hull App. 15A, p. 345); finite-difference e^{rT}(C(K−h) − 2C(K) + C(K+h))/h² (Hull p. 345; metricgate); the butterfly value must be ≥ 0 (no negative density); vertical spreads must be between 0 and e^{−rT}h.
- Sticky rules (Hull p. 337; Wilmott p. 216–217; Derman 1999): sticky strike σ(K) fixed ⇒ Δ = Δ_BS; sticky delta σ(K/S) fixed ⇒ Δ = Δ_BS + vega · ∂σ_imp/∂S, which for an equity skew (∂σ/∂S > 0 at fixed K) makes Δ > Δ_BS; sticky implied tree ⇒ Δ < Δ_BS. Neither heuristic is arbitrage-free except for a flat surface.
