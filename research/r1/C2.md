## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

If a price can only go to two places, you can copy an option's payoff exactly with some shares and some borrowed cash, so the option must cost what the copy costs — and the chance of going up never entered the calculation.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**A puzzle (a bet the fresher will lose).** Wilmott §15.14, in full: "Two stocks A and B. Both have same value, same volatility and are denominated in the same currency. Both have call options with the same strike and expiration. Stock A is doubling in value every year, stock B is halving. Therefore both call options have the same value. But which would you buy?" (p. 278). Before that: *A stock is 20. In three months it will be 22 or 18. What would you pay today for the right to buy it at 21?* Almost every fresher reaches for a probability — "half of 1 is 0.50", or "depends on how likely 22 is". The answer is 0.633 and needs no probability at all. Hull says it himself: "we get the same option price when the probability of an upward movement is 0.5 as we do when it is 0.9. This is surprising and seems counterintuitive" (p. 203). A guess typed before the reveal is the proven hook (catalogue A2 rule 14); the surprise *is* the chapter.

**A story with stakes: the market-maker who must not care.** You sold the 21-call and collected the premium. The stock will be 22 or 18 and you do not know which. Can you arrange never to lose? Wilmott: "There are two ways to ensure that we have −99/2 tomorrow. 1. Buy one option and sell one half of the stock. 2. Put the money under the mattress. Both of these 'portfolios' must be worth the same today" (p. 265). Hull's version: if the option sold for more than 0.633 "the portfolio would cost less than 4.367 to set up and would earn more than the risk-free rate" (p. 201). Wilmott's undercutting story makes the price *enforced*: "A sells the option for 0.55 … Along comes B who sells the option for just 0.53; now he takes away all the business from A, who responds by dropping his price to 0.52 etc." (p. 266). Stakes: free money for whoever sees it first.

**An everyday object: the Galton board, on its side.** The fresher has already dropped 500 balls in 0.2 and watched the board rotate (F10): pegs became days, bounces became up/down moves, buckets became where the price ends. Now paint an option's payoff on the buckets — the ones above 21 light up by how much — and the option's price is some weighted average of the lit buckets, discounted. Which weights? Not the ones the balls actually use. Wilmott: "The binomial tree therefore contains within it an approximation to the probability density function for the lognormal random walk" (p. 280). Continuity, not surprise: the lattice is a thing they own.

**Strongest: the puzzle, as a typed bet, immediately followed by the market-maker story that resolves it; the Galton board is the stage both play on.** The Galton hook alone gives recognition but no tension; the bet gives the wrong intuition a name before the hedge argument kills it.

### Common misconceptions (with the source that corrects them)

- *"p is the probability the stock goes up."* Hull p. 205: "In general this is not the same as the probability of an up movement in the real world"; Wilmott p. 273: "Do not think that this p′ is in any sense real."
- *"So probabilities are irrelevant to options."* Wilmott p. 266: the *range* matters — the two prices 22 and 18 are what volatility means here; and Hull p. 203: the real probabilities are already inside the stock's price of 20.
- *"Discount the real expected payoff at the stock's return."* Hull p. 205 footnote: the right rate for this call is 42.6 %, which you cannot know without the price; Harper: discounting "is always with the risk-free rate".
- *"You estimate q from data."* (Bionic Turtle learner.) q = (e^{rδt} − d)/(u − d) is fixed by the tree; nothing is estimated.
- *"More steps is always closer."* n = 20 gives 10.3513 (error −0.099) while n = 21 gives 10.5344 (+0.084): the sawtooth; only odd-with-odd and even-with-even improve monotonically (Leisen–Reimer).
- *"Exercise an American call early when it is deep in the money."* Hull §8.5 and p. 209: node B's early exercise is worth −8; the put at node C is the one that exercises (12 > 9.4636).
- *"The tree forecasts the path."* It is a lattice of possibilities that recombines: 31 terminal prices but 2³⁰ paths (Hull p. 213); one highlighted path is a story, the buckets are the distribution (0.2's lesson again).
- *"Δ is the probability of finishing in the money."* Δ = (f_u − f_d)/(S_u − S_d) is a slope and a share count (Hull §10.6); it only resembles a probability.

### Concept A — Step-through on the mini lattice: set the hedge by hand (B4 + B9 + B82 on the F10 board)

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right

- One step (Hull §10.1, pp. 201–203): Δ = (f_u − f_d)/(S₀u − S₀d); f = S₀Δ − (S₀uΔ − f_u)e^{−rT}; equivalently f = e^{−rT}[p f_u + (1 − p) f_d], p = (e^{rT} − d)/(u − d). Requires d < e^{rT} < u (else arbitrage) — a test case.
- Under p, E[S_T] = S₀e^{rT} (10.4, p. 204). Real-world q with return μ: q = (e^{μT} − d)/(u − d); the real expected payoff discounted at the *unknown* rate equals f (Hull p. 205).
- Two steps (10.5–10.8, p. 207): f = e^{−2rδt}[p²f_uu + 2p(1 − p)f_ud + (1 − p)²f_dd]; n steps: Σ C(n, k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ payoff(S₀uᵏdⁿ⁻ᵏ), discounted — the 0.2 binomial.
- American (§10.5): V = max(continuation, intrinsic) at every node, backward.
- CRR parameters (10.11–10.13, pp. 211–213): u = e^{σ√δt}, d = 1/u, p = (e^{rδt} − d)/(u − d); matches variance σ²δt to first order; volatility unchanged by the change of measure (p. 212).
- Limit: as n → ∞ the terminal distribution → lognormal with ln S_T ~ N(ln S₀ + (r − σ²/2)T, σ²T) and the price → Black–Scholes; Δ → ∂V/∂S; the recursion → the BS PDE (Wilmott §15.21). Error of CRR is O(1/n) and oscillates in n (Leisen–Reimer).

## 7. Where it connects

- **0.2** — the F10 lattice is reused unchanged; the path counts C(n, k) are the weights of eq. 10.8; the bell in the buckets is the one the fresher already made.
- **C1** — the payoff painted on the buckets; parity verified on the tree (Hull Problems 10.6, 10.12: call and put from the same tree satisfy c + Ke^{−rT} = p + S₀) — a free test.
- **B1** — the lattice as a random walk; **B2** — the discounted price as the meter that does not move along the tree; **B5** — q as a change of measure ("changing the measure", Hull p. 212; the forum's "pricing vector").
- **C3** — the n → ∞ limit is the formula; the Δ → ∂V/∂S handover.
- **C4 / D1** — Δ changes node to node (0.5064 → 0.7273 or 0), so the hedge is re-set every step; the market-maker of the hook becomes D1's hedger.
- **F1 / F2** — backward induction is the DP fill; Longstaff–Schwartz replaces the two-child average with a regression. **E2** — 2³⁰ paths on the same lattice.
- **The real-world moment:** the first pricing-library call that returns a price *and a delta* — and the first time someone asks "what's your delta?" meaning "how many shares are you holding against that option".
