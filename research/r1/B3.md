# B3 Brownian motion, GBM, quadratic variation, mean reversion

_R1 deep note, 2026-10-22. Options for discussion, not decisions. Companion: `research/r1/B4.md` (Itô), which assumes the "odometer" built here._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

Zoom into a random price path for ever and it never turns into a straight line, yet one tally on that same path — the running total of its squared wiggles — is as steady as a clock, and that single steady tally is the thing the rest of continuous-time finance is built on.

Secondary feelings: the wiggle over a short interval is proportional to the *square root* of the interval, which is why "√t" is everywhere; Mel from A1 in continuous time is geometric Brownian motion, and her median still trails her mean; a leash turns a wanderer into something that stays home (mean reversion), and a too-coarse clock makes the leash misbehave.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**(a) The puzzle: three dials on a drunk car.** A car drives a random road. On the dashboard: a *speedometer* (change in position ÷ the sampling interval), an *odometer* (total distance travelled, up-moves and down-moves both counted) and a strange third dial that adds up the *squares* of the moves. Now halve the sampling interval, and halve it again, and again. Which dials settle to a reading? Freshers reliably say "the speedometer, obviously; the others are bookkeeping". On a Brownian path the speedometer slams to the stop (its typical reading goes 4, 16, 64, 256 as the interval goes 1/16, 1/256, 1/4096, 1/65 536), the odometer runs away (3.2, 12.8, 51, 204 over one unit of time), and only the squares dial sits still — at exactly the elapsed time. Hull, p. 219, states the two "intriguing properties" the puzzle rests on: "The expected length of the path followed by z in any time interval is infinite" and "the expected number of times z equals any particular value in any time interval is infinite." The reader gets the guess wrong, and the wrong guess is the chapter.

**(b) The story with stakes: you can measure volatility from one morning, but not the expected return from ten years.** Shreve, p. 86: the variance of a random walk "is computed by taking an average over all paths" whereas the quadratic variation "is computed along a single path", and "from tick-by-tick price data, one can compute the quadratic variation along the realized path rather explicitly." Then §3.4.3, p. 106–107: the sum of squared log-returns over a window, divided by the window, "≈ σ²" (eq. 3.4.16) — with the honest warning that "there is a limit to how small the step size can be … the difference in prices due to the bid-ask spread can be as large as the difference due to price fluctuations." The stake for a fresher: the first number a desk asks for ("what's realised vol today?") is a quadratic-variation computation, and the first thing they learn not to trust (a drift estimated from a short history) is a first-order quantity that one path cannot pin down.

**(c) The everyday object: pollen, dust, a particle in a box.** Brown's 1827 pollen (mbrenndoerfer's opener; Hull p. 218: "the motion of a particle that is subject to a large number of small molecular shocks"). 3Blue1Brown has already built the picture: `_2019/diffyq/part2/staging.py` class `BlackScholes` hides the hundred jostling particles, keeps the big one's height, and draws that height against time under the Black-Scholes equation as a price chart. Gentle, memorable, but physics first and finance second.

**Strongest: (a).** It is a predict-then-reveal (catalogue rule 14), it contains every fact of the chapter (no derivative; infinite first variation; finite quadratic variation = t), and it gives B4 its central object. (b) becomes the pay-off section; (c) becomes the GBM/OU opener where "one rule changed" is the structure.

### Common misconceptions

- "(ΔW)² ≈ Δt step by step." Shreve Remark 3.4.4; riskhub's 68 %/0.455 numbers. The rule is about the *sum*.
- "Zoom far enough and the path is smooth." Hull p. 219, Shreve p. 102, Wikipedia: nowhere differentiable; the secant ratio grows like 1/√Δt.
- "Risk scales with time." It scales with √time (Hull p. 217–218); and for a mean-reverting quantity not even that (riskhub: 45 %/213 % overstatement).
- "Quadratic variation is just the variance." Variance is an average over paths; QV is read off one path (Shreve p. 86) — this is why σ is measurable and μ is not.
- "Any shrinking step works." Steps ∝ Δt freeze the walk; steps ∝ Δt^{1/4} blow it up (Wilmott's *Finiteness*, p. 74; mbrenndoerfer).
- "The average GBM path grows at μ." The mean does; the median grows at μ − ½σ² (Shreve p. 148; A1's 98.76).
- "Finer data always gives a better σ." Bid-ask bounce (Shreve p. 107).
- "Euler is fine for anything." κΔt ≥ 1 breaks OU (riskhub table); large Δt gives negative GBM prices (riskhub Euler-Maruyama post, from the R0 audit).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three different modes

### C1 — "Zoom forever" (scrubbable animation with an exact Brownian-bridge zoom)

Mode: scrubbable animation with two buttons, inside a short explorable.

1. The smooth curve from 0.1's F7 (cos x) with the zoom rectangle drawn on the wide shot; "Zoom ×10" — the curve and its tangent coincide. Scale label "×10". Second press: "×100", still a line. (Catalogue B12; `alt_calc.py` move.)
2. Cut to a Brownian path on the same axes. "Zoom ×10": the frame narrows and the path *grows new detail* — each press refines the stored path by midpoint displacement (midpoint = average of the ends + N(0, Δt/4)), seeded per interval so zooming out and back in reproduces the same wiggles. Scale label ×10, ×100, ×1,000, ×10,000. It never straightens.
3. Toggle "stretch the value axis by √(zoom)": the zoomed picture becomes statistically indistinguishable from the first — Yale's "time ×4, space ×2". Caption: *a Brownian path is the same at every scale, provided you measure height in √time.*
4. The secant triangle (chapter2.py `get_ds_dt_group`) pinned at the zoom point; its printed ratio reads 4, 16, 64, 256 … (typical |ΔW|/Δt = 1/√Δt) while the same widget on cos printed a converging decimal. Caption: *no derivative — not "hard to compute", none.*
5. Measured companion panel (rule 5): |Δ(path)| against Δt on log-log, slope ½ for the Brownian path, slope 1 for cos.
6. Closing line, Hull p. 219: when δt is small, "√δt is much bigger than δt."

Makes visible: nowhere-differentiability and √t scaling as *experiences*, not statements. Cost: medium — a seeded hierarchical path store (dyadic intervals keyed by level and index), the frame zoom, and the stretch toggle; the engine test is that the refined path's increments have the right variance at every level. Risk: a fresher may think the zoom is "making things up"; the caption must say the bridge is exactly what a Brownian path looks like in between its samples (Cook's post), and the stretch toggle must be explained before it is pressed.

### C2 — "Three dials on a drunk car" (comic strip → instrument panel)

Mode: a four-panel drawn comic strip, then an explorable with dials.

1. Comic, panel 1: a car on a jittery road; dashboard with three dials — speedometer (move ÷ interval), odometer (moves added up, both directions), and a third dial labelled "wiggle² total" (squares added up). Panel 2: the driver halves the clock tick. Panel 3: speedometer pinned at the stop, odometer spinning, the third dial unmoved. Panel 4: the passenger: "that one reads the clock."
2. Guess-then-reveal before the widget: "which dial settles?" with three buttons.
3. Instrument panel: one Brownian path on [0, 1]; a *Refine* button n = 16 → 256 → 4,096 → 65,536 (B14's step counter); the three dials with decimals (`OdometerScene` pattern): speedometer typical 4, 16, 64, 256; odometer 3.2, 12.8, 51, 204; squares 0.78, 0.96, 0.985, 1.003 (the fixed-seed run in §6) → 1. The path is *refined by bridge*, not redrawn, so the eye tracks the same wiggles (rule 17).
4. "Open the ledger": the per-step ratios (ΔW)²/Δt as a histogram beside the dial — 68 % below 1, median 0.455 — with Shreve's sentence "not near 1, regardless of how small". The dial is steady because errors *cancel*, not because each step is right.
5. Wedges: equal-Δt wedges shaded along the path, each contributing ≈ Δt to the dial; "accumulates quadratic variation at rate one per unit time" (Shreve p. 105).
6. Pay-off: run the same dial on Mel's log-price (σ = 0.4): it reads σ²t = 0.16 at t = 1 — Shreve's realised-volatility formula (3.4.16). A "measure σ from one path" readout; a "measure μ from one path" readout that will not settle.
7. Break-it: "add a bid-ask bounce of 2 ticks" — the dial over-reads at the finest refinement (Shreve p. 107).

Makes visible: convergence as something watched on an instrument; the difference between a sum that settles and terms that do not. Cost: low–medium; the dial component is reusable in B2, B4, D1 (catalogue B82). Risk: "speedometer = derivative" assumes 0.1's F7; the dials need units; the car must not become a joke that hides the maths — the comic is four panels, no more.

### C3 — "The same particle, one rule changed" (physical simulation clip → fan → leash)

Mode: a Primer-style particle clip that morphs into the fan explorable, then a kickable spring.

1. The 3b1b box: one big dot jostled by a hundred small ones; its height drawn as a chart to the right (the `BlackScholes` scene exactly). Fade the box; keep the chart.
2. "Run 1,000 of these" (rule 5): the fan with the √t envelope — Hull Example 11.1 numbers (sd 1 at one year, 2.236 at five) on the envelope.
3. Switch to "Mel": the same jostles applied as percentages (dS = μS dt + σS dW). The fan is now lognormal; two lines drawn: mean S₀e^{μt} and median S₀e^{(μ−½σ²)t}. For S₀ = 100, μ = 10 %, σ = 40 %: 110.52 vs 102.02 at one year, 271.8 vs 122.1 at ten. The Bilkent-style "mean and median separate as returns compound". Caption: *this is A1's 98.76 in continuous time; B4 explains where the ½σ² comes from.*
4. Clip a leash on (riskhub's dog): the dot is pulled back toward a post with strength κ; the fan stops widening at σ/√(2κ); half-life ln 2/κ marked; a "kick" button (B69) displaces the dot and the expected path decays home. Wilmott's "isn't going anywhere" (p. 86).
5. Break-it: a "clock tick Δt" dial with the κΔt readout; exact and Euler paths from the same random numbers (B54/B55); at κΔt = 0.5 the Euler path forgets its start in one step, at κΔt = 1 it oscillates and its spread "grows without limit".

Makes visible: random walk → Brownian motion → GBM → OU as one object with one rule changed per section (catalogue rule 3). Cost: medium–high — a collision simulation, three processes, common random numbers. Risk: the physics is charming but off the spine; GBM and OU in one figure is two ideas; the leash metaphor must not suggest the process is bounded (it is not — only its spread is).

## 5. My recommendation

**A sequence of two: C1 as a 90-second opener, C2 as the spine; GBM and OU as two short closing sections that lift beats 3–5 of C3 without the particle box.**

Easy to understand first: a dial with units and a zoom button are concrete; both answer a question the fresher just asked ("does it ever straighten?", "which dial settles?") rather than introduce notation. Interesting second: both are predict-then-reveal with a wrong guess built in; the "wiggle² dial reads the clock" is a genuine surprise, and it is the exact object B4 needs ("the (dW)² tile refuses to shrink" is this dial). Interactive third: the interaction is one button (zoom / refine) per figure, which is the catalogue's rule 10, and the dials are reused in B2, B4 and D1.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, and reference numbers for tests

Definitions and facts (with source):
- Brownian motion: W(0) = 0, continuous paths, independent increments, W(t) − W(s) ~ N(0, t − s) (Shreve Def. 3.3.1, p. 94; Hull eq. 11.1 and Property 2, p. 218). Hull's discrete form δz = ε√δt, ε ~ N(0, 1).
- Scaled random walk W⁽ⁿ⁾(t) = M_{nt}/√n with Var = t and [W⁽ⁿ⁾, W⁽ⁿ⁾](t) = t exactly (Shreve 3.2.7, 3.2.8, 3.2.10, pp. 86–88); Wilmott's bet √(t/n) (p. 74). Step scaling Δx = σ√Δt is forced by finite variance (mbrenndoerfer; Wilmott *Finiteness*).
- Quadratic variation: E[(ΔW)²] = Δt, Var[(ΔW)²] = 2(Δt)², Var(Q_Π) ≤ 2‖Π‖T, [W, W](T) = T (Shreve Thm 3.4.3, eqs 3.4.6–3.4.7, pp. 102–103). For n equal steps on [0, 1], sd(Q) = √(2/n): 0.354, 0.088, 0.0221, 0.0055 for n = 16, 256, 4,096, 65,536. Cross rules dW dt = 0, dt dt = 0 (3.4.14, p. 106). Ratio (ΔW)²/Δt ~ χ²₁: P(< 1) = 0.683, median 0.455 (riskhub).
- First variation Σ|ΔW| over [0, 1]: expectation √n · √(2/π) = 3.19, 12.8, 51.1, 204.3 for the same n (unbounded; Hull p. 219). Secant ratio |ΔW|/Δt typical 1/√Δt = 4, 16, 64, 256.
- Generalised Wiener dx = a dt + b dz: change over T ~ N(aT, b√T) (Hull 11.3, p. 219–221). GBM dS = μS dt + σS dz, δS/S ~ φ(μδt, σ√δt) (Hull 11.6–11.9, pp. 223–224). Closed form S(t) = S₀ exp{σW(t) + (μ − ½σ²)t} (Shreve 3.2.16 p. 92 and 4.4.28 p. 148; Wilmott p. 85; Hull 11.18–11.19 p. 228 — B3 may *state* it and defer the derivation to B4, or use Shreve's binomial route p. 91–93 which needs only log(1 + x) = x − ½x², i.e. A1's F2).
- Lognormal moments at time t: mean S₀e^{μt}, median S₀e^{(μ−½σ²)t}, mode S₀e^{(μ−3σ²/2)t} (randomservices; A1 §6).
- Realised variance: (1/(T₂ − T₁)) Σ (log S(t_{j+1})/S(t_j))² ≈ σ² (Shreve 3.4.16, p. 107).
- OU / Vasicek: dR = (α − βR)dt + σ dW; R(t) = e^{−βt}R(0) + (α/β)(1 − e^{−βt}) + σ∫e^{−β(t−s)}dW(s); mean e^{−βt}R(0) + (α/β)(1 − e^{−βt}); variance (σ²/2β)(1 − e^{−2βt}) (Shreve Ex. 4.4.10, pp. 150–151; Wilmott 4.12, p. 87). In κ, θ notation: long-run sd σ/√(2κ), half-life ln 2/κ; exact step uses e^{−κΔ}; Euler gap factor 1 − κΔ (riskhub). Discrete-time AR(1) is OU sampled (Wikipedia; link A4).
