## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

Three jobs a desk does every day with no formula in sight: decide, on simulated paths, when to exercise (a regression on the paths themselves); make a model agree with the market's prices (a least-squares fit with a long flat valley in it); and get every sensitivity of a price in one pass by differentiating the program rather than bumping it. Each is ordinary arithmetic the reader already has — least squares from A3, paths from E2, the chain rule — pointed at a problem that used to take a formula.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: eight paths and a put you may exercise early.** Hull's (and Longstaff–Schwartz's own) example: a three-year put struck at 1.10 on a stock at 1.00, eight simulated paths, exercise allowed at years 1, 2, 3. At year 2 the option is in the money on five paths. Which of them should exercise? You cannot know the value of waiting from one path — but you can fit a curve through the five paths' later cash flows against their current prices and read it off. Three coefficients, two regressions, and the price is 0.1144 against 0.10 for exercising now. Strongest, because every number fits on one screen and the reader can check each regression; it is A3's least squares doing a job A3 never mentioned.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: the Greeks that took all night.** A bank's overnight risk run on a book of exotics bumps every input and reprices: forty inputs, forty-one Monte Carlo runs, hours. Giles and Glasserman's 2006 paper showed that differentiating the pricing *program* backwards gives all forty sensitivities for about the cost of one more run; the paper was named "Smoking adjoints" and the technique "is used extensively by Credit Suisse and other major banks" (REF impact case study). The fresher's version is smaller and exact: carry a derivative alongside every intermediate number in Black-76 and delta, vega and theta fall out to fifteen digits, where a bump gives six on a good day and nonsense on a bad one. Good as Figure 3's opener.

**Hook C — the everyday object: tuning a radio to a station.** Calibration is turning knobs until the model's prices match the screen's, and the fresher's expectation is that the knobs have one right setting. They do not: on one expiry there is a long valley of settings that fit equally well (D3's round 3 said so; this chapter shows the valley), and a desk picks a point in it for reasons the smile cannot give. Wilmott's candour — "from a practical perspective it is hard to argue against calibration; you cannot hedge with something if your theoretical price is very different from its traded price. But from a modeling and empirical perspective it is hard to argue in its favor" — is the caption.

**Recommendation: A opens, C is Figure 2's frame, B opens Figure 3.**

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

- **Mike Giles, Oxford lecture notes "Advanced Monte Carlo Methods: Computing Greeks"** (people.maths.ox.ac.uk/gilesm/mc/module_6/adjoints.pdf): the cleanest ladder — finite differences → likelihood ratio → pathwise → "Smoking adjoints" implementation — and the note that "Generating the pathwise sensitivity code is tedious, but straightforward, and can be automated". Works because it puts the four methods in order of what each needs to know about the program.
- **Giles & Glasserman, "Smoking adjoints: fast Monte Carlo Greeks"** (Risk, Jan 2006; Oxford report 05/15): "an adjoint method to accelerate the calculation of Greeks by Monte Carlo simulation. The method calculates price sensitivities along each path; but in contrast to a forward pathwise calculation…" and the result that "the computational advantage of the adjoint method grows in proportion to the number of initial forward rates". The REF 2021 impact study: "used extensively by Credit Suisse and other major banks".
- **Huge & Savine, "Differential Machine Learning"** (arXiv 2005.02347): pathwise differentials "conveniently and efficiently computed with automatic adjoint differentiation (AAD) over Monte-Carlo paths as explained in the founding paper Smoking Adjoints" — the modern descendant, one sentence in the close.
- **Informa Connect, "Lessons from 10+ years of Algorithmic Differentiation in computational finance"**: "Computational Finance has become one of the major target applications for AAD" and the caveat "Non-smoothness" — the digital-payoff warning in practitioners' words.
- Not opened: Longstaff & Schwartz (2001) itself (RFS 14:113–147; Hull reproduces its example), Andersen (2000) on boundary parametrisation, Capriotti's AAD papers.

### Common misconceptions

1. **"Regression on the same paths is cheating."** It biases the estimate (optimistic at small n, low in the limit); the cure is a second set of paths for the valuation, and the page says which bias it is showing.
2. **"More basis functions are always better."** Over-fitting at the exercise decision; Glasserman's Table 8.1 shows a poor basis costs more than a few thousand paths, and a cubic on our put moves the answer by half a standard error.
3. **"Calibration finds the model's true parameters."** It finds a point in a valley; D3's κ and this chapter's (ρ, ν) ridge are the pictures.
4. **"A smaller bump is a better derivative."** Truncation error falls with h, round-off rises as 1/h: the best relative bump for a central difference is about 10⁻⁵ here and the error curve is a V (Glasserman §7.1).
5. **"Automatic differentiation is symbolic differentiation."** It differentiates the *program*, number by number, by the chain rule; no expression is ever formed. Forward mode: one pass per input. Reverse mode: one pass for all inputs.
6. **"The pathwise delta works for everything."** Not for a digital payoff — the derivative is zero almost everywhere; likelihood-ratio and "vibrato" methods exist for that (Giles).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — Hull's eight paths (step-through with a bet — the regression you can check)
(1) The eight paths as eight lines (Table 20.1), the put's strike 1.10, exercise dates at years 1, 2, 3. (2) Bet: "at year 2, path 1 is at 1.08 — exercise (worth 0.02) or wait?" (3) The five in-the-money paths at year 2, their later cash flows discounted one year plotted against their price, the fitted parabola −1.070 + 2.983S − 1.813S² and the five continuation values against the five exercise values: 4, 6, 7 exercise. (4) The same at year 1 (2.038 − 3.335S + 1.356S²): 4, 6, 7, 8 exercise. (5) The cash-flow table 20.4 and the average of the discounted cash flows, 0.1144, against 0.10 for exercising today and 0.0564 for a European put on the same eight paths. Readouts: coefficients, continuation vs exercise count, price. Steps only.

### Concept 2 — At scale: the LSM price against F1's grid (dial on paths; segmented basis) + the calibration valley (heat-map with two dials)
(a) Hull's Example 18.1 put (F1's textbook American) on 50 exercise dates; dial **paths** 1,000 → 50,000; the price with its error bar against the grid's 4.284 (F1) and the European 4.076; the exercise boundary the regression found as a staircase beside F1's; segmented **basis** quadratic / cubic. (b) The calibration valley: SABR on the real 34 dots; a heat-map of the rms error over (ρ, ν) with α set by the at-the-money vol; dials **ρ** and **ν** that move a dot on the map and redraw the fitted smile over the dots; the valley (rms within 0.05 vol points of the best) shaded — ρ from −0.4 to −0.2, ν from 1.75 to 4.25. ### Concept 3 — The tape (step-through) + bump-and-revalue's V + the Monte Carlo delta (one dial)
(1) Black-76 as six nodes — ln(F/K), d₁, d₂, N(d₁), N(d₂), price — each carrying its value and its derivative with respect to F (a dual number): the last node's derivative is delta, 0.50642…, equal to C4's closed form to fifteen digits; switch the seed to σ and the tape gives vega 1,562.07; to T, theta. (2) Dial **relative bump** 10⁻¹ → 10⁻¹²: the forward and central difference errors against the exact delta on a log-log axis — the V, with its floor near 10⁻⁵ (central) and the round-off climb below 10⁻⁸. (3) The Monte Carlo delta of the real call on 20,000 seeded draws: pathwise 0.5065 ± 0.0036; bump with common random numbers 0.5063 ± 0.0033; bump with fresh numbers 0.579 ± 0.018 — the picture of why E2 insisted on the same seed. Caption: forward mode is one pass per input; reverse mode (the adjoint) is one pass for all; the digital payoff's zero derivative.
