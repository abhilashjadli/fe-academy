# C4 Greeks I: delta, gamma, theta, vega, rho — and the equation that ties them

_Phase R1 deep research note (sprint of 2026-10-22). Options for discussion with the sponsor, not decisions. Prerequisites: 0.1 (Taylor — this chapter is the promise that chapter ended on: "every Greek is a Taylor coefficient"), C1–C3. Stands alone: the price curve is drawn before any Greek is named._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

An option's price is a curved surface over "where the stock is" and "how long is left"; the Greeks are just the slope, the bend, and the sinking rate of that surface at the spot you are standing on — and the surface sinks exactly as fast as it bends, which is why nobody gets curvature for free.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the story with stakes: Hull's bank that sold a call on 100,000 shares.** A financial institution sells a call for $300,000 that is worth $240,000 (S = 49, K = 50, r = 5 %, σ = 20 %, 20 weeks). Do nothing and "the cost is liable to range from zero to over $1,000,000" (Hull p. 300). Try the obvious fix — buy the stock when it crosses $50, sell when it crosses back — and the simulation says you cannot get the cost's standard deviation below 0.70 of the premium "regardless of how small δt is made" (Table 14.1, p. 302): "As ε → 0, the expected number of trades tends to infinity." Then the real fix, buying 52,200 shares (delta 0.522) and adjusting weekly: the cost lands at $263,300 or $256,600 on two sample paths, close to the $240,000 price (Tables 14.2–14.3). The Greeks are what you read off to do that. Strongest: it is the one story every fresher at a sell-side firm will live, and the naive hedge failing first is the Ciechanowski move (B8).

**Hook B — the puzzle: "a bet that pays if the stock moves — in either direction — and still loses money."** Buy a call and sell the right amount of stock: you are flat. Stock goes up: you make money. Stock goes down: you also make money (Hull Fig. 14.8b, p. 313). So where is the catch? Every day the position quietly leaks. Natenberg: "Every option position is a tradeoff between market movement and time decay … The trader can't have it both ways. Either he wants the market to move, or he wants it to sit still" (p. 111). The hook is a puzzle whose resolution is the PDE. Very good, but needs delta hedging explained first, so it sits in the middle of the chapter.

**Hook C — the everyday object: speed, acceleration, and the odometer.** Moontower's picture: P&L is distance, delta is speed, gamma is acceleration, so the gamma P&L is the triangle ½·a·t², exactly as in school physics; "The graphs are exactly the same we just changed the words." An engineer feels at home instantly, and it reconnects to 0.1 (the ½ is 1/2!). Cheapest hook; not a story.

**Strongest: A as the opening, C as the mid-chapter re-explanation of the Taylor terms, B as the turn before the PDE.**

### Common misconceptions

- "Delta is the probability of finishing in the money." Approximately, and only for the risk-neutral measure; the exact probability is N(d₂) (Natenberg p. 102; bionicturtle; macroption).
- "Theta is a risk to be hedged." Hull p. 311: "there is no uncertainty about the passage of time … it does not make any sense to hedge against the effect of the passage of time"; theta is a *descriptive* number and a proxy for gamma.
- "Theta is always negative." Hull p. 310 fn. 8 and Natenberg p. 113: deep ITM European puts (and calls on high-rate currencies) can have positive theta.
- "Gamma of a put is negative." Natenberg p. 103: "both calls and puts must have positive gammas … This often confuses the new trader."
- Units: theta per year vs per calendar day (÷365) vs per trading day (÷252); vega per 1.00 vs per 1 %; Natenberg's deltas in "percent format" (50 = 0.50). Hull p. 310; drwong conventions. The chapter must print units on every tile.
- "A delta-hedged position has no risk." It has gamma risk between rebalances (Hull Fig. 14.7) and vega risk always (Natenberg p. 110: raising the vol assumption moves delta from 25 to 35 and the "delta-neutral" trader is 40 deltas short).
- "Gamma and theta are independent numbers." The PDE makes them one number in a delta-neutral book (Hull §14.7). Natenberg: "a large positive gamma goes hand in hand with a large negative theta" (p. 111).
- "Vega makes sense for every contract." Wilmott p. 128: only for single-signed gamma.
- "Rho is important." Natenberg p. 117: "few traders pay much attention to the rho"; treat it in one figure.

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "Walk on the surface" (mode: explorable — 3-D price surface over (S, T) with a probe and a synced 2-D slice)

Beats:
1. Open with the C3 price curve V(S) drawn flat. Rotate it up into a 3-D frame; copies at T = 1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0.05 are laid behind it and a translucent surface fills in (B65, `TalkThrough1DHeatGraph.show_surface`). At T = 0 the surface is the hockey-stick payoff. The dashed gold ridge S = K is drawn (drwong 3-D).
2. A probe (B66): drag it on the surface; a 2-D slice at the probe's T is drawn beside it with three readouts colour-locked for the chapter — height (price), slope (Δ, red tangent), bend (Γ, yellow arc) — the `show_value_slope_curvature` triple.
3. Turn the camera 80°: the same probe, the other leg — a nudge along T. The sinking rate is Θ (the `ShowDelTermsAsTinyNudges` move). Caption: "Two legs, one surface. Delta is how steep it is where you stand; theta is how fast the floor is dropping."
4. Pin (B53): freeze the Δ slice at T = 1, drag T to 0.05, the S-curve becomes a step (Natenberg Fig. 6-11 fanning; Hull Fig. 14.4). Do the same for Γ (bell → spike, Natenberg Fig. 6-9) and Θ (Fig. 6-17).
5. Guess-then-run (B60): "Which is largest for an ATM option one day from expiry: Δ, Γ or Θ?" — commit, then slide T.
6. Vega and rho as two small panels off the surface: vega as the surface inflating when σ is turned (Natenberg: "Decreasing volatility is similar to decreasing time to expiration" — the σ dial and the T dial do the same thing to the surface); rho as a one-line figure.
7. Greeks in dollars (B89): a toggle "1,000 contracts of 100 shares" multiplies every readout — the tile that says Γ = 0.0188 becomes "$1 move changes your share-equivalent by 1,876 shares".

Makes visible: that all the Greeks are *the same object* looked at along different directions, and the whole landscape (Natenberg's 20 figures in one). Costs: Three.js surface, probe, synced slice; the heaviest build in C. Risk: a 3-D surface can be pretty and unread; the synced 2-D slice and the three colour-locked readouts are mandatory, and the surface must be built by stacking the curve the learner already knows, not shown ready-made.

### Concept 2 — "The slope, the bend, the clock" (mode: comic strip in three rows, with Hull's stop-loss table as an animated failure in the gutter)

Beats:
1. Row 1 "The slope": the bank from §2A has sold the call. Three frames: naked (a sweating figure under a $1,000,000 cloud), covered (the same figure holding 100,000 shares and losing $900,000 when the stock drops to $40), and the stop-loss hedger buying at K + ε and selling at K − ε with the path crossing the strike again and again — the frame animates Hull Fig. 14.1 and Table 14.1 counts up (δt = 5 … 0.25 weeks, performance stuck at 0.76). Last frame: "hold 0.522 shares instead" — delta as the slope at the current price (Hull Fig. 14.2).
2. Row 2 "The bend": the slope is different two weeks later (0.4 → 0.5, Hull p. 242); each frame the hedger buys or sells a sliver. Natenberg's trader short 300 deltas who is short 900 after a 10-point rally. Schwab's round trip: short 30 more at 480, buy back at 475, pocket $150. Caption: "because the curve bends, the hedge must move — that bend is gamma, and re-hedging is how gamma turns into money."
3. Row 3 "The clock": the same hedger on a day the stock does not move; the price tile ticks 2.75 → 2.70 → 2.65 (Natenberg p. 111). "Every option position is a tradeoff between market movement and time decay." The strip ends with Hull's Fig. 14.8 drawn as four tiny parabolas: slightly/largely positive gamma, slightly/largely negative.
4. Gutter panel: Hull's Table 14.2 as a 20-week flip-book — the delta column, the shares bought/sold, the cumulative cost converging to $263,300, and the Week-9 reconciliation ($414,500 − $1,442,900 + $1,613,300 ≈ $4,100 net).

Makes visible: *why* each Greek exists, as the failure it fixes (B8), in the order a desk discovers them. Costs: drawn panels, two small animations (crossing path with a trade counter; the table flip-book). Risk: low on comprehension; it does not teach the formulas or the surface, so it must be paired with Concept 1 or 3.

### Concept 3 — "Hedge the book for 30 days" (mode: game, with the P&L split into Δ, Γ, Θ bars)

Beats:
1. The learner is short the Hull call (or the riskhub ATM call), long some shares. One button: "next day". The stock moves (seeded GBM, same seed for everyone so the story is replayable).
2. After each day three bars appear — Δ·δS, ½Γ·δS², Θ·δt — and a thin fourth "everything else" bar (drwong's residual). Their sum is drawn against the exact repricing; the Taylor payoff of 0.1 is now a daily ritual. A big day shows the Γ bar jumping (the ½·a·t² triangle from Moontower is drawn once beside it).
3. The learner chooses each day whether to re-hedge to delta zero (cost in basis points) or not. A score is the standard deviation of the running P&L, shown next to Hull's Table 14.4 numbers (weekly 0.19, daily ≈ 0.09 …).
4. Reveal after 30 days: the Θ bars summed ≈ −(the Γ bars summed) when realised vol = implied, and the position has earned ≈ r on the premium — Wilmott's "you make money 32 % of the time and lose it 68 %, but when you make it you make more; the net effect is the risk-free rate" is checked against the bars.
5. The PDE appears as the day's three bars rearranged: Θ·δt + ½Γ·(σS)²δt + rSΔ·δt = rV·δt, i.e. "what you lose to the clock equals what the average day's bend pays, plus carry".
6. Break-it: set realised vol to 28 % (drwong's default) and watch the Γ bars win; set it to 12 % and watch Θ win. (This is deliberately the door into D1; C4 stops at 30 days and one path.)

Makes visible: the Taylor decomposition as lived experience and the gamma–theta cancellation as an arithmetic fact, not a slogan. Costs: a discrete hedging engine (shared with C3's bookmaker panel and D1), seeded paths, bar chart. Risk: overlaps D1 (whose job is the attribution across many paths and the discrete-hedging error); and a game with a score may read as the "overall game" the charter forbids — it must stay a single in-chapter mechanic with no persistence.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, and reference numbers

Definitions (Hull §14.4–14.9; Wilmott §7.3–7.8), non-dividend stock, N′(x) = e^{−x²/2}/√(2π):
- Δ_call = N(d₁); Δ_put = N(d₁) − 1. With yield q: e^{−qT}N(d₁), e^{−qT}(N(d₁) − 1). Futures: q = r; currency: q = r_f.
- Γ = N′(d₁)/(S₀σ√T) (same for call and put); with q: e^{−qT}N′(d₁)/(S₀σ√T).
- Θ_call = −S₀N′(d₁)σ/(2√T) − rKe^{−rT}N(d₂); Θ_put = −S₀N′(d₁)σ/(2√T) + rKe^{−rT}N(−d₂); per year — ÷365 for calendar day, ÷252 for trading day (Hull p. 310). With q add +qS₀e^{−qT}N(d₁) (call) / −qS₀e^{−qT}N(−d₁) (put) and use e^{−qT} on the first term.
- Vega = S₀√T·N′(d₁) (per unit σ; ÷100 for "per 1 %"); with q multiply by e^{−qT}.
- ρ_call = KTe^{−rT}N(d₂); ρ_put = −KTe^{−rT}N(−d₂) (per unit r; ÷100 for "per 1 %").
- PDE identity (Hull §14.7, eq. 12.15): Θ + rSΔ + ½σ²S²Γ = rV; delta-neutral: Θ + ½σ²S²Γ = rΠ. With yield q: Θ + (r − q)SΔ + ½σ²S²Γ = rV (Wilmott eq. 5.7).
- Taylor P&L (Hull App. 14A, eq. 14A.1): δΠ = Δ δS + Θ δt + ½Γ δS² + (vega) δσ + …; delta-neutral: δΠ ≈ Θ δt + ½Γ δS² (eq. 14.6).
- Hedge-performance measure (Hull Tables 14.1, 14.4): sd(cost of writing + hedging) / BS price.
