## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

A smile is what you get when the wobble is allowed to change instead of being one fixed number — and there are three honest stories for how it changes (it depends on where the price is; it has a random life of its own; or the price sometimes jumps) — all three draw the same curve through today's quotes, and the only way to tell them apart is to ask what they say about tomorrow.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: "three different machines, one identical curve."** Take D2's 34 real dots (calls and puts, 22,250–23,050, 23 Oct). Fit Merton's jump story, Heston's random-wobble story and SABR's desk formula to them. Our key gets rms 0.155, 0.162 and 0.160 vol points respectively — the same as the plain quadratic (0.159). On the screen the three curves lie on top of each other across every quoted strike; at 22,000 they read 17.9 / 17.4 / 17.5 % and at 23,300 15.2 / 15.4 / 15.4 %. The question "which model is right?" cannot be answered by today's chain, and the fresher can see that with their own eyes. **Strongest hook**: it turns a parade of models into one question (what would tell them apart?), and the answer — a second expiry, and what the smile does when the index moves — is the chapter.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: the 5-cent bid for a 13.7-sigma move.** Gatheral Table 5.1 (p. 51): on 15 Sep 2005, with SPX at 1227.73 and expiry the next morning, there was "a 5-cent bid for a 1,160 put which is over 67 points out-of-the-money expiring the following morning … a 67 point move corresponds to around 13.7 standard deviations. The probability of a normally distributed variable making such a move is zero (to about 40 decimal places)." His verdict: "Diffusions just can't generate the size of moves over very short timescales that would be able to generate any value for such options." Someone paid real money for a thing the bell curve says is worthless; the jump story is the only one of the three that can explain the bid. Good as the entry to jumps; a dated, printed artefact.

**Hook C — the everyday object: the same road, three reasons it is bumpy.** hypercall's line for Heston: "Spot is a car and variance is the road surface … The bumpier the road, the noisier the ride." Local vol says the bumpiness is painted on the map (this stretch of road is always rough); Heston says the road itself is alive (rough patches come and go, and tend to come when you drive downhill — ρ < 0); jumps say the road is smooth but there are occasional potholes. Easy, carries all three stories in one picture, and becomes the chapter's running caption rather than its opener.

**Strongest: A as the opener, C as the frame for the three stories, B as the turn into jumps.**

### Common misconceptions

- "Local vol fits every vanilla, so it is the best model." Gatheral p. 8 ("effective theory"), Ch. 8 (future surfaces flat), hypercall ("the smile flattens — wrong"). The right interpolator, the wrong forecaster.
- "Heston's five parameters are five facts about the market." hypercall; Gatheral p. 35 (one expiry gives λ and ρη at best). Our fit: κ = 2 and κ = 20 give identical rms on the real chain.
- "Stochastic vol explains the steep short-dated skew." Gatheral p. 39 and Fig. 5.1: one day out "the Heston smile is flat and completely inconsistent"; that skew is jumps.
- "Jumps make a smile at every maturity." Gatheral Fig. 5.4: beyond T* the distribution "looks more and more normal".
- "SABR is a model of the world." Gatheral p. 91 (no mean reversion); quantt (β fixed, formula as interpolator, negative density in the wings).
- "Model-free smile ⇒ model-free exotics." quantnet (variance swap yes, cliquet no); hypercall (two desks, same vanillas, different barriers).
- "Local volatility is the volatility the stock will have when it gets there." It is E[v_T | S_T = K] — an average over the random wobble of every path ending there.

**Storyboard (8 beats):**

2. Story 1, *painted on the map* (local vol): a strip under the price axis coloured by local wobble (from §6's local-vol curve: 21.7 % at 22,000 down to 14.4 % at 22,750). 6. Break-it: the *second-expiry* button (the data ask). ## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

**Models (risk-neutral, forward form F, r = q = 0 unless stated; T in years):**
- Local vol: dS/S = σ_L(S,t) dZ. Dupire (Gatheral (1.6), forward terms): σ_L²(K,T) = (∂C/∂T) / (½K² ∂²C/∂K²); with rates and dividends (1.4): ∂C/∂T = ½σ²K² ∂²C/∂K² + (r − D)(C − K ∂C/∂K). In implied total variance w(y,T) = σ_BS²T, y = ln(K/F): (1.10) v_L = (∂w/∂T) / [ (1 − (y/w) ∂w/∂y)² − ¼(¼ + 1/w − y²/w²)(∂w/∂y)² + ½ ∂²w/∂y² ]. No skew ⇒ v_L = ∂w/∂T. **Identity (1.12):** σ_L²(K,T) = E[v_T | S_T = K]. Implied-from-local rule (3.11): σ_BS²(K,T) ≈ (1/T)∫₀ᵀ v_L(x̃_t, t) dt along the straight path in log-space.
- Heston: dS = √v S dZ₁, dv = −κ(v − θ) dt + η√v dZ₂, ⟨dZ₁dZ₂⟩ = ρ dt (Gatheral uses λ, v̄; we write κ, θ). Price by the Lewis/Gatheral integral (5.6) with the characteristic function (2.15): C = F − (√(FK)/π) ∫₀^∞ Re[e^{−iuk} φ_T(u − i/2)] / (u² + ¼) du, k = ln(K/F), φ_T(u) = exp(C(u,T)θ + D(u,T)v₀) with (2.12). ATM variance ≈ (3.18); ATM variance skew ≈ (3.21) ρη(1 − (1 − e^{−κT})/(κT))/(κT) → ρη/2 as T → 0. Feller: 2κθ > η². Simulation: Milstein (2.18), full truncation.
- SABR (β = 1 and general): Hagan (2.17a); β = 1 collapses to Gatheral (7.7); ATM skew ∂σ/∂k|₀ = ρ/2 in the short-dated limit.
- Merton: dS/S = (−λk̄) dt + σ dZ + (J − 1) dq, ln J ~ N(μ_J, σ_J²), k̄ = e^{μ_J + σ_J²/2} − 1. Price = Σ_{n≥0} e^{−λ′T}(λ′T)ⁿ/n! · BS(S, K, r_n, σ_n, T), λ′ = λ(1 + k̄), σ_n² = σ² + nσ_J²/T, r_n = r − λk̄ + n ln(1 + k̄)/T (full rate r_n in the discount too). Short-dated ATM variance skew ≈ −2μ_J^{comp} = 2λk̄ (Gatheral (5.10)); characteristic time T* from −k̄ ≈ σ√T*.

What one expiry can and cannot show, honestly: it pins **level, tilt and curvature** — three numbers — and nothing else. Heston's κ and θ are invisible (the κ = 2 / κ = 20 rows); only ρη's sign and rough size and v₀ are read; Merton's λ trades off against μ_J and σ_J (two rows, same curve); SABR's three knobs are exactly the three numbers, which is why desks use it per expiry. Local vol needs ∂w/∂T, which one expiry does not have; the chapter can show it only under a stated assumption (flat total-variance term structure) and must say so. **A second expiry** (the 24 Nov monthly, D2's standing data ask) adds the term structure of ATM vol (pins v₀ vs θ roughly, via (3.18)) and of skew (pins κ and ρη, Gatheral p. 35; separates Merton's fast-dying skew from Heston's 1/T decay) and lets local vol be computed properly. The chapter's honest claim: "with one date, every story fits; with two, they start to disagree; with the index moving, they disagree loudly."

**Assumptions to state:** European options; forward form with one rate; the fits are least squares on IV with equal weights; the Heston integral is the Gatheral/Lewis form (no complex-log branch issue with his C); Merton with r_n in the discount; SABR's Hagan formula is an approximation that degrades in the wings (negative density check: the butterfly flags from D2's engine run on every fitted smile).
