## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

A fair game is one where, knowing everything up to now, your best guess for your money tomorrow is your money today — and no clever way of betting or quitting can change that average, which is exactly why a price can be computed as an average.

## 2. Why a fresher should care — three hook candidates

**(a) The puzzle: "I'll stop when I'm ahead."** A fair coin, ±1 per toss, quit whenever you like. Strategy: quit the first time you are up by 1. Within 1,000 tosses you get there 97.5 % of the time, so you walk away +1 almost surely. Have you beaten a fair game? Reveal: the other 2.5 % are down 38 on average, and the overall average is exactly zero; to make the strategy *certain* you need unlimited time and unlimited credit (the Cambridge lecture's line: "If you have time and infinitely good debit card: go ahead!"). Stakes: this is the trader's "I'll close it when it comes back", and the risk desk's reason to look at the tail, not the hit rate.

**(b) The story with stakes: the croupier and the doubling system.** Bet 1; lose; bet 2; lose; bet 4 … one win recovers everything plus 1. With a bankroll of 31 you can absorb five losses; one cycle in 32 you lose the lot. Over 100 cycles 96 % of players hit that ruin at least once, the median player is +4 and the mean is 0.00. The name is literally this: Shreve's notes (p. 77) — "The term martingale was apparently first used by Ville [158], who assigned the name to a betting strategy." Stakes: every "sure thing" blow-up in markets (short volatility, "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller") has this shape — many small wins, one loss that returns them.

**(c) The everyday object: the weather forecast.** The forecast for Sunday's chance of rain, updated daily: Monday 40 %, Tuesday 55 %, Wednesday 30 %. If the forecaster is honest, today's number is the average of what tomorrow's number will be — if she could see it would rise tomorrow, she would raise it today. A sequence of honest forecasts is a martingale, and that is the shape of every price in this firm: a price is the forecast of a discounted payoff.

**Which is strongest.** (a). It is a trap the fresher walks into in ten seconds, it costs nothing to explain, and its resolution contains the three ideas the chapter owns — conditional expectation ("given what you know now"), the stopping rule, and the fine print (bounded time or bounded loss). (b) is the best *story* and supplies the chapter's name; (c) is the best bridge to pricing and should close the chapter rather than open it.

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Book chapters read

- **Shreve, SCF II §2.1 "Information and σ-algebras", pp. 49–52 (PDF 65–68).** Does well: builds the information diary by hand. Three coin tosses; told only the first, the resolved sets are A_H = {HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT} and A_T (2.1.1); F_1 = {∅, Ω, A_H, A_T}; told two tosses, the four atoms A_HH, A_HT, A_TH, A_TT and F_2 with 16 sets (2.1.3); F_3 all 256 subsets; F_0 = {∅, Ω} "if we are told nothing". "A filtration tells us the information we will have at future times" (p. 51). Example 2.1.4 fixes the running numbers: S_2(HHH) = S_2(HHT) = 16, S_2(HTH) = … = 4, S_2(TTH) = S_2(TTT) = 1, i.e. S_0 = 4, up ×2, down ×½. Does badly for a fresher: 256 subsets is terrifying; show atoms only.
- **Shreve §2.3 "General conditional expectations", pp. 66–77 (PDF 82–93).** The one-step formula a fresher can compute: E_2[S_3](HH) = pS_3(HHH) + qS_3(HHT), etc. (2.3.4–7). Definition 2.3.1: E[X|G] is (i) G-measurable and (ii) partial-averaging — "the value of the conditional expectation has been chosen to be that constant that yields the same average over the atom as the random variable S_3 being estimated" (p. 68). Theorem 2.3.2, the five properties: linearity; "taking out what is known"; iterated conditioning (tower); independence; conditional Jensen (pp. 69–70); (2.3.25) E[E[X|G]] = EX, "an unbiased estimator". Lemma 2.3.4, the Independence Lemma: hold the known variables fixed, integrate the independent ones out, then substitute back (p. 73). Definition 2.3.5 (p. 74): martingale E[M(t)|F(s)] = M(s) — "It has no tendency to rise or fall"; submartingale "no tendency to fall"; supermartingale "no tendency to rise". Definition 2.3.6 Markov. Exercise 2.2 (p. 77): the two-period tree S_0 = 4, S_1 = 8/2, S_2 = 16/4/1 under P̃ = ¼ each and P = (4/9, 2/9, 2/9, 1/9). Does well: exact, small, every number checkable. Badly: Lebesgue integrals arrive on page three.
- **Shreve §3.2.3, p. 85.** The martingale property of the random walk in four lines (3.2.5): E[M_ℓ|F_k] = E[M_ℓ − M_k|F_k] + E[M_k|F_k] = 0 + M_k. The chapter's one displayed proof.
- **Shreve §8.2 "Stopping times", pp. 340–345 (PDF 356–361).** The best small example in any book. Definition 8.2.1: τ is a stopping time if {τ ≤ t} ∈ F(t) — "the decision to stop at time t must be based on information available at time t" (p. 341). Theorem 8.2.4 (optional sampling): "A martingale stopped at a stopping time is a martingale", with the intuition quoted in full: "After being frozen, a process goes neither up nor down, path-by-path. The only way the martingale property could be violated is if the stopping decision looked ahead" (p. 342). Fig. 8.2.2: M_0 = 4, M_1(H) = 6.40, M_1(T) = 1.60, M_2(HH) = 10.24, M_2(HT) = M_2(TH) = 2.56, M_2(TT) = 0.64 under p̃ = q̃ = ½ (the discounted stock of Vol. I §4.3). Fig. 8.2.3: the *non*-stopping time ρ that stops at 0 "if there is an H on the first toss" — it peeks — ρ(HH) = ρ(HT) = 0, ρ(TH) = 1, ρ(TT) = 2; the stopped process averages ¼(4 + 4 + 1.60 + 0.64) = 2.56 < M_0 = 4 (p. 343). Also p. 339: a European option's discounted price is a martingale under the risk-neutral measure, an American's is a supermartingale.
- **Shreve §11.2.5, pp. 467–468.** N(t) − λt is a martingale (Theorem 11.2.4): a second martingale that is not a coin — the sawtooth of Fig. 11.2.2.
- **Hull, OFOD 5e §21.3 "Martingales", pp. 488–490 (PDF 503–505).** "A martingale is a zero-drift stochastic process… dθ = σ dz… A martingale has the convenient property that its expected value at any future time is equal to its value today. This means that E(θ_T) = θ_0" (p. 488). The equivalent-martingale-measure result: with numeraire g, f/g is a martingale when the market price of risk equals g's volatility, so f_0 = g_0 E_g(f_T/g_T) (21.15, p. 489); with the money-market account, f_0 = Ê(e^{−r̄T} f_T) (21.19) and f_0 = e^{−rT}Ê(f_T) for constant r (p. 490). Does well: states the pricing consequence in two lines. Badly: "zero drift" hides the conditioning and assumes Itô; an unconditional E(θ_T) = θ_0 is weaker than the definition.
- **Wilmott on QF §4.2–4.4, 4.7, pp. 71–73, 76 (PDF 92–94, 97).** The coin game; E[R_i] = 0, E[R_i²] = 1; E[S_6|R_1, …, R_5] = S_5 named "the conditional expectation" (p. 72); §4.4: "You know how much money you have won after the fifth toss. Your expected winnings after the sixth toss, and indeed after any number of tosses if we keep playing, is just the amount you already hold… This is called the martingale property" (p. 73); and the warning that Markov ≠ martingale. §4.7 p. 76: the integrand is evaluated at the left point, "non-anticipatory… we take some action such as choosing a portfolio and only then does the stock price move" — the filtration in trading words. Does well: no symbols beyond S_i. Badly: no stopping, no betting system, no pricing.

### Best beginner explanations found on the web (opened)

1. **deeconometrist.nl, "Intuition and definition of a martingale" (2026-03-03).** €0 start, ±€1 per round; the filtration as "an archive that grows as time passes" in which "nothing disappears"; "Here lies the essence of a martingale: a process in which, given all available information, you have no predictable advantage"; "The only thing that matters is that the average change, given the past, is zero." Why it works: the archive is defined before any symbol, and the generalisation (variance may vary; only the conditional mean change must be zero) is said in one sentence.
2. **metricgate.com, "What is a martingale? Why it works: sub/super are introduced as "tilts".
3. **flip-a-coin.com, "Martingale strategy: why it isn't a guaranteed win."** The table: a losing streak of 5 / 10 / 15 / 20 has probability 1/32 / 1/1,024 / 1/32,768 / 1/1,048,576 and needs a bankroll of $31 / $1,023 / $32,767 / $1,048,575; over about 700 cycles the chance of at least one 10-loss streak is roughly 50 %; a $1–$1,000 table allows nine doublings. 4. **Wikipedia, "Optional stopping theorem."** The three sufficient conditions (bounded stopping time; finite E[τ] with bounded increments; bounded stopped process), the gambler's reading — "on average, nothing can be gained by stopping play based on the information obtainable so far" — and the counterexample: "all three of the conditions fail for a one-sided stopping time with an unrestricted state space."
5. **Cambridge CL, Probability Lecture 8 "The Optional Stopping Theorem" (Sauerwald et al., Lent 2019).** Valid stopping times ("first time the gambler wins", "first time the gambler reaches 50 or 0") versus invalid ("two steps before the gambler reaches 50"); gambler's ruin from K with target N: P(reach N) = K/N and E[T] = K(N − K), so K = 1, N = 10 gives E[T] = 9; the "good" strategy T_good = min{i : S_i = K + 1} has E[T_good] = ∞. Why it works: the invalid-stopping-time list is the fastest way to teach "no peeking".
6. **randomservices.org/random/martingales/Stop.html (Siegrist).** "A stopping time is a random time that does not require that we see into the future"; "If the games are fair or unfavorable, then no stopping time, regardless of how cleverly designed, can help the gambler"; the target strategy τ_c gives E(X_{τ_c}) = c ≠ 0 only because E(τ_c) = ∞ — "unsustainable"; the two-sided version P(τ_{−a} < τ_b) = b/(a + b) "is a workable strategy, but alas has expected value 0."
7. **towardsdatascience, "Filtrations in reinforcement learning."** A filtration as "a sequence of filters" over a three-day up/down lattice; "In essence, it tells us whether an event happened or not"; the rule "do not utilize information that has not yet been revealed". 8. **quantnet thread "martingale & filtration."** "The simplest way to think of a filtration is just as the accumulation of information"; survivorship bias — a backtest that uses today's S&P 500 members "is using future information" — as a filtration violation. Also records the live misconception "what's the difference from a sequence of iid?".
9. **quantnet thread "risk-neutral pricing / martingale pricing."** "By *definition* of a martingale the future expectation is equal to the current value of the discounted process", hence price = expected discounted payoff; the numeraire can be another asset (Margrabe). Why it matters: it is the B5 preview in one sentence.

### Common misconceptions

- **Martingale = Markov, or = independent increments.** Neither (Wilmott p. 73; quantnet). The compensated Poisson process and a Doob forecast are martingales with nothing i.i.d. about them.
- **"A martingale is flat/boring."** Its mean stays put; its spread grows like √t (metricgate; B1).
- **"Prices are martingales."** Under the real-world measure a stock drifts (a submartingale); it is the *discounted* price under the *risk-neutral* measure that is a martingale (Hull p. 488; Shreve p. 339). B5 owns the measure; B2 must not overclaim.
- **"Stop when ahead beats a fair game."** It needs infinite expected time and unbounded losses (Cambridge; randomservices).
- **"Doubling guarantees +1."** Only with an infinite bankroll and no table limit; otherwise "a delayed loss" (flip-a-coin).
- **"Conditional expectation is a number."** It is a random variable — the forecast changes as information arrives (Shreve Def. 2.3.1 (i)).
- **"The filtration is the data we looked at."** It is what is *knowable* by then; survivorship bias and look-ahead in backtests are filtration violations (quantnet).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three different modes

### Concept A — "The croupier's table" (comic strip with a drawn croupier, last panel live)

Mode: comic strip; the final panel is a small physical simulation. Beats:

1. A croupier at a red/black table (fair, no zero). Our character: "I've got a system." Bet 1, lose. "Double." Bet 2, lose. Bet 4, win: ledger −1 −2 +4 = +1. Croupier: "Congratulations. One chip."
2. Panel of the ledger over twenty cycles: +1, +1, +1 … a staircase up. Thought bubble: "it can't lose".
3. Panel: a run of five reds. Bets 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 — all lost; the next bet, 32, is above the table limit. Ledger −31. Croupier, deadpan: "Table limit, sir." The thirty-one +1s are gone.
4. Panel: the probability sheet — a streak of 5 is 1 in 32; of 10, 1 in 1,024, needing 1,023 chips to survive. "The more you can afford to lose, the rarer the night you lose it — and the bigger."
5. Live panel: *Play 100 cycles, 10,000 players*. A histogram of final chips: a tall spike at +4 (the median) and a long left tail; a readout: mean 0.00, 62 % finished ahead, 96 % met the table limit at least once. The meter labelled *average profit* is pinned at zero and does not move while the histogram builds.
6. Panel: Ville, 1939, names the fair-game process after this system (Shreve p. 77). "The maths is named after the thing that doesn't work."

What it makes visible: the shape of a sure thing — many small wins paid for by one loss that returns them — and that the average is zero *before* any symbol. Cost: low — six drawn panels, one histogram with a seed. Risk: the fresher takes "62 % finish ahead" as a recommendation; the mean readout and the tail must be louder than the spike.

### Concept B — "Beat the fair game" (playable; every strategy's average profit tracked live)

Mode: game / explorable with a break-the-rules button (B29 + B82 + B56). Beats:

1. A fair coin, ±1 per toss, 200 tosses, bankroll 50. Two panels: *your path* (the wealth odometer, the running sum from 0.2), and beside it a meter *average profit over 10,000 replays of this strategy* that starts at 0.00.
2. Strategy buttons: *quit when up by 1*; *quit when up by 5 or down by 20*; *double after a loss, cap 16*; *bet more after wins*; *custom* (the reader decides bet size and whether to quit, toss by toss). Pick one, press *play me*: your path draws with the prime-race reveal, slowly for ten tosses, then squished.
3. Press *replay 10,000*: a faint fan of paths, the terminal histogram, and the meter — it settles at 0.00 for every button. "Quit when up by 1": the histogram is a spike at +1 (92 % within 200 tosses) and a thin tail reaching −40; mean 0.00. "Up 5 or down 20": 80 % of players at +5, 20 % at −20; mean 0.00. The doubling button reproduces Concept A's histogram.
4. A sentence appears after the third strategy: "You may choose how much to bet and when to leave, using anything you have seen. The average does not move. This is what 'martingale' means."
5. The *cheat* button, labelled honestly: *peek at the next toss*. The meter moves to +1.00 per peeked toss; a second cheat, *stop two tosses before a losing run*, moves it too. The caption uses Shreve's words: the only way to move it is "if the stopping decision looked ahead". A footnote shows the two-period version with numbers: stop at 0 only if the first toss will be H → average 2.56 instead of 4 (Shreve Fig. 8.2.3).
6. The fine print, as two more buttons: *unlimited credit, unlimited time* (the quit-when-up-by-1 histogram loses its tail and the meter reads +1 — but the mean time to get there reads ∞, the counter never stops) and *a house edge of 2.7 %* (every strategy's meter drifts negative; the doubling one fastest).
7. Close with the payoff: replace "your wealth" by "the discounted price of a stock in a two-period tree" (4 → 6.40 / 1.60 → 10.24 / 2.56 / 0.64, Shreve Fig. 8.2.2); the meter at the root reads 4 whatever node-splitting you do; "a price is the average of what it will be — B5 explains which average".

What it makes visible that text cannot: that *every* information-based strategy has the same average, felt by the reader's own hand, and that peeking is the one thing that moves the meter. Cost: medium — a 200-step coin engine with five strategy rules and one custom mode, a seeded replay fan, and the meter; no new maths beyond sums. Risk: the reader cheats first and misses the point — the cheat button should unlock after two honest strategies; and the fan plus histogram plus meter is three instruments — the meter must be the biggest.

### Concept C — "The information diary" (drawn step-through for filtrations and conditional expectation)

Mode: drawn step-through / illustrated text, with the split-while-conserving move (B82) as its one animation. Beats:

1. Three coin tosses drawn as Shreve's tree, S_0 = 4, up ×2, down ×½. A diary with four pages: *before any toss*, *after one*, *after two*, *after three*. Page 0 says "I know nothing: all eight paths are possible." Page 1, after H: "four paths remain — HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT." Page 2, after HT: "two." Page 3: "one." The diary never loses a page — the archive only grows.
2. On each page, a forecast of S_3 in that page's handwriting: page 0 writes one number, page 1 two (one per atom), page 2 four, page 3 eight — the forecast is a quantity *that depends on which page you are on*. (Shreve 2.3.4–7 with p = q = ½: page 2 writes 20, 5, 5, 1.25; page 1 writes 12.5 and 3.125; page 0 writes 7.8125.)
3. The tower rule as a sentence: "Monday's forecast of Tuesday's forecast is Monday's forecast" — the grid averaged by rows then columns (shadows `ShowGridSum`), same number either way.
4. Discount the tree (divide by 1.25 per period): 4 → 6.40 / 1.60 → 10.24 / 2.56 / 0.64. Now each node *is* the average of its children: (6.40 + 1.60)/2 = 4, (10.24 + 2.56)/2 = 6.40. The meter at the root reads 4; press *split a node* (basel `split_light_source`) and watch it not move.
5. "What you may use": the trading rule must be written on today's page (Wilmott p. 76: choose the portfolio, *then* the price moves). A backtest that uses tomorrow's page is survivorship bias.
6. A footnote names σ-algebra and filtration once, with the atoms of F_2 drawn as four boxes, and says the 16-set version exists and is not needed.

What it makes visible: that conditional expectation is a *per-page* number and that a filtration is a diary, with no set notation. Cost: low — static drawings plus one split animation. Risk: the diary is cute but the fresher may not see why it matters; beat 5 (the backtest) is the reason and must not be cut.

## 5. My recommendation

**A sequence of two: Concept A as the hook (four panels, not six) and Concept B as the spine, with Concept C folded into B's closing beats as illustrated text.** Judged by the brief: *easy first* — the comic needs no symbols and the playable needs one coin; *interesting second* — the reader personally fails to beat a fair game, then finds the one button that does and sees it is cheating; *interactive third* — interaction is spent where it teaches (choosing a strategy, pressing cheat), not on sliders.

Why invent this rather than adapt: the catalogue is right that no benchmark treats martingales visually, because a martingale is a statement about conditional expectation, not a mechanism. The invention that makes it a mechanism is the **meter that will not move** — a live average over replays, pinned at zero across every strategy — paired with the single cheat that moves it. That turns Doob's theorem into something you do with your hands, and it is the same instrument B5 will reuse ("the measure changes, the price meter does not"). The diary (C) is the right carrier for filtrations precisely because filtrations are not interactive; it should be text with one animation, as the catalogue's provisional lean said.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, and reference numbers

**Definitions.** Filtration: σ-algebras F(t), with F(s) ⊂ F(t) for s ≤ t (Shreve Def. 2.1.1, p. 51); adapted process: X(t) is F(t)-measurable (Def. 2.1.6, p. 53). Conditional expectation E[X|G]: G-measurable and ∫_A E[X|G] dP = ∫_A X dP for all A ∈ G (Def. 2.3.1, p. 68); properties: linearity; E[XY|G] = X E[Y|G] for X G-measurable; E[E[X|G]|H] = E[X|H] for H ⊂ G; E[X|G] = EX if X independent of G; Jensen (Thm 2.3.2, pp. 69–70); E[E[X|G]] = EX (2.3.25). Martingale: E[M(t)|F(s)] = M(s), 0 ≤ s ≤ t; sub (≥), super (≤) (Def. 2.3.5, p. 74). Stopping time: {τ ≤ t} ∈ F(t) (Def. 8.2.1, p. 341). Optional sampling: a martingale stopped at a stopping time is a martingale (Thm 8.2.4, p. 342); E[M_τ] = M_0 when τ is bounded, or E[τ] < ∞ with bounded increments, or the stopped process is bounded (Wikipedia; Cambridge Lecture 8). Discrete conditional expectation in the coin model: E_n[X](ω_1…ω_n) = Σ p^{#H} q^{#T} X(ω_1…ω_n ω_{n+1}…ω_N) (Shreve 2.3.1, p. 66). Random walk martingale: Shreve (3.2.5), p. 85. Compensated Poisson N(t) − λt (Thm 11.2.4, p. 467). Hull: E(θ_T) = θ_0 for a zero-drift process (p. 488); f_0 = e^{−rT} Ê(f_T) with constant r (p. 490).
