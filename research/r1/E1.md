Prerequisites assumed: 0.2 (the bell and its percentiles), A1 (the real index has fat tails — Figure 1 of D2 counts them), A2 (correlation is a shear; the corner where everything falls together), A5 (volatility clusters, EWMA), C4 (delta, gamma, theta), D1 (Raj's hedged book). ## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

A VaR number is not a forecast and not a worst case — it is a line drawn through a pile of past days ("on 99 days in 100 you lose less than this"), and the only honest way to know whether the line was drawn in the right place is to count how often the next year crossed it.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the story with stakes: "25-standard-deviation moves, several days in a row."** In August 2007 Goldman Sachs' CFO David Viniar explained a quarter lost in a week by his quant funds with the sentence “We were seeing things that were 25 standard deviation moves, several days in a row”. The sentence has been quoted ever since as the textbook failure of a model, because under the bell “a seven standard deviation event is only supposed to occur once every 3.1 billion years, according to the 2008 research paper "How Unlucky is 25-Sigma?”, and a 25-sigma day is roughly “comparable to the probability of winning the lottery 21 or 22 times in a row”. The fresher does not need to know what a quant fund is to feel the point: *the model said this could not happen; it happened on Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday.* The chapter's job is to show where that sentence goes wrong — not in the arithmetic (2.33 standard deviations is 1 %), but in the choice of distribution, which is exactly the choice between the three methods in the title. **Strongest opener**: a real quote, a real loss, no symbol.

**Hook B — the puzzle: the same ₹1 crore, three different answers.** Put ₹1 crore in NIFTY on 3 Nov 2026 and ask three desks "how much can I lose tomorrow, 99 days in 100?" The desk that reads the past ten years says ₹2.65 lakh; the desk that fits a bell to the same days says ₹2.36 lakh; the desk that asks "and when it is worse than that, how much?" says ₹4.33 lakh. Same data, same position, same question — and the answers differ by 80 %. Which one is right? (Our numbers, §6: the honest answer is *the one whose line the next 2,183 days crossed about 22 times*, and that turns out to be the first.) Good as the mid-chapter turn because it is the real index the reader has already met in A1/D2, and the resolution is a count, not an argument.

**Hook C — the everyday object: the flood wall.** A river town does not build its wall for the worst flood imaginable; it builds it for the hundred-year flood — the level exceeded, on average, once a century — and accepts that the hundred-and-first year is on the town. VaR is that wall for a book of positions; Expected Shortfall is the question the insurer asks ("and when the water does come over, how deep is it?"); backtesting is the town looking at the high-water marks on the wall and counting. Easy, true, and it carries the two ideas freshers get wrong (VaR is a threshold not a maximum; the exceedances are the point, not a failure). Best as the organising image, used once in the standfirst and once at the close.

**Recommendation: A opens, C frames, B is the chapter's own data.**

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

- **AnalystPrep, "Backtesting VaR"** (FRM Part II notes, analystprep.com/study-notes/frm/part-2/market-risk-measurement-and-management/backtesting-var/): the cleanest plain statement — “Backtesting is the process of comparing losses predicted by a value at risk (VaR) model to those actually experienced over the testing period”; the expected-count arithmetic (“a 99.0% daily VaR should be exceeded by about 10 days over a four-year period; 1% * 4 * 252 days = 10.08 days”) and the z-test for the count, “z=(x-pT)/√(p(1-p)T)”. Works because it turns "is the model right?" into a counting problem a fresher can do by hand.
- **Ryan O'Connell, CFA, FRM — "Value at Risk (VaR): Formula, Methods, and Examples"** (ryanoconnellfinance.com): the single sentence freshers most need — “95% VaR does not mean the maximum possible loss. It means there is a 5% chance — roughly 1 trading day per month — of losing more than the VaR amount. The actual loss on those days could be significantly larger.” Plus the worked $1 m example (“σdaily = 15% / √252 = 15% / 15.87 = 0.945%”, “VaR95 = $1,000,000 × 1.645 × 0.00945 = $15,545”) and the Basel traffic light (“Green 0–4 Model is performing as expected” exceptions per 250 days). Works because every number is small and checkable.
- **AnalystPrep, "Estimating Market Risk Measures"**: the ordered-observation rule for historical VaR — “The 99% VaR would be given by the (1 – 0.99) 300 + 1 highest observation, i.e., the 4th highest value” — and the one-line motive for ES: “Despite the significant role VaR plays in risk management, it stops short of telling us the amount or magnitude of the actual loss.”
- **Motley Fool / Felix Salmon / Business Standard on the Viniar quote** (fool.com 2007-08-15; felixsalmon.com "One Question for David Viniar"; business-standard.com "Fat-tailed statements", 2015): the hook's sources. Salmon's version of the objection: “the universe isn't old enough for even one sixteen-standard-deviation event to have ever happened”. - **Bettersolutions, "What is Value At Risk?"** (bettersolutions.com/finance/risk/value-at-risk.htm): the regulatory recipe in one list — “Confidence level 99%, 10-day holding period (can be scaled to 1 day using the sqrt(10) rule), 250 days minimum historic period, Back testing of VaR model conducted quarterly based on a sample of 250 trading days” — and the plain limitation, “When extreme errors occur VAR estimates tend to underestimate the true value at risk”.

### Common misconceptions

1. **"VaR is the most I can lose."** It is the *least* you lose on the bad days (Tsay's regulator's definition: "the minimal loss under extraordinary market circumstances"). Fixed by Figure 0's reveal and by ES.
2. **"99 % VaR means it will not happen."** It means 2–3 days a year. Our record: 25 crossings in 2,183 days — and seven of them in March 2020.
3. **"The √10 rule is a law."** Hull: exactly true only for i.i.d. normal changes; our 10-day historical VaR is ₹7.41 lakh against √10 × ₹2.65 lakh = ₹8.39 lakh (overlapping windows, mean-reverting-ish), while √10 × the parametric figure happens to land at ₹7.47 lakh.
4. **"A delta-neutral book has no VaR."** Raj's hedged book has a linear VaR of ₹144 and a true one-day 99 % VaR of about ₹6.3 lakh (§6). Hull's Problem 16.22 in one number.
5. **"VaR adds up."** Two desks' VaRs can sum to less than the firm's (sub-additivity fails for VaR; holds for ES). Wilmott §19.9; a two-bet example is in §6.
6. **"Historical simulation has no assumptions."** It assumes the last N days are the next day's menu, with equal weights — March 2020 is on the menu with the same weight as a sleepy Tuesday until it falls off the window's edge 500 days later (and then VaR drops overnight by a third: a "ghost" effect worth one still).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "Sort the days" (scrubbable animation on real data, with one bet)

The whole chapter can be seen once in one picture: the 2,683 real NIFTY days.

1. A strip of 2,683 thin bricks in date order, each coloured by that day's P&L on ₹1 crore (red left, green right); the 2020 crash is a visible red scar, 2024–26 a lighter one.
2. The bet: "99 days in 100 you lose less than — ₹1 lakh / ₹2.5 lakh / ₹5 lakh / ₹10 lakh?" (anchored on the worst day, which the strip shows is ₹13 lakh).
3. Scrub: the bricks slide to their P&L columns (the 3b1b brick-row move) and become the pile. A line walks in from the left and stops at the 27th brick: **₹2.65 lakh**. Reveal.
4. Hover a brick in the tail: it is a date ("23 Mar 2020, −₹12.98 lakh"; "4 Jun 2024, election count day, −₹5.9 lakh"). The tail is made of days the reader may remember.
5. Second line: the average of the 27 bricks beyond the first line — **₹4.33 lakh**, Expected Shortfall: "when it is worse, this is how much worse, on average".
6. Lay the bell over the pile (parametric desk): its 1 % line sits at ₹2.36 lakh, a few bricks to the right of the real one; its tail runs out where the real pile still has eleven bricks.

Makes visible: VaR is a *rank*, not a formula; ES is the mass beyond it; the bell's line is in a different place for a reason the reader can see. Cost: low (we have the data, the Plot primitives, Bet, and the brick move is a few hundred lines). Risk: 2,683 bricks is a lot of DOM — draw as one canvas or bucket into 400 columns; and the strip must be labelled "₹1 crore long NIFTY, 2016–2026 (real)".

### Concept 2 — "Did the line hold?" — the backtest strip (annotated real artefact + two toggles)

The idea freshers never see in a textbook: the number is only as good as its track record.

1. A ten-year strip: each day's loss as a dot, and the *VaR line of that day* — computed from the previous 500 days only — drawn as a moving fence. Days above the fence light red.
2. Counter: **25 crossings in 2,183 days; expected 22**. A verdict card: "passes" (Kupiec's test LR 0.44 against 3.84 — said as "the count is what a right model would give on most runs").
3. Toggle: **bell with a 500-day σ** — the fence is smoother, lower in calm years, and the counter jumps to **41** (nearly twice the expected). Toggle: **EWMA 0.94 bell (RiskMetrics)** — the fence hugs the recent days, and the count is **43**, but the red days are *less clustered* (the fence rose during March 2020 while the historical one sat still until a 500-day window swallowed the crash).
4. Zoom into March 2020: the historical fence on 20 Mar 2020 read ₹3.71 lakh; 23 Mar lost ₹12.98 lakh — 3.5 times the line. Seven red days in one month against an expected 0.2: *the crossings cluster*, which is A5's clustering turned into a risk-management sentence.
5. The Basel traffic light as a caption: 0–4 green, 5–9 yellow, 10+ red crossings per 250 days.

Makes visible: the difference between the three methods is not philosophy, it is a count; a model that passes on average can still fail in a bunch. Cost: medium (rolling computation in the engine — fine; the strip is a Plot). Risk: the EWMA result needs a careful caption (it fails the count because the bell is wrong at 1 %, not because EWMA is wrong — Student-t EWMA passes; one line in the close).

### Concept 3 — Raj's book: the ₹144 VaR (break-it, one dial, step-through)

The chapter's bridge from D1/C4 to risk management, and Hull's "poor results for low-delta portfolios" made real.

1. Raj's book from D1 on 23 Oct: short 6,500 of the 22,600 call, long 3,292 futures; net delta 0.27. The linear VaR formula: 2.33 × |Δ| × S × σ_day = **₹144**. A result card: "the model says Raj can lose ₹144 tomorrow at 99 %".
2. Step: full revaluation — move the index by every one of the 2,683 real days (historical simulation on the option book, each day repriced with Black-76 one day older) and sort: **₹6.3 lakh**. The gap is the gamma (and the clock pays back ₹75 k of it).
3. Step: the quadratic model δP = Δ·δS + ½Γ·δS² (Hull 16.6) with a normal δS: ₹7.3 lakh — close, and it needs no repricing. Cornish–Fisher on the same book (skew −2.9) gives a nonsense ₹1.4 lakh — said plainly: the expansion is for mild skew (Hull's example has −0.4).
4. One dial: **the hedge** (0 → 6,500 futures). At zero futures the linear VaR is ₹8.6 lakh and nearly right; at delta-neutral it is ₹144 and absurd. The dial shows where the linear model is safe and where it lies: it lies exactly where a hedger lives.

Makes visible: VaR for options is a revaluation problem; delta alone is a trap for the hedged. Cost: low-medium (bs.ts exists; one-day reval is a loop). Risk: a reader who skipped C4/D1 — one restating line each ("the slope and the bend from C4; Raj's book from D1").

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

- **Definitions.** VaR_p over horizon h: the loss L such that P(loss > L) = p (p = 1 % or 5 %); as a quantile of the P&L distribution (Tsay 7.1). ES_p = E[loss | loss > VaR_p] (Hull's C-VaR, p. 347–348). Normal case: VaR = z_p σ (mean zero), ES = σ φ(z_p)/p (z_0.01 = 2.326, φ/p = 2.665). Historical: sort n P&Ls, VaR = the ⌈pn⌉-th worst (n = 2,683 → the 27th; AnalystPrep's "(1−c)·n + 1" convention gives the 28th — pick one and say it). √h scaling exact only for i.i.d. normal (Hull p. 348).
- **Real record, ₹1 crore long NIFTY, 2,683 daily returns 2016-01-04 → 2026-11-03 (nifty50-long.json), P&L = 1e7 × (e^r − 1).** Historical 1-day 99 % VaR ₹265,327 (27th worst); 95 % ₹147,472; ES 99 % ₹432,791; ES 95 % ₹234,215. Daily σ of P&L ₹101,535 → parametric 99 % ₹236,172, 95 % ₹167,026, parametric ES 99 % ₹270,833. Worst five days: −₹1,298,050 (23 Mar 2020), −₹830,194, −₹761,208, −₹592,934, −₹574,450. 10-day overlapping historical 99 % VaR ₹741,064 vs √10 × 1-day historical ₹839,036 and √10 × parametric ₹746,840.
- **Backtest, rolling 500-day window, T = 2,183 test days, p = 1 %, expected 21.8.** Historical: 25 exceptions (binomial P(≥25) = 0.27; Kupiec LR = 0.44 < 3.84 → not rejected). Parametric 500-day σ: 41. EWMA λ = 0.94 normal: 43. Exception dates (historical): 2 Feb 2018; 4, 5 Oct 2018; 6, 10 Dec 2018; 8 Jul 2019; 1, 28 Feb 2020; 6, 9, 12, 16, 18, 23 Mar 2020; 4 May 2020; 4 Jun 2024; 5 Aug 2024; 3 Oct 2024; 6 Jan 2025; 28 Feb 2025; 7 Apr 2025; 1 Feb 2026; 13, 19, 23 Mar 2026. VaR on the close of 20 Mar 2020: ₹370,964; loss on 23 Mar: ₹1,298,050 (3.50×). Kupiec: LR = −2 ln[(1−p)^{T−x} p^x] + 2 ln[(1−x/T)^{T−x} (x/T)^x] ~ χ²₁. Basel zones per 250 days: green 0–4, yellow 5–9, red ≥ 10.
- **Raj's book (D1 conventions: F 22,603, K 22,600, r 6.5 %, T 11/365, σ 15.853 %; short 6,500 calls, long 3,292 futures; realised daily σ 1.02 %).** Call 249.15, Δ 0.5064, Γ 0.000640; book delta +0.268. Linear 99 % VaR 2.326 × 0.268 × 22,603 × 0.0102 = **₹144**. Full revaluation one day on (T − 1/365, normal δx at 1.02 %, 50,000 draws): 99 % VaR ≈ **₹633,000**, 95 % ≈ ₹345,000, best case +₹74,600 (= one day's theta, 74,633). Delta-gamma without theta: 99 % VaR ≈ ₹735,000. Cornish–Fisher on the delta-gamma P&L: μ −110,293, σ 157,198, skew −2.89 → w = −2.326 − (2.326² − 1)/6 × (−2.89) = −0.20 → "VaR" ₹142,000 — wrong, because the expansion assumes small skew; keep Hull's Example 16.2 (skew −0.4: 5.326 → 5.976) as the test and this as the warning. Exact Wilmott bound for a short-gamma book: δV ≤ Δ²/(2|Γ|) on the upside (= 0.268²/(2 × 6,500 × 0.00064) ≈ ₹8.6 — the hedged book cannot gain from a move, only from the clock).
- **Hull Ch. 16 tests.** $10 m Microsoft at 2 %/day: 1-day 99 % VaR $466,000 (2.33 × 200,000), 10-day $1,473,621; AT&T $5 m at 1 %: $116,500 / $368,405; combined ρ = 0.3: σ = √(200,000² + 50,000² + 2 × 0.3 × 200,000 × 50,000) = 220,227, VaR $513,129 / $1,622,657; diversification benefit $219,369 (p. 351–352). Example 16.1 (options, deltas 1,000 and 20,000, prices 120 and 30): σ = 7.099 (thousands), 5-day 95 % VaR 1.65 × √5 × 7,099 = $26,193 (p. 355). Example 16.2 Cornish–Fisher: −5.326 → −5.976 (p. 371). Bond mapping, Appendix 16A: 0.8-year bond → $37,397 / $331,382 / $678,074 in 3 m / 6 m / 1 y, σ = 1,621.3, 10-day 99 % VaR $11,946 (p. 369).
- **Wilmott tests.** Table 19.1 quantiles (2.326342, 2.053748, 1.88079, 1.750686, 1.644853, 1.281551). Tsay Example 7.2: σ̂² = 0.000336 → 5 % quantile −0.03025 → $302,500; 1 % −0.04265 → $426,500 on $10 m (p. 292); §7.6.1's five-method table as a tolerance check on our empirical-quantile code against his $216,030 / $365,709 only in method, not value (different data).
- **Sub-additivity counter-example for the still.** Two independent bets, each losing ₹100 with probability 4 %: each has 95 % VaR = 0; the pair loses ≥ ₹100 with probability 7.84 % → 95 % VaR = ₹100 > 0 + 0. ES is sub-additive (Artzner et al. 1999, via Hull fn. 3 and Wilmott 19.9).
- **Tests to write.** Historical VaR/ES against numpy's sorted array on the record (exact); normal VaR/ES against scipy; backtest counts 25/41/43 and the exception-date list; Kupiec LR; Hull's six figures above to the printed rupee/dollar; Raj's full-reval VaR within 2 % of the key (seeded draws or quantiles of the normal — prefer the 2,683 real days as the scenario set so the test is exact); delta-gamma identity on a grid; the sub-additivity example exactly.
