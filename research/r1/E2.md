_Phase R1 deep research note (2026-11-10). Options for discussion with the sponsor, not decisions. Prerequisites assumed: 0.2 (the bell), A2 (correlation as a shear — E2 uses it to build correlated paths), B3 (the cloud of paths and √t), B5 (price = average payoff under the weight on futures), C3 (the cloud of futures at expiry is already a Monte Carlo pricer in disguise), D3 (hestonPath / mertonSmile already run on paths). ## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

Throwing random darts is a perfectly respectable way to compute a number, as long as you also compute how wrong you probably are — and the price of one more decimal place is always a hundred times more darts, unless you are clever about which darts you throw.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: the fresher's own cloud was already a pricer.** In C3 Figure 1 the reader grew a cloud of 2,000 index levels for 3 Nov and read 249 off it as the average of max(S − 22,600, 0). Nobody called it Monte Carlo. Open E2 with that cloud and one question: "that 249 — how sure are you?" Regrow the cloud with a different seed: 256. Again: 241. The number wobbles by about ±8 (2,000 random dots; C3's dots were quantiles, which is why its 249 never moved — the chapter says so), and the chapter is about three things: why it wobbles (the standard error), how fast the wobble shrinks (√n — four times the dots for half the wobble), and the tricks that shrink it without more dots (correct each dot by where the index itself landed and the band halves; for an option on the *average* the same idea collapses it a hundredfold). Strongest because the artefact is the reader's own and the first beat costs nothing new.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: Ulam's solitaire.** Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from an illness in 1946, played solitaire and wondered what fraction of deals come out; the combinatorics was hopeless, so he thought: deal a hundred hands and count. He told von Neumann; the name came from the casino where Ulam's uncle borrowed money. (Hull: *"The term quasi-random is a misnomer. A quasi-random sequence is totally deterministic"* is the chapter's footnote-joke descendant.) A good two-sentence opener but the fresher cannot *do* anything with it; demoted to the standfirst.

**Hook C — the everyday object: darts and the circle.** Throw darts at a square with a circle inside; the fraction inside is π/4. Every beginner source on the web uses it (below), and it carries the one law that matters — though one popular tutorial states it as “each tenfold increase in darts buys roughly one more correct decimal place at the cost of ten times the work”, which is off by a square root (tenfold buys half a decimal; Glasserman: *"adding one decimal place of precision requires 100 times as many points"*, p. 2). That slip is itself teachable: the chapter can show the log-log slope of −½ and let the reader see that a hundredfold is one decimal. Use the dartboard as Figure 0's warm-up only if the sponsor wants a non-finance opening; otherwise Hook A's cloud does the same job on the real chain.

**Recommendation: A opens (the reader's own cloud wobbles), C is a 20-second aside inside it, B is one line in the standfirst.**

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Book chapters read

- **Glasserman, *Monte Carlo Methods in Financial Engineering*, Ch. 1 §1.1 p. 1–3 (text pp. 312–400).** The best three pages written on the subject, and quotable whole: *"Monte Carlo methods are based on the analogy between probability and volume… Monte Carlo uses this identity in reverse, calculating the volume of a set by interpreting the volume as a probability"* (p. 1). The error law in plain words: *"Cutting this error in half requires increasing the number of points by a factor of four; adding one decimal place of precision requires 100 times as many points"* (p. 2). The reason it survives: *"the O(n^{-1/2}) convergence rate holds for all d. In contrast, the error in a product trapezoidal rule in d dimensions is O(n^{-2/d})… Thus, Monte Carlo methods are attractive in evaluating integrals in high dimensions"* (p. 3) — and *"Monte Carlo is generally not a competitive method for calculating one-dimensional integrals"* (p. 2), which the chapter should say out loud so the fresher does not think it is magic. The sample standard deviation s_f as the free error bar (p. 2).
- **Glasserman Ch. 4 §4.1 control variates p. 185–189, §4.2 antithetic variates p. 205–207.** Control variate defined in one line: *"the observed error X̄ − E[X] serves as a control in estimating E[Y]"* (p. 186), unbiased for any b, optimal b = Cov/Var; Table 4.1 (p. 187): the correlation between S(T) and the call payoff for S₀ = 50, σ = 30 %, r = 5 %, T = 0.25 at K = 40 … 70 is 0.995, 0.968, 0.895, 0.768, 0.604, 0.433, 0.286, so the fraction of variance removed by using the stock as the control is 0.99, 0.94, 0.80, 0.59, 0.36, 0.19, 0.08 — *"the effectiveness of a control variate can vary widely with the parameters of a problem"*. Example 4.1.2 Kemna–Vorst: the geometric-average Asian (closed form) as the control for the arithmetic-average Asian — the single most effective trick in the book and a natural E2 figure. Antithetics: *"if U is uniformly distributed over [0,1], then 1 − U is too… a large value of one is accompanied by a small value of the other"*; in normal terms, Z and −Z, *"the reflection of the path about the origin"* (p. 205); works on the antisymmetric part of the payoff, so a call (mostly monotone) gains and a symmetric straddle does not — on our chain the call's pair-variance ratio is 1.8 and the straddle's 0.5, i.e. worse than independent draws (§6) — a figure in itself.
- **Hull OFOD 5e §18.6–18.7 p. 410–417 (text pp. 21348–21610).** The five-step recipe (sample a path in a risk-neutral world, payoff, repeat, average, discount), the exact log-step S(t+δt) = S(t) exp((μ̂ − σ²/2)δt + σε√δt) vs the Euler step that is *"true only in the limit"* (18.14/18.15), the jump-to-T shortcut for European payoffs, the twelve-uniforms normal (18.17) and Cholesky for correlated ε, the standard error ω/√M and *"To double the accuracy of a simulation, we must quadruple the number of trials"* (p. 413), Greeks by common random numbers (*"the random number streams… should be the same for calculating both f and f*"*, p. 414), §18.7's tour — antithetic, control variate f_A = f̂_A − f̂_B + f_B (18.18), importance sampling for the deep-OTM call, stratified sampling with N⁻¹((i − 0.5)/n), moment matching, quasi-random with the Sobol' picture (Figure 18.14: *"successive points do tend to fill in the gaps left by previous points"*) and the 1/M claim. Hull is the catalogue; Glasserman is the reason.
- **Wilmott Ch. 80 "Monte Carlo Simulation" p. 1263 ff. (text pp. 62337–62530), §80.2–80.5 reopened.** The spreadsheet pictures (Figure 80.1 a call and a put from one column of paths; Figure 80.2 an Asian option from the running average) are the fresher's version — a column of random numbers, a column of prices, one cell with the average. §80.5's list of advantages, and the sentence worth keeping: Monte Carlo *"is in a sense the most straightforward"* of the numerical methods because you simulate what the contract does. Wilmott Ch. 19's bootstrapping (E1) is Monte Carlo with history as the random-number generator.
- **Shreve SCF II**: not reopened (no numerical chapter). **Gatheral**: Ch. 2's Milstein step (2.18) and the negative-variance problem for Heston paths — already in D3's note; E2 reuses hestonPath as the "path with its own randomness" example without re-deriving it. **Tsay**: nothing on Monte Carlo pricing.

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

- **Loyola intro CS, "Case Study: Monte Carlo Simulation"** (introcs-python.cs.luc.edu/case_studies/monte_carlo.html): the π-by-darts estimate with a printed convergence table — “Each tenfold increase in darts roughly halves the error — the price of one more decimal place is 100× more work” and the exercise “Does the slope of the error curve match the expected –½ on a log-log scale?” — which is exactly Figure 1's log-log panel. - **Codesignal, "Estimating pi with darts"**: the honest small-n failure — “Suppose we throw 20 darts at the unit square and 16 of them land inside the quarter-circle. - **Bohrium / Feynman-style computational physics notes, "Monte Carlo integration fundamentals"**: the point most beginner pages miss — “it's better to average the function's value than to play a simple game of 'in or out'” (the sample-mean estimator beats hit-or-miss: the first variance-reduction trick is to not throw away information) and the dimension argument, “Whether you are integrating over a line or over a space with a million dimensions, the error shrinks in exactly the same way.”
- **Patrick Boyle, "What is the Monte Carlo method? - **The Best Interest blog, "Monte Carlo Simulation"**: the monkeys-with-darts telling — “If I had a million (or billion, or trillion) monkeys throw their darts into the square, the percentage of darts that land inside the circle would be equal to π/4”; 10 million darts → 3.141794 — “Out of 10 million darts, the monkeys hit the target about 600 times more than expected, for a percent error of 0.0006%.” Works because it shows that even ten million darts leave an error, and says how big.
- **Learnsignal, "Monte Carlo Simulation in Finance: How It Works and Where It's Used"** (Sept 2026): “Is Monte Carlo simulation the same as scenario analysis? No.” and “Monte Carlo simulation is only as good as the assumptions behind it” — the two misconceptions in one page; thin otherwise.

### Common misconceptions

1. **"More paths fix everything."** Ten thousand paths get you ±1 %; a million get ±0.1 %; the model's wrong vol gets you nothing. Glasserman's 100× rule, said with our cloud.
2. **"The average of the paths is the price."** The *discounted* average under the *risk-neutral* drift is (B5). Simulate with the real drift and you price an option at the wrong number — a dial in Figure 1 shows it (drift 0 → 12 %: the "price" climbs; the market's is at r).
3. **"Random means unrepeatable."** Seeds. The Greeks figure depends on it (common random numbers: same seed, bumped input, otherwise the delta estimate is noise). Hull p. 414.
4. **"Antithetics always halve the error."** Only for the antisymmetric part; a straddle gains nothing (Glasserman p. 207). One toggle shows a call's band shrinking and a straddle's not.
5. **"Euler steps are exact."** Hull 18.14 vs 18.15: for GBM the log step is exact at any δt; for Heston no step is, and Gatheral's Milstein + reflection is the fix (D3 already does it).
6. **"Quasi-random is random."** Hull's footnote 17. Sobol' points are a deterministic lattice that fills gaps; the error scales like 1/n (times logs) not 1/√n, and only in modest dimension.

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "How sure are you?" (explorable on the reader's own cloud, one dial, one toggle)

1. C3's cloud of 2,000 terminal index levels for 3 Nov (same F = 22,603, σ = 15.853 %, 11 days), the 22,600 call's payoff shaded; readout **average payoff → price 248 ± 8** (the ± is the new thing: s/√n; the payoff's spread is about 370 a draw).
2. Button **re-roll**: a new seed, the cloud redrawn, 241 … 256. The ± band is drawn as a bar; the true 249.15 as a tick. Most rerolls land inside the bar; about one in twenty does not. Said so.
3. Dial **paths**: 100 → 100,000 on a log scale; the band shrinks √10 per decade, a log-log panel beside it shows the −½ slope. Readout: paths, price, ±.
4. Toggle **mirror** (antithetic): every dot gets its reflection through the forward; at the same 2,000 dots the band shrinks from ±8.2 to ±6.1 (variance ratio 1.8 — the mirrored dot cancels the slope part of the payoff, the bend remains). A second payoff on the same toggle, the straddle |S − K|: its band gets *wider* (ratio 0.5) — a symmetric payoff and its mirror agree, so the pair carries less information than two independent dots. One trick, two outcomes, one sentence.
5. Toggle **control** (the stock as control variate, Glasserman Table 4.1): correct each payoff by b × (S_T − F); at K = 22,600 the correlation between the index and the payoff is 0.86, so the stock removes 74 % of the variance and the band halves (±8.2 → ±4.2); the caption gives the whole table's lesson: deeper out of the money the stock knows less about the payoff and the trick fades.

Makes visible: an estimate carries an error bar you can compute from the same draws; √n; two tricks that cost nothing. Cost: low (engine: seeded normal generator + antithetic/control toggles; the cloud component exists). Risk: three controls — keep *re-roll* as a button, not a control; drop the control-variate toggle to a caption if it crowds.

### Concept 2 — "Paths, not points" — the Asian option (step-through + annotated real artefact)

The reason Monte Carlo exists on a desk: payoffs that depend on the *road*.

1. The contract: a call struck at 22,600 on the *average* NIFTY close over the seven trading days 26 Oct → 3 Nov (an Asian option; NSE does not list one, so it is labelled a what-if on the real dates). The real road is drawn — the seven closes we have, 22,753 → 23,811, average 23,063 — and its payoff, 463.
2. Step: one simulated road — seven exact log-steps (Hull 18.15), the running average drawn as a second line, the payoff at the end.
3. Step: 2,000 roads as a faint bundle (B3's cloud grown day by day), the pile of averages narrower than the pile of endpoints — *"an average wobbles less than a price"*: the Asian is cheaper than the 3 Nov call (168.7 vs 249.15; key).
4. Step: the control variate — the *geometric* average has a closed form (Kemna–Vorst); per road, subtract b × (geometric payoff − its known price). The band collapses by a factor of about 300 in width (variance ratio ~80,000 at seven fixings over eleven days, where the arithmetic and geometric averages are almost the same number — Glasserman's "particularly effective" at its most extreme; on a one-year monthly Asian the factor is tens, not hundreds, and the caption says so). A sentence: "we priced the thing we cannot solve by correcting it with the thing we can".
5. Step: the real road's payoff, 463, against the price 169: one road is one draw, and this one landed in the top of the pile (the index rose 5.4 % in the week). Then the Euler aside in one line: "for a stock, the log step is exact; for D3's breathing wobble it is not, which is why those paths needed Milstein".

Makes visible: path dependence; why the average is cheaper; the best trick in the book, done once. Cost: medium (an Asian pricer, geometric closed form, control estimate; all small). Risk: the what-if label must be clear — the only real objects are the dates and the seven closes.

### Concept 3 — "Raj's book, a thousand tomorrows" — Monte Carlo VaR with correlated paths (game-light: guess-the-error)

E1 owned the ₹144 lesson; E2 owns the machinery — and the fresher's first real use of A2's shear.

1. Two risk factors: the index (σ 1.02 %/day) and its implied vol (the 22,600 IV's daily moves from D1's week; ρ with the index strongly negative — key). Cholesky in one picture: two independent bells sheared into one tilted cloud (A2 Figure 2 redrawn).
2. Raj's book repriced on 5,000 correlated tomorrows (index × vol), sorted: the 50th worst is the Monte Carlo 99 % VaR; beside it E1's historical number for the same book.
3. The guess: "how wide is the error bar on a 1 % quantile from 5,000 draws?" Options ±1 %, ±5 %, ±15 %. Reveal ≈ ±10 % (the quantile's standard error √(p(1−p)/n)/f(q) — said in words: "the tail is where the draws are thin"); 50 draws past the line is a small sample, however big n looks.
4. Toggle: **drop the correlation** → the VaR falls (a short-gamma, short-vega book is hurt by down-and-vol-up together); the toggle is A2's corner in rupees.

Makes visible: Monte Carlo VaR is "sample, reprice, sort"; the tail estimate is noisy; correlation matters most exactly where the losses are. Cost: medium (vol series from D1's week is only six points — the ρ would be a stand-in unless the sponsor supplies a longer IV series; see §8). Risk: the reader must hold two chapters (E1 + A2) in mind; keep it as Figure 2 with one restating line each.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

- **Estimator and error.** α̂_n = (1/n)Σ f(U_i); SE = s_f/√n; 95 % interval ±1.96 s_f/√n (Glasserman p. 2; Hull p. 413). Error ∝ n^{−1/2} in every dimension; trapezoid n^{−2} in 1-D, n^{−2/d} in d (Glasserman p. 2–3). π by darts: estimate 4 × (inside/n), SE = 4√(p(1−p)/n) with p = π/4 → ±0.0164 at n = 10,000, ±0.0016 at 10⁶ (checks the Loyola table's 0.0028 / 0.00002 run as plausible draws).
- **Paths.** Exact GBM step S exp((r − σ²/2)δt + σ√δt Z) (Hull 18.15); jump-to-T for European payoffs; Euler step bias for GBM O(δt) in the drift; correlated normals ε₂ = ρx₁ + √(1−ρ²)x₂ and Cholesky L with Σ = LLᵀ (Hull p. 412–413).
- **The chain's cloud (C3 conventions: F 22,603, K 22,600, r 6.5 %, T 11/365, σ 15.853 %).** True Black-76 price 249.15. Discounted payoff standard deviation per draw 368.8 → SE at n = 2,000 random dots ±8.2 (400,000-draw check: mean 248.3). C3's cloud is *stratified* — 2,000 quantile dots — so its "price" is exact to 0.1; E2 must say that C3's dots were already the stratified trick, and draw *random* dots for the wobble. Antithetic: call variance ratio 1.83 per pair against two independent draws (band ±8.2 → ±6.1); straddle ratio 0.50 (worse). Control variate with S_T at K = F: ρ(S_T, payoff) = 0.862, ρ² = 0.743, band ±8.2 → ±4.2; Glasserman Table 4.1's shape (ρ² falling from 0.99 deep in to 0.08 far out) reproduced on our chain as a test.
- **Glasserman Table 4.1 as a test (S₀ 50, σ 30 %, r 5 %, T 0.25):** ρ(S_T, payoff) at K = 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70 = 0.995, 0.968, 0.895, 0.768, 0.604, 0.433, 0.286 (within ±0.01 from 10⁶ draws or by numerical integration).
- **Asian what-if on the real dates (7 fixings 26 Oct → 3 Nov at the actual day offsets 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11 days; F 22,603, K 22,600, σ 15.853 %, r 6.5 %, forward-form log steps).** Arithmetic-average call by MC: 168.71 ± 0.39 at 400,000 paths; geometric-average call closed form (ln G normal with mean ln F − ½σ² t̄ and variance σ² Σᵢⱼ min(tᵢ,tⱼ)/n²): 168.20, MC 168.10; control-variate estimate 168.81 ± 0.0014, variance ratio ≈ 8 × 10⁴; antithetic ratio for the Asian 1.85. Real road: closes 22,753.20, 22,749.75, 22,698.85, 22,936.75, 23,053.10, 23,440.80, 23,810.85, average 23,063.33, payoff 463.33.
- **Hull §18.7 checks.** Stratified representative values for n = 4: N⁻¹(0.125), N⁻¹(0.375), N⁻¹(0.625), N⁻¹(0.875) = −1.150, −0.319, 0.319, 1.150; the twelve-uniform normal has variance exactly 1 and kurtosis 2.9 (a test that it is *not* normal in the tails: P(|ε| > 6) = 0 against 2e−9); moment matching leaves mean 0, sd 1 exactly.
- **Monte Carlo VaR on Raj's book.** Same book as E1 §6; 5,000 correlated (index, IV) tomorrows; 99 % VaR = 50th worst; SE of the 1 % quantile ≈ √(0.01 × 0.99/5,000)/f(q) — report as a bootstrap interval from the key (expect ±8–12 %). With ρ(index, IV) = 0 vs the fitted negative value: the VaR gap (key).
- **Tests to write.** SE formula against the sample sd of repeated runs (100 runs × 2,000 draws); the −½ slope on a log-log fit (within 0.05); antithetic variance ratios for the call (≈ 1.8, ±0.1) and the straddle (≈ 0.5, ±0.05); control-variate ratios against Table 4.1 (±0.02); Kemna–Vorst closed form against a 10⁶-path MC (within 2 SE); the Asian < the European call; Cholesky reproduces the target correlation (±0.01 at 10⁵); Monte Carlo VaR within its own bootstrap band of E1's historical figure *only if* the scenario distribution is the same — otherwise the test is "the two are within 15 % and the gap is explained".
