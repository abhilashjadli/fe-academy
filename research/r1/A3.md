# A3 Regression and its failure modes

_R1 deep-research note, 2026-10-22. Options for discussion, not decisions. Companion catalogue entries: Part C §A3; Part B B43, B41, B56, B59, B64, B75, B86._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

A fitted line is only half the answer: the other half is whether the leftovers look like nothing, and when the leftovers have a shape (a fan, an echo, a slow wander) or two inputs are nearly the same thing, the line still gets drawn but every "how sure are we" number under it is a lie.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (the hook candidates — 3, each one paragraph: a puzzle, a story with stakes, an everyday object; which is strongest and why)

**Puzzle — "Make R² = 0.9 out of two coin-flip piles."** Two piles of coins. Each day each pile adds a coin heads-up or tails-up at random; plot the running count of heads in pile A against pile B. Nothing links them. Yet regress one on the other and R² comes out 0.6, 0.8, 0.9 with a t-statistic of 10. The r-bloggers simulation (opened, §3) gets a "significant" relation between two independent random walks 71 % of the time at n = 50 and 97 % of the time at n = 2,000 — more data makes it *worse*. Freshers arrive believing that a big t and a big R² settle an argument; this is the fastest way to break that belief, and it needs only the two piles.

**Story with stakes — the hedge that drifts.** A desk wants to hedge a 3-year Treasury position with 1-year Treasuries. A junior regresses the weekly 3-year rate on the 1-year rate (Tsay's real data, 1,967 weeks 1962–1999): slope 0.924, standard error 0.004, R² = 95.8 % (Tsay p. 80). She reports "hedge ratio 0.92, basically certain". The residual plot in time order (Tsay Fig. 2.19, p. 82) shows a slow wander that stays on one side for years — in 1980–82 the hedge is off by 1.5 percentage points for months. Her standard error assumed each week's error was a fresh coin; it was not. Redo it on weekly *changes* and the slope is 0.78 with an honest standard error (Tsay p. 82–85). The stakes are a real hedge ratio and a real P&L; the artefact is in the book with every number to reproduce.

**Everyday object — the taxi meter.** Guess the fare from the distance. Short rides land within a rupee or two of the line; long rides scatter wildly (traffic, tolls, detours). The line through the cloud is fine; what is wrong is treating a 20-km prediction as "±2" because the 2-km rides were. That is heteroskedasticity in one picture (the fan), and it is the picture every web explanation uses (metricgate, Statistics By Jim — household spending vs income; opened, §3).

**Strongest: the coin-pile puzzle as the opener, the Treasury hedge as the spine.** The puzzle is the cheapest shock (two random walks and a readout) and it attacks the exact belief freshers hold. The hedge story then gives the chapter stakes and a real artefact with book-cited numbers for the engine tests. The taxi meter is a one-panel aside inside the heteroskedasticity beat.

**Best beginner explanations found on the web (URL, who, the analogy or visual, why it works)**

1. https://metricgate.com/blogs/heteroscedasticity-explained-intuitively/ — MetricGate. "Same scatter / different scatter" etymology; the **megaphone residual plot** ("tight near the left, wide and fanned out on the right"); household spending vs income ("low-income households spend a fairly predictable amount … high-income households have enormous discretion"); the honest consequence: "Heteroscedasticity does not bias your coefficient estimates … it makes your *certainty* wrong." Why it works: one shape, one everyday case, one sentence on what breaks. Includes an R recipe (n = 200, slope 1.5, error sd = 0.5·x) we can replicate as a test.
2. https://statisticsbyjim.com/regression/heteroscedasticity-regression/ — Jim Frost. Fan/cone plot; cross-sectional size example ("very low values for Delaware and very high values for California"); "Heteroscedasticity tends to produce p-values that are smaller than they should be"; a worked accidents-vs-population case fixed by weighting 1/Population. Why it works: it shows a *fix* changing the picture, not just a diagnosis.
3. https://theanalysisfactor.com/multicollinearity-explained-visually — Karen Grace-Martin. **Overlapping shapes**: each predictor's variance overlaps Y's; when two predictors overlap each other, the shared slice "can't be attributed to either predictor alone"; "If when X1 goes up, so does X2, how can you separately estimate the effect of a one unit increase of X1 on Y and X2 on Y? … there just isn't enough unique information." Why it works: it makes the *standard error* explode for a reason you can see (the unique slice is tiny).
4. https://www.r-bloggers.com/2019/06/spurious-correlations-and-random-walks/ — three independent random walks with pairwise correlations −0.49, −0.29, +0.59 ("at least a little bit of a shock"); the table n = 50 … 2,000 with % significant 0.71 → 0.97 and avg |t| 3.57 → 23.39; Durbin–Watson 0.09 on the residuals; the diagnosis "the spurious correlation occurs because our estimate is not consistent"; the postscript that it gets "progressively worse as the AR(1) process approaches a random walk". Why it works: it is a reproducible experiment with a table, and the n-dependence is the surprising part.
5. https://www.estima.com/webhelp/topics/spuriousregression.html — Estima (RATS). The rule of thumb "if the R² is bigger than the Durbin-Watson, you may have a spurious regression"; "the Durbin-Watson can be .2 or even lower"; remedies (lags, per-capita, deflating). Why it works: a one-line diagnostic a fresher can apply on day one.
6. https://towardsdatascience.com/linear-regression-in-time-series-sources-of-spurious-regression/ — two random walks of length 50, 100 simulations, mean |t| = 4.59 against the usual cutoff 2; cites Granger & Newbold's R² = 0.997 with DW = 0.53. (The post's own prose is internally inconsistent — "about a quarter (71 times)" — so quote the table, not the sentence.)
7. https://allaboutwork.org/2014/05/27/spurious-correlations-or-why-nicolas-cage-must-be-stopped/ — the Tyler Vigen examples (Nicolas Cage films vs pool drownings; mozzarella vs civil-engineering PhDs). Why it works: laughter before the lesson; the author's point that the correlations are "statistically significant … yet still meaningless".
8. https://setosa.io/ev/ordinary-least-squares-regression/ — Powell & Lehe. Opened, but the body is JS-rendered; the R0 audit (catalogue B64) records the move: literal **squares drawn on each residual**, drag a point, the line snaps. The single best "what least squares means" trick. **(interactive not re-run)**

**Common misconceptions (from forums, StackExchange, textbooks' own warnings)**

- "High R² means the relationship is real." Tsay p. 85 and every spurious-regression source: R² and t can be huge with no relationship at all when both series wander.
- "Heteroskedasticity makes the slope wrong." It does not; it makes the *standard error* wrong (metricgate, Jim Frost). Freshers conflate bias with imprecision.
- "More data fixes it." For two random walks, more data makes the false t *grow* (r-bloggers table).
- "Multicollinearity ruins prediction." It ruins *attribution* (which input gets the credit) and the per-coefficient SEs; the fitted plane and its predictions can be fine (Analysis Factor; Tsay's distinction between consistent β̂ and inconsistent Cov(β̂), p. 86–87).
- "A regression coefficient and an AR coefficient are the same kind of thing." Tsay p. 67: β₁ can be anything; φ₁ must be inside (−1, 1). This is the hand-off to A4.
- "Autocorrelated residuals are a time-series problem only." It is the same lie as heteroskedasticity: the SE formula assumed independent errors.

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept A — "The hedge that drifted" (annotated real artefact + guess-then-reveal)

*Mode:* annotated real artefact with a predict-then-reveal and live-drawn residuals (B59, B60). *Data:* Tsay's weekly 1-year and 3-year Treasury rates 1962–1999 if the sponsor can obtain the file (`w-gs1n36299.txt`, Tsay's site — not opened); otherwise FRED weekly GS1/GS3, same story.

1. Both rate series on one chart, 37 years, two colours. Caption: "You hold the 3-year. You can only trade the 1-year. How much 1-year per unit of 3-year?"
2. Scatter of levels (Tsay Fig. 2.18a). The learner drags a line through it; the engine shows their slope and the OLS slope (0.924). **Bet:** "How sure is that slope — within ±0.01, ±0.05, ±0.2?" Learner commits.
3. Reveal: naive SE 0.004, R² 95.8 %. A green "certain" badge. Then the residuals are **drawn in time order, left to right, slowly** (B59). The eye watches the leftovers stay above zero for 1970–73, dive to −1.5 in 1980. The badge turns amber.
4. The residual ACF appears bar by bar: 0.97, 0.95, 0.93 … "the leftovers remember themselves for years." Caption: the SE formula assumed each week's leftover was a fresh coin.
5. One button: "Use weekly changes instead." Scatter of changes (Fig. 2.18b), slope 0.78, SE 0.0075, residual ACF collapses to a small lag-1 spike (Fig. 2.21). The learner's bet is scored against the *honest* interval.
6. Closing still: three t-ratios for the same slope — 104.6 (naive), 46.7 (White), 40.1 (Newey–West) — "same line, three levels of honesty" (Tsay p. 87).

*Makes visible:* that "certainty" is a separate claim from "fit"; a residual pattern as an *event in time*; the fix changing the honest number. *Cost:* medium — one real dataset, OLS + HC/HAC in the engine, one live-draw. *Risk:* rates are an unfamiliar object for a fresher; the first panel must say "a rate is just a number that moves" and nothing more. Levels-vs-changes could read as a trick; the caption must say *why* (A4's unit root) in one line and defer the rest.

### Concept B — "The three liars" (comic strip / illustrated static text)

*Mode:* a drawn-character comic strip, six panels, no interaction; each liar gets one small static residual plot and one sentence of what breaks. Characters: **Funnel** (heteroskedasticity), **Echo** (autocorrelation), **the Twins** (multicollinearity); a fourth cameo, **the Drunks** (two random walks), for spurious regression.

1. Panel 1 — the honest fit: a scatter, the line, the residual squares (setosa), the leftovers as a flat band of noise. "This is what OLS assumes: leftovers that look like nothing."
2. Panel 2 — Funnel: the taxi-meter cloud; residual band widens to the right; the SE badge says ±2 while the right-hand points scatter ±10. One line: "slope fine, certainty wrong."
3. Panel 3 — Echo: residuals in time order, long runs of the same sign; a tiny ACF with tall bars. "Each leftover copies the last; the formula counted them as independent."
4. Panel 4 — the Twins: two near-identical columns (x₂ = x₁ + tiny noise); the plane balances on a knife-edge; coefficients +50 / −49 with SEs of 30 each; the *fit* is perfect. Overlapping-circle inset (Analysis Factor). "Prediction fine, credit assignment impossible."
5. Panel 5 — the Drunks: two random walks, R² = 0.9, DW = 0.1; axis icons swapped as in the 3b1b telestration scene — the dots don't change, the headline does.
6. Panel 6 — the detective's card: four questions to ask every regression (fan? runs? twins? wander?) and the one-line fixes (robust SE / HAC / drop or combine / difference).

*Makes visible:* that the four failures are one failure (the SE formula's assumptions) wearing four costumes; the cost of each in the one number that matters. *Cost:* low — six static figures and copy; engine needed only to generate the panels. *Risk:* static panels can feel like a textbook; mitigated by the characters and by keeping each panel to one sentence. The Twins' exploding coefficients need the ±50/−49 numbers on the panel or they will not land.

### Concept C — "Fool the regression" (game with a score)

*Mode:* a game. The learner's job is to get the highest R² and t out of *pure noise*, then to catch others doing it.

1. Round 1 — "You have two coin piles." Press **Flip 50 days**; running heads-counts of two independent piles are plotted and regressed. Readouts: R², t, and a score = R². Press **New piles** as often as you like; the best score sticks. Most learners will hit R² > 0.8 within five tries.
2. Round 2 — "More days should help, right?" A slider 50 → 2,000 days. The t grows (avg 3.6 → 23, r-bloggers); R² does not shrink. The score panel reads "fooled 71 % → 97 % of the time".
3. Round 3 — the honest detector: the DW readout appears (≈ 0.1) with the rule "R² > DW: suspect"; the **Difference** button shows R² collapse to ≈ 0.
4. Round 4 — "Spurious or real?": ten pairs of series shown one at a time (random-walk pairs, genuinely related pairs, Vigen-style pairs). Guess; reveal R², DW, and the differenced R²; score out of 10.
5. End card: the learner's best fake R², their detection score, and one sentence: "you cannot tell from the fit; you can tell from the leftovers."

*Makes visible:* that spurious fits are *easy to manufacture* and that sample size does not save you; the detector as a habit. *Cost:* low–medium — random-walk generator, OLS, DW; ten curated pairs. *Risk:* the game could teach "regression is useless"; the end card and Round 4's genuinely-related pairs (R² survives differencing) must show that real relationships pass the test.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right (formulas, assumptions) and reference numbers for tests, with their source (book + page)

**Model and estimator.** y = Xβ + e. OLS: β̂ = (X′X)⁻¹X′y (Tsay p. 86, eq. after 2.48). Residuals ê = y − Xβ̂ are orthogonal to every column of X (X′ê = 0): the projection fact that Panel 1 draws. Under independent, equal-variance errors, Cov(β̂) = σ²(X′X)⁻¹ (Tsay p. 86). For one regressor, Var(β̂₁) = σ²/Σ(xᵢ − x̄)².

**Hand-checkable test (projection property).** x = (1, 2, 3), y = (1, 2, 4): β̂₁ = 1.5, β̂₀ = −2/3; residuals (1/6, −1/3, 1/6), which sum to 0 and satisfy Σxᵢêᵢ = 0; SSE = 1/6; SST = 14/3; R² = 1 − (1/6)/(14/3) = 0.9643; σ̂² = SSE/(n − 2) = 1/6; SE(β̂₁) = √((1/6)/2) = 0.2887. (Derived by hand for this note; the Python key must reproduce it.)

**Heteroskedasticity.** Coefficients stay unbiased; Cov(β̂) is wrong. White's HC estimator Cov(β̂)_HC = (Σxₜxₜ′)⁻¹ [T/(T−k) Σ êₜ² xₜxₜ′] (Σxₜxₜ′)⁻¹ (Tsay eq. 2.49, p. 87). Simulation test from metricgate (opened): n = 200, x = Exp(rate 0.25) + 1, y = 2 + 1.5x + ε with sd(ε) = 0.5x, seed 42 — slope recovers ≈ 1.5, HC3 SE "meaningfully larger" than naive; the key should assert HC SE > naive SE and the slope within 2 SE of 1.5.

**Autocorrelation.** Newey–West HAC with Bartlett weights w_j = 1 − j/(ℓ+1), ℓ = int(4(T/100)^{2/9}) (Tsay eq. 2.50 and text, p. 87). Durbin–Watson DW = Σ(eₜ − eₜ₋₁)²/Σeₜ² ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂₁) (Tsay p. 85). Reference artefact (Tsay pp. 80–87; data not in repo — reproduce only if the sponsor supplies it): levels regression r₃ = 0.911 + 0.924 r₁, σ̂ₑ = 0.538, R² = 95.8 %, SE (0.032, 0.004); changes regression c₃ = 0.0002 + 0.7811 c₁, σ̂ₑ = 0.0682, R² = 84.8 %, SE (0.0015, 0.0075); with MA(1) errors c₃ = 0.0002 + 0.7824 c₁, eₜ = aₜ + 0.2115 aₜ₋₁, σ̂ₐ = 0.0668, R² = 85.4 %; t-ratio on c₁: 104.63 naive, 46.73 HC, 40.08 HAC.

**Multicollinearity.** VIF_j = 1/(1 − R_j²) where R_j² is from regressing xⱼ on the other columns; SE(β̂ⱼ) scales with √VIF. Test values (two regressors with correlation r): r = 0.9 → VIF 5.26, SE ×2.29; r = 0.99 → VIF 50.25, SE ×7.09; r = 0.999 → VIF 500.25, SE ×22.4. (Standard formula; computed for this note; no book page — ISLP ch. 3 would be the citation, not opened.) Engine must also show the fitted values barely change when a twin column is added (prediction vs attribution).

**Spurious regression.** Two independent random walks, OLS of one on the other: the t does not converge to zero as n grows. Statistical targets (r-bloggers, opened; 100 reps per n): share with p < .05 ≈ 0.71 (n = 50), 0.85 (100), 0.85 (200), 0.86 (500), 0.88 (1,000), 0.97 (2,000); avg |t| ≈ 3.57, 6.58, 8.88, 10.63, 17.05, 23.39; DW on residuals ≈ 0.1. The key should test these as rates with tolerance (±0.08) at a fixed seed, and assert the differenced regression rejects at ≈ 5 %. Classic signature (Granger & Newbold 1974, cited in the TDS post; original **not opened**): R² = 0.997 with DW = 0.53. Rule of thumb for the UI: R² > DW ⇒ suspect (Estima).

**Assumptions to state in the chapter (once, in words):** errors have mean zero, equal variance, no correlation with each other, no correlation with the regressors (the endogeneity sentence — one panel, deferred to G1), and the regressors are not linear combinations of each other.

## 7. Where it connects (previous/next chapters, and the one real-world moment a fresher will meet it)

- **From A2:** correlation as the tilt of a cloud; β̂₁ = ρ·s_y/s_x is the same tilt read as a slope; the two regression lines (y-on-x, x-on-y) from the QuantEcon bivariate-normal figure.
- **To A4:** "the leftovers remember themselves" *is* autocorrelation; the wandering residual *is* a unit root; differencing is A4's cure. Tsay p. 67's sentence (β can be anything, φ must be inside the circle) is the hand-off.
- **To A5:** the fan (variance changing with the input) becomes the fan *in time* (variance changing with the date) — heteroskedasticity → conditional heteroskedasticity → ARCH.
- **To G1/G3:** regularisation as the cure for the Twins; leakage and spurious backtests as the Drunks in disguise.
- **Real-world moment:** the first time a fresher is asked for a hedge ratio or a factor beta and reports the t-stat from `statsmodels` without looking at the residual plot. The chapter should end on that screen: the summary table, and the question "did you plot the leftovers in time order?"
