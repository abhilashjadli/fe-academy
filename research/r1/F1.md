## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

A price is a curve over the index, and the equation says how that curve relaxes as you walk back from expiry: every point moves toward the average of its neighbours, faster where the index is higher. A computer does exactly that, one neighbour at a time — and if it takes too big a step it does not get a slightly wrong answer, it gets a billion rupees for a ₹249 option.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: the heat rod from B6 was a pricer all along.** In B6 the reader watched a hot spot on a rod spread out and read the Black–Scholes equation as a heat equation. Open F1 with the payoff of the real 22,600 call — a sharp corner at 22,600 — laid along the rod as a temperature, and one rule: each point moves toward the average of its two neighbours. Step back a day, then two, then eleven: the corner rounds into a curve, and the value over 22,603 reads **249.15**, the formula's number, obtained without the formula. Strongest, because the reader already owns the rod and the number.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: ₹1.5 billion for a ₹249 option.** Take the same rule with a one-day step on a 100-point grid: the price of the call comes out as ₹1,468,530,074, and the grid holds values of ±₹1.5 billion either side of the strike. Hull's own Table 18.2 has the gentler version (negative option prices of −0.11 and −0.13 in the top-left of an explicit grid). Nothing is wrong with the equation; the step was 5½ times too long for the grid, and the three "probabilities" the scheme secretly uses went negative. A strong mid-chapter turn: the fresher sees that numerical methods fail loudly, and that the fix (take smaller steps, or use the implicit scheme) is a decision, not a detail.

**Hook C — the everyday object: a cooling cup, checked every minute.** Predict a cup's temperature by assuming it loses a fixed share of its excess every minute — fine at one-minute checks, and if you check once an hour and apply the hourly share in one go you can overshoot room temperature and oscillate. That is the explicit scheme's instability in a sentence; the implicit scheme is "decide the next temperature so that it is consistent with its own rate of cooling", which can never overshoot. Good for the standfirst; it names the two schemes without a symbol.

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

(Books cover this chapter unusually well; the web was used for the stability picture and the American-exercise free boundary.)
- **Wikipedia, "FTCS scheme"** and **"Crank–Nicolson method"**: the stability condition for the heat equation, r = αΔt/Δx² ≤ ½ (our forward-form scheme: δt ≤ δF²/(σ²F_max²)), and the note that Crank–Nicolson is unconditionally stable but can oscillate for discontinuous initial data unless damped — the kink-at-the-strike problem; Rannacher's fix (a few implicit half-steps first) named.
- **Wilmott forum / QuantStart, "Crank–Nicolson oscillations at the strike"**: the practitioner's version — place the strike on a node, start with implicit steps, or use a finer time grid; our numbers in §6 show the effect (Crank–Nicolson with 2 steps is 27 points wrong, with two Rannacher half-steps 0.8).
- **Hull & White 1990 (JFQA 25, 87–100)**, via Hull's footnote 26: the explicit method converges when the grid is in ln S — the fix the chapter mentions and does not build.

### Common misconceptions

1. **"A smaller step is always safer."** For the explicit scheme a smaller *time* step is; a finer *price* grid at the same time step is what breaks it (δt must shrink like δF²). The dial in Figure 1 shows the limit moving.
2. **"The implicit scheme is more accurate."** It is more *stable*; its error in time is the same order as the explicit scheme's. Crank–Nicolson is the accurate one — except at a kink, where it rings.
3. **"The PDE and the formula are different theories."** The formula is the PDE's exact solution for one payoff; the grid solves the same equation for any payoff. Figure 0 reads 249.15 off the grid.
4. **"American options need a different equation."** Same equation plus one rule each step: never let the value fall below what exercising pays. The exercise boundary is where the rule binds (Figure 3).
5. **"Negative prices mean a bug."** Hull's Table 18.2 has them with no bug — the scheme's three weights went negative; the trinomial reading explains why.
6. **"NSE index options can be exercised early."** They are European (NSE moved all equity derivatives to European exercise in 2010–11); the American figure is a labelled textbook example.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

- **Equation (forward form, as the site prices):** V_t + ½σ²F²V_FF − rV = 0 with V(F, T) = max(F − K, 0); grid F ∈ [18,000, 28,000], δF = 10,000/M, time steps δt = T/N; boundaries V(18,000) = 0, V(28,000) = 28,000 − K e^{−rτ}. Stencil: a_j = ½σ²F_j²/δF², L V = a_j(V_{j−1} − 2V_j + V_{j+1}) − rV_j. Explicit: V^{n+1} = V^n + δt L V^n; implicit: (I − δt L)V^{n+1} = V^n; Crank–Nicolson: (I − ½δt L)V^{n+1} = (I + ½δt L)V^n; tridiagonal solve (Thomas algorithm). Explicit stability: δt ≤ δF²/(σ²F_max²) — on δF = 100, F_max 28,000: **0.000507 y = 0.185 days**, i.e. at least 60 steps for 11 days; on δF = 200: 0.74 days.
