# A4 Time series: stationarity, AR/MA/ARIMA, random walk vs mean reversion, unit roots

_R1 deep-research note, 2026-10-22. Options for discussion, not decisions. Companion catalogue entries: Part C §A4; Part B B23, B60, B61, B62, B64, B70, B71, B76._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

A series has a memory, and one number says how fast it forgets: if it forgets, it keeps coming home and you can say where it will be next year; if it never forgets, every shock is permanent, it wanders off for good, and no chart can tell you which of the two you are looking at — only a test can.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (the hook candidates — 3, each one paragraph: a puzzle, a story with stakes, an everyday object; which is strongest and why)

**Puzzle — "Which of these nine are stationary?"** Hyndman & Athanasopoulos open their stationarity chapter (opened, §3) with a nine-panel figure: Google's share price, its daily changes, US strikes, house sales, egg prices, pigs slaughtered, lynx furs, beer, gas. Only two are stationary — the daily changes and the lynx furs — and almost everyone picks wrong: the lynx series has huge cycles and "looks" non-stationary, the price series looks like it has a level. The puzzle teaches the definition by making the learner feel their own wrong rule ("stationary = flat and boring"). It costs nothing to build: nine small charts and a reveal.

**Story with stakes — "it always comes back."** A pairs trader shows a fresher the spread between two bank stocks: it has crossed zero forty times in five years. "It always comes back — we buy when it's low." The fresher's job: is it a rubber band or a drunk? If the spread forgets (φ < 1), the expected time to return is finite and the trade has an edge. If it is a random walk (φ = 1), the expected time to return is *infinite* and the "forty crossings" were luck. The ADF test on the spread gives the answer; the stakes are a position with real money that either mean-reverts or never comes home. Tsay's own words on the random walk: "Thus, for all forecast horizons, point forecasts of a random-walk model are simply the value of the series at the forecast origin. Therefore, the process is not mean-reverting" (p. 64).

**Everyday object — the ball in a bowl, and the forgetting machine.** A marble in a bowl, kicked at random every second: it keeps falling back toward the centre, and how fast it falls back is the steepness of the bowl. Flatten the bowl to a table and the same kicks send the marble wandering anywhere. Tilt the bowl upside down and it flies off. The steepness is φ. The same mechanism as arithmetic: "today = φ × yesterday + news" — the *forgetting machine*. After k days, a shock is worth φᵏ of what it was: with φ = 0.8 the ACF bars are 0.8, 0.64, 0.51, 0.41 … exactly Tsay's Fig. 2.3 (p. 35).

**Strongest: the nine-panel puzzle as the opener, the bowl as the chapter's one picture.** The puzzle is cheap, humbling, and puts the definition in the learner's hands within 60 seconds. The bowl carries every later idea (φ as steepness; the unit root as a flat table; the stationary distribution as where the marble spends its time; the ACF as how fast kicks are forgotten). The pairs-trade story is the stakes paragraph before the unit-root test.

**Common misconceptions (from forums, StackExchange, textbooks' own warnings)**

- "Stationary means flat / no cycles." FPP3's lynx panel and Ripley's random-phase sine: cycles are fine if their timing is not fixed.
- "You can see a unit root." Tsay's S&P log index (Fig. 2.12) looks like it has trends and levels; ADF p = 0.95. The TDS point that "the next forecast is equal to the last value" is the only honest forecast of a random walk.
- "A random walk 'has to' come back to its mean." It has no mean to come back to; its variance is ℓσ² and grows without bound (Tsay p. 65). The pairs-trade story must be allowed to fail.
- "Returns are stationary, so prices are too." Returns ≈ stationary, log prices = their running sum = a unit root (Tsay §2.7.4, p. 67–68). Differencing is the bridge.
- "The constant term is the mean." In AR(p) the mean is φ₀/(1 − φ₁ − … − φₚ); in a random walk with drift the constant is the *slope* in time; in MA(q) it is the mean (Tsay p. 67).
- "The AR coefficient is a regression coefficient." Tsay p. 67: φ must be inside the circle, β need not be.
- "A unit-root test proves a random walk." Low power in short samples (catalogue B62) — the test fails to reject, it does not confirm; and "stationarity is a property of the model, not the data" (Ripley).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept A — "Stationary or not?" (guess-then-reveal gallery with a score)

*Mode:* guess-then-reveal, nine captioned panels, no sliders (B60, B61).

1. Nine small charts (adapt FPP3's nine with our own data: a stock price; its daily returns; a volatility index; a yield; an FX rate; a spread that mean-reverts; a seasonal volume; a cyclic-but-aperiodic series like the lynx; a trending series). Prompt: "Tick the ones whose rules don't change over time."
2. Submit. Each panel flips to show the answer and *one* reason (level drifts / variance grows / seasonal / cycles are aperiodic so fine).
3. Second ask, the hard one: for the price and for the lynx series, "which of these two is stationary?" — most will swap them.
4. Reveal with the ACF under each panel: the price's ACF bars stay near 1 for 30 lags (Tsay Fig. 2.11b, p. 70); the lynx ACF oscillates and dies. Caption: "the ACF of non-stationary data decreases slowly."
5. The score card: "you got 5/9 — the two you swapped are the two everyone swaps." Then the definition, in the FPP3 words, now earned.
6. Last panel: the price's *daily changes*, ACF collapsing to nothing — "differencing: the one-step fix", and the hand-off to Concept B.

*Makes visible:* the definition as a judgement the learner has already made and got wrong; the ACF as the honest version of the eye. *Cost:* low — nine series (mostly simulated to be safe), one ACF routine. *Risk:* nine panels at once is a wall; show three at a time on mobile. Real data (lynx) carries no finance meaning — use "an aperiodic cycle" simulated from AR(2) with complex roots instead, caption it honestly.

### Concept B — "The forgetting machine" (physical simulation / scrubbable animation)

*Mode:* physical simulation with one dial and a companion measurement panel (B70, B23, A1 rule 5).

1. A marble in a bowl, side view. Every tick a random kick (the "news"). The marble rolls back. Beside it, the series being traced: today's position. Caption: "today = φ × yesterday + news."
2. The dial is the bowl's steepness, φ, from −1 to 1.2. At φ = 0.9 the marble wanders but comes home; at 0.5 it snaps back; at −0.8 it overshoots side to side (oscillation; FPP3's φ < 0 case).
3. Press "release 500 marbles" from the same start: the cloud (B23) drifts and widens, then **stops changing** — the stationary distribution, drawn as a histogram that settles. The QuantEcon numbers appear as a check: with a = 0.9, b = 0.1, c = 0.5 the cloud settles at mean 1.0, variance 1.316.
4. Turn the dial to φ = 1. The bowl is flat. The marble wanders; the cloud of 500 keeps widening forever (variance ℓσ²). Caption: "no bowl, no home: a random walk." The forecast line is flat at today's value (Tsay p. 64).
5. Turn to φ = 1.05: the bowl inverts; marbles fly off the screen. "This never happens to a price for long, which is why the boundary matters."
6. Below the bowl, the ACF bars appear as the kicks are forgotten: at φ = 0.8 they are 0.8, 0.64, 0.51 … (Tsay Fig. 2.3); at φ = 1 they stay near 1. The step-through "+1 day" button shows one shock's worth shrinking by φ each click (B3 running column).
7. A second marble appears (AR(2)): two dials, and the bowl can be made to *ring* (complex roots) — the path cycles with Tsay's period formula shown live (for φ = (0.6, −0.4): 5.8 lags).

*Makes visible:* φ as a physical steepness, the unit root as the absence of a bowl, the stationary distribution as where the marbles settle, the ACF as forgetting. *Cost:* medium — a 1-D kinematic animation, AR(1)/AR(2) simulation, histogram, ACF. *Risk:* the bowl is a metaphor for *a multiplication*, not a potential well; the caption must say "the bowl is a picture of the rule today = φ·yesterday + kick" so nobody looks for gravity in the formula. Negative φ as a bowl is strained — use the cobweb inset (3b1b spider web) for that case.

### Concept C — "One symbol at a time" (symbol-into-picture step-through)

*Mode:* button-driven derivation, Back / Forward with a frame counter (B4, B83), each symbol colour-locked to a picture part; ends in the roots-on-a-circle explorable as an appendix.

1. Frame 1: rₜ = φ rₜ₋₁ + aₜ. The three symbols fly from the picture: rₜ₋₁ is yesterday's bar, φ is the dial, aₜ is the kick.
2. Frame 2–4: substitute once, twice, three times: rₜ = aₜ + φaₜ₋₁ + φ²aₜ₋₂ + …; each new term appears as a bar whose height is φᵏ — the bars form a decaying comb (the ψ-weights, Tsay eq. 2.11). "The present is a weighted memory of every past kick."
3. Frame 5: variance = σ²(1 + φ² + φ⁴ + …) = σ²/(1 − φ²); the geometric sum is the comb's total area; at φ = 1 the area is infinite — the boundary appears as the sum refusing to converge (B57).
4. Frame 6: the ACF ρ_ℓ = φ^ℓ read off the same comb.
5. Frame 7: MA(1) as a comb with only two teeth (rₜ = aₜ − θaₜ₋₁): finite memory, ACF cuts off after lag 1; ARMA as "a comb that starts with a few free teeth and then decays".
6. Frame 8: the random walk as the comb with every tooth of height 1 (ψᵢ = 1, Tsay p. 65); differencing removes the comb; "I" in ARIMA is the number of times you difference.
7. Frame 9 (appendix explorable): the characteristic polynomial's roots on a plane with the unit circle; drag φ₁, φ₂ in the triangle (right plane) and watch the roots (left plane) and the impulse-response comb; cross the circle and the comb grows (the 3b1b roots/coefficients and s-plane moves).

*Makes visible:* that AR, MA, ARMA, ARIMA are one object — a comb of weights on past kicks — differing only in the comb's shape; that the unit root is the comb that never decays. *Cost:* medium–high — a reconfigurable derivation (A1 rule 20), the comb bound to the dial, the two-plane widget. *Risk:* nine frames of symbols is exactly "rocket science" if the comb is not on screen from Frame 2; the roots plane is for the appendix only.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right (formulas, assumptions) and reference numbers for tests, with their source (book + page)

**Returns.** Rₜ = Pₜ/Pₜ₋₁ − 1; rₜ = ln(1 + Rₜ) = pₜ − pₜ₋₁; multi-period log return is the sum (Tsay eqs. 1.1–1.6, pp. 3–5). Test: monthly log 4.46 % ↔ simple 100(e^{0.0446} − 1) = 4.56 %; quarterly log return of 4.46 %, −7.34 %, 10.77 % is 7.89 % (Tsay Example 1.1, p. 6). Compounding table: $1 at 10 % → 1.10000 annual, 1.10250 semi-annual, 1.10381 quarterly, 1.10471 monthly, 1.10506 weekly, 1.10516 daily, 1.10517 continuous (Tsay Table 1.1, p. 4).

**Weak stationarity.** E(rₜ) = μ constant; Cov(rₜ, rₜ₋ℓ) = γ_ℓ depends on ℓ only; γ₀ = Var, γ₋ℓ = γ_ℓ (Tsay p. 25). ACF ρ_ℓ = γ_ℓ/γ₀; sample ρ̂_ℓ per eq. 2.2 (p. 26); under iid, SE ≈ 1/√T. Ljung–Box Q(m) = T(T+2) Σ ρ̂_ℓ²/(T − ℓ) ~ χ²_m (eq. 2.3, p. 27). Test target (Tsay p. 30, IBM monthly simple returns 1926–1997, T = 864): ρ̂₁…ρ̂₁₀ = .07 .01 −.02 −.01 −.01 −.01 −.00 .07 .05 .04, Q(5) = 5.4 (p = .369), Q(10) = 14.1; S-Plus output Q(5) = 5.4474, p = 0.3638 — reproduce only if the data file is available; otherwise test the Q formula on a simulated iid series (Q(5) ~ χ²₅, reject rate ≈ 5 %).

**AR(1).** rₜ = φ₀ + φ₁rₜ₋₁ + aₜ; E = φ₀/(1 − φ₁); Var = σ²/(1 − φ₁²); ρ_ℓ = φ₁^ℓ; stationary iff |φ₁| < 1 (Tsay pp. 33–34). Test: φ₁ = 0.8 → ρ₁…ρ₅ = 0.8, 0.64, 0.512, 0.4096, 0.32768; φ₁ = −0.8 alternates (Fig. 2.3, p. 35). Half-life ln(0.5)/ln|φ₁|: φ₁ = 0.8 → 3.11 lags (Tsay p. 49 states the concept; its printed formula is a misprint — see §3). Marginal-density recursion μₜ₊₁ = aμₜ + b, vₜ₊₁ = a²vₜ + c² with a = 0.9, b = 0.1, c = 0.5 → μ* = 1.0, v* = 0.25/0.19 = 1.3158 (QuantEcon, opened; derived from the stated formulas). Forecasts: r̂_h(1) = φ₀ + φ₁r_h, error variance σ²; two-step error variance (1 + φ₁²)σ²; r̂_h(ℓ) → μ as ℓ → ∞ (Tsay pp. 47–49).

**AR(2).** ρ₁ = φ₁/(1 − φ₂); ρ_ℓ = φ₁ρ_ℓ₋₁ + φ₂ρ_ℓ₋₂ (eq. 2.13, p. 36); characteristic equation 1 − φ₁x − φ₂x² = 0, stationary iff both roots exceed 1 in modulus (p. 39); complex roots iff φ₁² + 4φ₂ < 0; average cycle length k = 2π/cos⁻¹[φ₁/(2√−φ₂)] (p. 37). Tests: (φ₁, φ₂) = (0.6, −0.4): φ₁² + 4φ₂ = −1.24 < 0 ⇒ complex; ρ₁ = 0.4286, ρ₂ = −0.1429, ρ₃ = −0.2571 (computed from eq. 2.13 for this note); k = 2π/cos⁻¹(0.4743) ≈ 5.84 lags. Fig. 2.4's four pairs (1.2, −0.35), (0.6, −0.4), (0.2, 0.35), (−0.2, 0.35) are all stationary (p. 37). GNP AR(3) (eq. 2.15, p. 38): 0.0047 + 0.35rₜ₋₁ + 0.18rₜ₋₂ − 0.14rₜ₋₃, σ̂ₐ = 0.0098, factors as (1 + 0.52B)(1 − 0.87B + 0.27B²), cycle k = 2π/cos⁻¹[0.87/(2√0.27)] = 10.83 quarters (p. 39). Stationarity triangle: φ₂ < 1 − φ₁, φ₂ < 1 + φ₁, |φ₂| < 1 (FPP3 §9.3, opened).

**MA(1).** rₜ = c₀ + aₜ − θ₁aₜ₋₁; Var = (1 + θ₁²)σ²; ρ₁ = −θ₁/(1 + θ₁²); ρ_ℓ = 0 for ℓ > 1; invertible iff |θ₁| < 1 (Tsay pp. 51–52). Test: θ₁ = 0.5 → ρ₁ = −0.4.

**Random walk and unit roots.** pₜ = pₜ₋₁ + aₜ: p̂_h(ℓ) = p_h for all ℓ; Var[e_h(ℓ)] = ℓσ²; ψᵢ = 1 (Tsay pp. 64–65). With drift μ: pₜ = tμ + p₀ + Σaᵢ; conditional sd √t·σ (p. 65); 3M example μ = 0.0115/month, σ̂ₐ = 0.0639 (eq. 2.37, p. 66). Dickey–Fuller: regress pₜ on pₜ₋₁ (eqs. 2.38–2.39), DF = (φ̂₁ − 1)/std(φ̂₁); ADF adds lagged differences (eq. 2.40, p. 69); the statistic is *not* t-distributed — use tabulated critical values. Test targets (Tsay, real data, reproduce only if available): log US GDP ADF(p = 10) = −1.131, p = 0.7038, β̂ = 0.9994 (p. 70); daily log S&P 1990–2003, ADF(p = 14) = −0.9648, p = 0.9469 (p. 71). Simulation test: on a true random walk the ADF rejection rate at 5 % ≈ 5 %; on AR(1) with φ = 0.8, T = 500, power should be high (≥ 0.9).

**Assumption to state once:** every formula above is about the *model*; "stationary" is a property of the rule generating the data, not of one chart (Ripley).
