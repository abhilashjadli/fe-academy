# B6 Feynman–Kac: PDE ↔ expectation

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

"Today's value is the average over all the futures" and "today's value obeys a rule that says how it changes as you move the starting point" are the same statement written twice, and the binomial tree already computes both at once: each node is the discounted average of its children, and "a point equals the average of its neighbours, corrected for curvature" is what a diffusion equation says.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (the hook candidates)

**(a) The pub crawl (Wilmott's puzzle).** "At 5pm you are in the office. … At 6pm you will be at one of three places: The Pub; Still at the office, working late; Madame Jojo's. … We are going to look at the probability that at midnight you are tucked up in bed" (Wilmott p. 174). You do not need to know anything about what happens after 6pm except the three numbers "probability of bed from the pub / office / Jojo's". The probability from the office at 5pm is the weighted average of those three. Chop the hour into minutes, write that average with a Taylor series, and out falls a differential equation — the backward Kolmogorov equation (10.4) — "which is very similar to the Black–Scholes equation for the fair value of an option." Strong because it contains the whole theorem (value now = weighted average of values one step later) with no finance and no calculus, and the reader can feel "the answer flows backwards" before anything is derived.

**(b) Two pricers that must agree (a story with stakes).** Shreve opens Chapter 6 with it: "There are two ways to compute a derivative security price: (1) use Monte Carlo simulation to generate paths … and use these paths to estimate the risk-neutral expected discounted payoff; or (2) numerically solve a partial differential equation" (p. 263). On a desk both exist. They agree on a vanilla to three decimals, and when the Monte Carlo prints 4.747 against the PDE's 4.759 the fresher must know that the difference is 1/√N noise, not a bug — and must know why the PDE "give[s] the function g(t, x) for all values of (t, x) simultaneously" while "the Euler method … converges slowly and gives the function value for only one pair (t, x)" (p. 268). The stakes are real but the story needs the theorem first; good as the closing beat.

**(c) The cooling rod (an everyday object).** 3Blue1Brown's heat-equation lesson: a hot rod cools because "the rate of change for the temperature of a point is proportional to the second difference around it" — "Second derivatives give a measure of how a value compares to the average of its neighbors." Now hold the rod differently: paint the option payoff along it as a temperature, and let it "cool" as you walk back from expiry. The kink at the strike melts into the smooth Black–Scholes curve. The reader already knows what cooling looks like; the chapter only has to show that "node = discounted average of its two children" is the same rule as "point moves toward the average of its neighbours".

Strongest: (c) as the opening image with (a) as the explanation of *why* averaging-over-neighbours is the same as averaging-over-futures; (b) closes the chapter.

### Common misconceptions

- "The PDE and the expectation are two different models." They are two computations of one number (Shreve §6.1; theorempath "same object viewed from two sides").
- "Feynman–Kac works with the real-world drift." The expectation that solves the Black–Scholes PDE is under the risk-neutral measure (Shreve p. 271; Wilmott p. 180 "replace μ with r").
- "The tree is a model; finite differences are a numerical method." Wilmott p. 292: the binomial method "is just a simple version of an explicit finite-difference scheme"; Kwok's b₀ = 0.
- "Any time step works." The explicit scheme is stable only when the implied probabilities are non-negative (Kwok); the tree is stable *because* its weights are probabilities.
- "The value function f(t, X(t)) is a martingale." Not with discounting until multiplied by e^{−rt} (Shreve p. 270).
- "Monte Carlo and PDE should agree exactly." They agree to about 1/√N; 10 000 paths give ±0.05 on a 4.76 option (§6).
- "Heat flows forward, so the equation runs forward in time." The density runs forward (Fokker–Planck); the *value* runs backward (Wilmott §10.4 vs §10.6; theorempath).

### Concept A — "One clock, two answers" (explorable: two synchronised panels, one number)

Mode: explorable with a single draggable start point and one clock.

Storyboard:
1. The terminal payoff h(x) is drawn as a curve on the right edge of both panels (default: the call max(S − 40, 0); a "x²" toggle for the exact-answer case).
2. **Left panel:** the reader clicks a start point (t, x). One walker runs forward to T (Euler steps as in Shreve p. 267), reads the payoff where it lands, and the number appears in the middle box: "1 future: 2.31".
3. 100 walkers, then 10 000 (B22's cadence); their landing values pile into a column; the column is levelled into one bar (B32) and the box reads "average of 10 000 futures: 4.75 ± 0.05". A bracket shows the ±1/√N width shrinking.
4. **Right panel:** the same payoff curve at the right edge of a grid. Step one column back: every cell becomes the discounted weighted average of its neighbours to the right (two children for the binomial, three for the trinomial toggle; Wilmott (15.2)). The colour rod under the grid shows the value profile at that column.
5. Fill the whole grid backwards; the cell at the clicked (t, x) lights; the box reads "backward rule: 4.76". The two numbers sit side by side; a tick appears when they agree within the bracket.
6. **The drag.** Move the start point. The right panel already has the answer (Shreve p. 268: "for all values of (t, x) simultaneously"); the left panel has to re-run and its bracket re-opens. Caption: "the grid answers every start point at once; the walkers answer one."
7. **Curvature arrows** (B72) on the value profile: where the profile is convex the arrows point up — the cell sits *above* the plain average of its neighbours by an amount proportional to the second difference. Caption in 3b1b's words: "how a value compares to the average of its neighbors." The symbol ½σ²S²∂²V/∂S² appears under the arrow only now.
8. Toggle "x², drift 0, vol 1": the grid cell at x = 0.5, one unit from T, reads 1.25 = x² + (T − t); the walkers' average reads 1.25 ± 0.01. The closed form is printed as the check.

Makes visible: that the two computations are literally the same number; that the grid's rule is "average your children"; where the curvature term lives; the trade-off (one point vs every point). Cost: medium-high — a walker engine, a tree/FD engine, two renderers on one clock, three toggles. Risk: two panels compete for attention; mitigated by the single number box and by running the panels one at a time in beats 2–5 before they run together.

### Concept B — "The payoff melts" (physical simulation clip)

Mode: a 15-second clip (Manim or canvas), no controls, embedded in the text.

Storyboard:
1. A rod coloured by the call payoff at expiry: cold (zero) below the strike, hotter and hotter above it; the graph above the rod is the hockey stick.
2. The clock runs *backwards from expiry* (time-to-expiry increases). The explicit scheme from `update_graph` advances the profile; the kink at the strike rounds off first.
3. Curvature arrows appear only near the kink (that is where the second difference is large); the rod's colour boundary blurs.
4. After "six months" the profile is the Black–Scholes curve; a probe at S = 42 reads 4.76 (Hull p. 249).
5. Freeze. Release 1 000 walkers from the probe forwards in time; they fan out; their landing values on the payoff pile up; the pile levels to 4.76.

Makes visible: that a diffusion *smooths* — the kink is where the value differs most from the average of its neighbours; that the Black–Scholes curve is a cooled payoff. Cost: low-medium (the scheme is twenty lines; the walkers reuse Concept A's engine). Risk: a fresher may take "cooling" literally and ask what the temperature is; the title card must say the heat equation is an analogy for the *rule*, not the physics.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right

**Feynman–Kac (Shreve Thm 6.4.1, p. 268).** dX = β(u, X) du + γ(u, X) dW; g(t, x) = E^{t,x}h(X(T)); then g_t + β g_x + ½γ² g_xx = 0, g(T, x) = h(x). Assumption E^{t,x}|h(X(T))| < ∞. Proof principle: g(t, X(t)) is a martingale (Lemma 6.4.2), Itô gives dg = [g_t + βg_x + ½γ²g_xx] dt + γg_x dW, set the dt term to zero.

**Discounted Feynman–Kac (Thm 6.4.3, pp. 269–270).** f(t, x) = E^{t,x}[e^{−r(T−t)}h(X(T))] solves f_t + β f_x + ½γ² f_xx = r f, f(T, x) = h(x); the martingale is e^{−rt}f(t, X(t)).

**Black–Scholes–Merton (Shreve (6.4.9), p. 271; Hull (12.15), p. 243; Wilmott p. 291).** v_t + r x v_x + ½σ²x² v_xx = r v, with the drift r because the expectation is under P̃. Terminal data max(S − K, 0) or max(K − S, 0); the same equation prices "any option that pays off some function of S(T)".

**Backward Kolmogorov (Wilmott (10.4), p. 175; (10.6), p. 179).** ∂p/∂t + ½B²∂²p/∂y² + A∂p/∂y = 0 for the transition density in its backward variables; for dS = μS dt + σS dX it is p_t + ½σ²S²p_SS + μS p_S = 0; the expected payoff p_F has p_F(S, T) = F(S).

**Trinomial = explicit stencil (Wilmott p. 171).** φ± = ½(δt/δy²)(B² ± A δy), δy = O(√δt).

**Tree = explicit finite differences (Kwok; Wilmott (15.1)–(15.3)).** Explicit scheme V^{n+1}_j = b₁V^n_{j+1} + b₀V^n_j + b₋₁V^n_{j−1} in x = ln S; with Δx = σ√Δτ the middle coefficient b₀ = (1 − σ²Δτ/Δx²)e^{−rΔτ} = 0 and b₁ = p/R, b₋₁ = (1 − p)/R, R = e^{rΔτ}. Stability ⇔ all coefficients non-negative ⇔ 0 ≤ p ≤ 1. The CRR p = (e^{rΔt} − d)/(u − d) and the FD coefficient p_FD = ½ + (r − ½σ²)√Δt/(2σ) differ at O(Δt), so the two values agree to O(Δt) at coarse steps and to all printed digits at fine steps.

(iii) Monte Carlo average of discounted payoffs over N paths is within 3 standard errors of the grid value, standard error ≈ 1.58/√N for this option. (iv) For h(x) = x² with zero drift and unit volatility the grid cell equals x² + (T − t) to 1e−6 at any grid resolution (the scheme is exact for quadratics). (v) The explicit scheme with σ²Δt/Δx² > 1 produces a negative coefficient and oscillates — the test asserts the engine refuses such a grid. (vi) Discounting: the undiscounted grid for h(x) = x returns x; the discounted grid returns x·e^{−r(T−t)}… no: for a forward payoff S − K the discounted grid must return S − Ke^{−r(T−t)} (Hull Example 12.6, p. 244).

## 7. Where it connects

Previous: B2 (g(t, X(t)) is a martingale — the theorem is "find the martingale, set the dt term to zero"), B4 (Itô supplies the dt term), B5 (the expectation is under Q, which is why r and not μ is in the PDE), C2 (the tree being extended). Next: C3 (Black–Scholes "both derivations" meet here: hedging gives the PDE, Feynman–Kac gives the expectation), C4 (theta = −½σ²S²Γ − rSΔ + rV is the PDE read as a Greek identity; the curvature arrows are gamma), F1 (finite differences proper: implicit and Crank–Nicolson, stability), E2 (Monte Carlo is the left panel at scale), H1 (barriers are absorbing boundaries; Wilmott §10.7–10.9 first-exit problems are the same backward equation with different boundary data). The real-world moment: the week the fresher is handed a PDE pricer and a Monte Carlo pricer for the same product and asked why one prints 4.759 and the other 4.747 — and, soon after, why the PDE handles an American exercise feature in one line while the Monte Carlo needs Longstaff–Schwartz (F2).
