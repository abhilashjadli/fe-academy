# B5 Risk-neutral measure, Girsanov, change of measure

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

The price of a bet you can hedge is fixed by the hedge and not by anyone's opinion of the odds; the "risk-neutral probabilities" are simply the odds that would make today's price an honest discounted average, and switching to them re-weights the futures without changing which futures are possible or how jumpy the path is.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (the hook candidates)

**(a) The garden-path puzzle (Wilmott).** A stock is 100 today and will be 101 or 99 tomorrow; you are told, truthfully, that the chance of 101 is 60 %. A ticket pays 1 if the stock goes to 101 and 0 otherwise. What is the ticket worth? Almost everyone says 0.6. Wilmott's own words: "No, the answer is *not* 0.6. If that is what you thought, based on calculating simple expectations then I successfully 'led you up the garden path' to the wrong answer. … The correct answer is… ½" (Wilmott p. 264). The reason is a trade anyone can do: sell the ticket and buy half a share; the package is worth −49.5 tomorrow whether the stock rises or falls, so the ticket must cost exactly ½ today. Then the twist that makes the chapter: change 60 % to 90 % or 10 % — the hedge still works, and the price still does not move. His companion puzzle (p. 279): "Stock A is doubling in value every year, stock B is halving. Therefore both call options have the same value. But which would you buy?" This is the strongest hook, because the reader commits to a number, is shown a better one, and then watches it refuse to move.

**(b) The house that cannot lose (a story with stakes).** Raghu Ramachandran's casino (GlobalCapital): 100 people each pay 1 to guess a fair coin; 80 guess heads. If a correct guess pays 2, the house loses 60 when heads comes up and wins 60 on tails. So the house changes the payouts: 1.25 for a correct heads, 5.00 for a correct tails — now it ends at exactly 0 either way. A player who asks "what coin would make this bet fair for me?" finds an 80/20 coin, although "the actual coin is unchanged. Only the weights change, and these new weights are the risk-neutral probabilities." The stakes become real with riskhub's one-period tree (100 → 125 or 80, r = 4 %): the call priced with the real 65 % up-probability comes out 15.613, the hedge-consistent price is 12.854 — "about 21 % too high". A fresher who prices with beliefs is the counterparty everyone wants.

**(c) The bookmaker's odds (an everyday object).** A bookmaker's posted odds are not his forecast; they are set so that his book is balanced — he collects the same whatever happens. Punters still read "2-to-1" as a probability, and it is one, just not the bookmaker's belief. metricgate's phrase for the mathematical object is useful here: the Radon–Nikodym derivative is "a local exchange rate between two probability measures" — one number per outcome telling you how much to re-weight it. The bookie image reads instantly, but it needs a second step to get to "the odds that make the price an average", so it is better as a sidebar than as the opener.

Strongest: (a), with (b)'s 80/20 coin as the second beat (it explains *where* the risk-neutral numbers come from) and (c) as a one-line aside.

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Book chapters read

- **Hull OFOD 5e, Ch. 10 §10.1–10.2, pp. 200–205 (PDF 215–220).** The gentlest entry anywhere. Stock 20 → 22 or 18, call K = 21, r = 12 %, three months. Riskless portfolio: long 0.25 shares, short 1 call, worth 4.5 either way, present value 4.367, so f = 0.633 (p. 201). Then (10.2)–(10.3): f = e^{−rT}[p f_u + (1−p) f_d], p = (e^{rT} − d)/(u − d) = 0.6523. The paragraph "Irrelevance of the Stock's Expected Return" (p. 203) is the chapter's thesis in Hull's words: "we get the same option price when the probability of an upward movement is 0.5 as we do when it is 0.9 … The probabilities of future up or down movements are already incorporated into the price of the stock." §10.2 shows p is "the probability of an up movement in a risk-neutral world", and "Real World vs. Risk-Neutral World" (p. 205) computes the real-world q = 0.7041 for a 16 % expected return, the real-world expected payoff 0.7041, and the footnote that the correct discount rate for the option would then be 42.6 % — "it is not easy to know the correct discount rate". *Good:* every number is small and checkable. *Bad for a beginner:* the word "risk-neutral world" invites the misreading that we assume people don't care about risk.
- **Hull §10.7, pp. 211–212 (PDF 226–227).** Figure 10.9 shows the same one-step tree twice, once with q and once with p. "When we move from the real world to the risk-neutral world the expected return on the stock changes, but its volatility remains the same … This is an illustration of an important general result known as Girsanov's theorem … Moving from one set of risk preferences to another is sometimes referred to as *changing the measure*." The whole Girsanov message in one figure of two trees with different labels.
- **Hull §12.7, pp. 244–245 (PDF 259–260).** Risk-neutral valuation as a three-step recipe (assume μ = r, compute the expected payoff, discount at r) and the warning: "risk-neutral valuation … is merely an artificial device … When we move from a risk-neutral world to a risk-averse world, two things happen. The expected growth rate in the stock price changes and the discount rate … changes. It happens that these two changes always offset each other exactly."
- **Hull Ch. 21 §21.1–21.4, pp. 483–492 (PDF 498–507).** Market price of risk λ = (μ − r)/σ; "A measure is the units in which we value security prices"; the equivalent martingale measure result; alternative numéraires. Too general for B5 (rates models are out of scope); keep only the sentence "As we move from one market price of risk to another, the expected growth rates of security prices change, but their volatilities remain the same" (p. 486).
- **Shreve SCF II §1.6, pp. 32–39 (PDF 48–55).** Theorem 1.6.1: P̃(A) = ∫_A Z dP with Z ≥ 0, E Z = 1; Ẽ X = E[XZ]. The sentences a fresher needs: "we need to reassign probabilities in Ω using Z to tell us where in Ω we should revise the probability upward (where Z > 1) and where we should revise the probability downward (where Z < 1)" (p. 33); the two measures "must agree on what is possible and what is impossible; they may disagree on how probable the possibilities are" (p. 35); "*there is only one world* … we do not change our view of the world of possibilities" (p. 35); "We want to change the distribution of Y *without changing the random variable Y*. In finance, the change from the actual to the risk-neutral probability measure changes the distribution of asset prices without changing the asset prices themselves" (p. 37). Example 1.6.4: Ω = [0, 1], P uniform, Z(ω) = 2ω, P̃[a, b] = b² − a². Example 1.6.6: X standard normal, Y = X + θ, Z = exp(−θX − θ²/2) makes Y standard normal under P̃. Note: the *finite* example Shreve refers to is Volume I Example 3.1.2 (not available); in Volume II the finite formula appears only as (5.2.4), Z(ω) = P̃(ω)/P(ω), p. 211.
- **Shreve §5.1–5.2, pp. 209–221 (PDF 225–237).** Theorem 5.2.3 (Girsanov): Z(t) = exp{−∫Θ dW − ½∫Θ² du}, W̃(t) = W(t) + ∫Θ du is Brownian under P̃. Market price of risk Θ = (α − R)/σ (5.2.21); dS = R S dt + σ S dW̃ (5.2.23); risk-neutral pricing formula V(t) = Ẽ[e^{−∫R}V(T) | F(t)] (5.2.31); §5.2.5 derives the Black–Scholes–Merton formula from the expectation. The paragraph on p. 217 is the best statement of "same paths, different weights" in print: "In discrete time, the change of measure does not change the binomial tree, only the probabilities on the branches of the tree. In continuous time, the change … changes the mean rate of return of the stock but not the volatility. The volatility tells us which stock price paths are possible … After the change of measure, we are still considering the same set of stock price paths, but we have shifted the probability on them. If α(t) > R(t), as it normally is, then the change of measure puts more probability on the paths with lower return." *Bad for a beginner:* the proof goes through Lévy's theorem and iterated conditioning; nothing is drawn.
- **Wilmott, Ch. 15 "The binomial model", pp. 261–281 and 291–292 (PDF 282–302, 312–313).** The coin-flip example above with every number; §15.4 "Which part of our 'model' didn't we need?": "The value of an option does not depend on the probability of the stock rising or falling. … This is because we have hedged the option with the stock … We do care about the stock price range, however." §15.5.1: "The expected payoff is definitely 0.6 for this option. It's just that this has nothing to do with the option's value." §15.7 adds interest (r = 0.1, one day: discount factor 0.9996, value 0.51963). §15.8: "We pay less than the future expected value because the stock is risky." §15.10 "The real and risk-neutral worlds": on the "risk-neutral planet" they solve p′·101 + (1 − p′)·99 = 100, get p′ = 0.5, price 0.5 — "Damn and blast! They have found the correct answer for the wrong reasons! … Two wrongs in this case do make a right. And this technique will always work." §15.12–15.13: p′ = ½ + r√δt/(2σ) against the real p = ½ + μ√δt/(2σ): "where one has the interest rate r the other has the drift μ, but otherwise they are the same. Strange." "Observe that the risk-free interest rate plays two roles … once for discounting … and … as the drift rate." §15.21: the limit is the Black–Scholes equation — "Again, the drift rate μ has disappeared." *Good:* the only source that makes the reader guess wrong first. *Bad:* the "risk-neutral planet" story, read literally, is the misconception the chapter must kill.
- **Wilmott §10.11–10.12, pp. 179–181 (PDF 200–202).** The backward equation for the expected payoff has μ; "forget that there is such a quantity as μ and replace it … with r. The resulting equation is *exactly* the Black–Scholes equation." Box: "The fair value of an option is the present value of the expected payoff at expiry under a risk-neutral random walk." §10.12 "A common misconception": delta is *not* the probability of finishing in the money — "There is a μ in the formula for the probability. There is no μ in the option's delta."

### Common misconceptions

- "Risk-neutral means we assume investors don't care about risk." Hull p. 245 ("artificial device"), tradermath, Wilmott's "Do not think that this p′ is in any sense real. No, the real probabilities are still 60 % and 40 %."
- "The option price is the expected payoff discounted at r." Only under Q. Under P you would need a 42.6 % discount rate (Hull p. 205 footnote).
- "Delta is the probability of finishing in the money." Wilmott §10.12: wrong twice (μ vs r, and d₁ vs d₂).
- "Changing the measure changes the paths / the volatility." Shreve p. 217; Hull p. 212: the volatility is untouched.
- "Option-implied probabilities are forecasts." Lihong; riskhub's VaR table (1-year 99 % VaR 32.65 under P vs 35.94 under Q — "the choice moves the answer by about 10 %").
- "A measure change is like a change of basis" (quantnet): no — the outcomes are the same objects; only their weights change.
- "Q can give weight to something P says is impossible." Shreve p. 35 and metricgate: both measures must agree on what is impossible.

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept A — "The bet you can't lose on" (game / guess-then-reveal)

Mode: guess-then-reveal game with one belief dial.

Storyboard:
1. A coin with a **belief dial** (P(up) = 60 %), a stock at 100 that goes to 101 or 99, a ticket paying 1 on up. The reader is asked to type a price. The page records it (most will type 0.60).
2. Reveal: a market-maker character sells the ticket at the reader's price and buys ½ share. Two columns, "if 101" and "if 99", both end at −49.5 plus the ticket price. The ticket is worth exactly ½; the reader's 0.60 shows as 0.10 of free money for the market-maker (Wilmott p. 267: "A sells the option for 0.55, gets all the business and makes a guaranteed 0.05 profit").
3. **The dial moment.** The reader spins belief from 10 % to 90 %. Two needles: "your belief" swings; "the price" does not. The hedge columns do not change because they never contained the probability.
4. "What odds would make this price an honest average?" A second slider searches; it stops at 50 %. Label it: *risk-neutral probability = the odds under which today's price is the discounted average payoff.* Ramachandran's 80/20 coin appears as a one-panel aside.
5. Change the *range*: up = 103 instead of 101. The price jumps to 1.2 (Wilmott p. 268, Δ = 0.6). Caption: "the range matters; the odds do not."
6. Switch on interest: one day at 10 %, discount factor 0.9996; hedge unchanged; price 0.51963; the honest-average odds move to 0.51984 (Wilmott pp. 269, 274).
7. "Hull mode": 20 → 22/18, K = 21, r = 12 %, 3 months: Δ = 0.25, price 0.633, p = 0.6523; a separate "forecast" panel shows the real-world q = 0.7041 and expected payoff 0.7041 with no wire into the price box.
8. Challenge sentence: "Set your belief to 99 %. Did the price move? Now move the up-price to 103. Did it?"

Makes visible: that the probability is *absent* from the calculation, not cancelled by luck; where the risk-neutral number comes from (solving backwards for an honest coin). Cost: low — a one-step tree engine and two needles. Risk: a fresher may conclude probabilities are irrelevant to finance in general; the forecast panel in beat 7 and the riskhub VaR aside exist to prevent that.

### Concept B — "Same futures, different weights" (explorable: a re-weighting dial over a histogram)

Mode: explorable with one dial and two readouts (re-weight, never re-simulate).

Storyboard:
1. 2 000 year-end prices simulated once from S₀ = 100, μ = 9 %, σ = 20 % (riskhub's parameters) shown as a dot histogram; the dots are the same objects for the whole page.
2. Readout 1: "discounted average of S_T" = 105.13, needle right of the "100" mark — after discounting, the stock is not a fair bet under these weights.
3. A **θ dial**. Each dot's weight becomes exp(−θW_T − θ²T/2), drawn as a thin line over the same axes (at θ = 0.25 it is 1.0579 × (S_T/100)^{−1.25}: 1.40 at 80, 1.06 at 100, 0.80 at 125, crossing 1 at 104.6). Bar heights change; dots do not move; a counter "total weight = 1.000" stays pinned (B34 keep: one slow update with the three beats before any fast-forward).
4. The needle reaches 100 exactly at θ = 0.25 = (μ − r)/σ. Label: *market price of risk — the only dial setting at which the discounted average is today's price.* Turn further and the needle overshoots.
5. Readout 2: call price = discounted weighted average of max(S_T − 100, 0): 13.33 at θ = 0, 9.93 at θ = 0.25, matching Black–Scholes 9.925 to within the sample noise.
6. Readout 3: the weighted spread of log(S_T/100) stays 0.20 for every θ; only the weighted mean moves (0.07 → 0.02). Caption: "the drift moved; the volatility did not."
7. Toggle to the **area view** (B32): bar widths = weights, heights = payoff; the levelled bar's height is the price; turning θ re-widens the bars with heights pinned.
8. Girsanov panel (illustrated text, no interaction): twenty paths with weights shown as line opacity; the Shreve p. 217 sentence as the caption; the one-line statement W̃ = W + θt.

Makes visible: that Q is P with each outcome multiplied by a known function of the outcome; that a single number (θ) does the job; that the spread is untouched. Cost: medium — a fixed sample, a weight function, three readouts, an area-view morph. Risk: the θ dial has no meaning to a fresher until beat 4 names it, and the exponential weight formula must not appear before the histogram has been tilted by hand.

### Concept C — "Three traders, one price" (drawn-character comic strip)

Mode: six-panel comic strip with three characters: the Optimist (70 % up), the Pessimist (30 % up), the Hedger (does not care).

Storyboard:
1. All three look at the 100 → 101/99 stock and the ticket. Optimist: "worth 0.70." Pessimist: "0.30." Hedger: "½."
2. The Hedger sells to the Optimist at 0.70 and buys half a share; her ledger shows +0.20 locked in both columns.
3. The Hedger buys from the Pessimist at 0.30 and sells half a share; +0.20 again.
4. Both notice: whatever they believe, trading with the Hedger loses them money unless the price is ½. Their beliefs converge on nothing; the price converges on ½ (Wilmott p. 267's undercutting story).
5. The Optimist asks: "but ½ *is* an average — of what coin?" The Hedger: "a 50/50 coin nobody owns. Two wrongs make a right" (Wilmott p. 273).
6. Last panel: the same three with a riskhub-style VaR question ("how likely is a 20 % fall?") — now the Hedger says "ask them, not me": forecast with P, price with Q.

Makes visible: that the price is a social fact enforced by the hedge, not a belief; the separation of forecasting from pricing. Cost: low build, high drawing effort (six panels, three characters, consistent with house style). Risk: comics tell rather than show — the reader never moves the dial, so this is an opener or a closer, not the core.

## 5. My recommendation

A sequence of two: **Concept A first, then Concept B**, with Concept C's panel 5 ("a coin nobody owns") as a single drawn aside between them, and Girsanov proper as illustrated text at the end of B.

Easy first: A is Hull §10.1 and Wilmott §15.2–15.4 made playable; it uses nothing beyond arithmetic and it puts the reader's own wrong number on the screen. Interesting second: B's dial moment (the needle landing on 100 at exactly θ = (μ − r)/σ, with the call price landing on Black–Scholes) is the surprise the chapter is for, and it carries the two sentences the catalogue flagged as missing everywhere — "the paths do not change, the weights do" and "the drift disappears, the volatility does not". Interactive third: both have one control each, named, with the reading stated. The path-space statement of Girsanov is not buildable and is not needed by a fresher; one figure and Shreve's sentence do it.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right

**One-period replication (Hull pp. 201–203; Wilmott pp. 264–268, 276–278).** Δ = (f_u − f_d)/(S₀u − S₀d). Riskless portfolio S₀Δ − f earns r, so f = e^{−rT}[p f_u + (1 − p) f_d] with p = (e^{rT} − d)/(u − d). Assumptions: two states, no arbitrage, borrowing and lending at r, no transaction costs. The real-world probability does not appear.

**Discrete change of measure (Shreve (5.2.4)–(5.2.5), p. 211; Thm 1.6.1 p. 33).** Z(ω) = P̃(ω)/P(ω); P̃(A) = Σ_{ω∈A} Z(ω)P(ω); E Z = 1; Ẽ X = E[XZ]; P and P̃ equivalent (agree on null sets). Radon–Nikodym theorem 1.6.7, p. 39.

**Continuous change of measure and Girsanov (Shreve Ex. 1.6.6 pp. 36–39; Thm 5.2.3 pp. 212–214).** Z = exp{−θW_T − ½θ²T}; E Z = 1; under P̃, W̃(t) = W(t) + θt is Brownian motion. Condition (5.2.13) E∫Θ²Z² du < ∞. With Θ = (α − r)/σ (5.2.21): dS = rS dt + σS dW̃ (5.2.23), S(T) = S₀ exp{σW̃(T) + (r − ½σ²)T}. Pricing formula V(t) = Ẽ[e^{−r(T−t)}V(T) | F(t)] (5.2.31). Black–Scholes–Merton from the expectation, (5.2.36): c = xN(d₊) − e^{−rτ}KN(d₋).

**Discrete-limit form (Wilmott (15.3) p. 278; p. 275).** u = 1 + σ√δt, v = 1 − σ√δt; real p = ½ + μ√δt/(2σ); risk-neutral p′ = ½ + r√δt/(2σ).
