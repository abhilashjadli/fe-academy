# E3 Portfolio theory, the efficient frontier, and PCA on yield curves

_Phase R1 deep research note (2026-11-10). Options for discussion with the sponsor, not decisions. Prerequisites assumed: 0.2 (mean and standard deviation), A2 (correlation is a shear — the tilted cloud and the ellipse are this chapter's objects), A5 (volatility as the width), E1 (the linear model's covariance matrix; Hull's two-asset diversification benefit; Hull 16.9's PCA for VaR). Everything else is taught here, including eigenvectors, which are introduced as "the axes the cloud already has"._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

Two things, one picture each: mixing assets that do not move together buys you less wobble for the same return, and the menu of best mixes is a single curve; and a cloud of correlated numbers — ten bond yields moving every day — has a few natural axes along which almost all the motion happens, so "the yield curve moved" is nearly always one of three sentences: it shifted, it tilted, it bent.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**Hook A — the puzzle: the bad asset that improves the portfolio.** Wilmott's five assets (Figure 18.1, p. 320): A has 15 % return at 20 % risk, D has 10 % at 30 %. Nobody would hold D alone — *"We can rule out D"*. Yet in Figure 18.3 the efficient frontier, drawn with all five, passes *above* A, and the portfolios on it hold D. The puzzle resolves in one sentence that freshers find surprising the first time: an asset's worth in a portfolio is not its own return and risk but how it moves against the rest. The chapter's own version uses real NSE indices (§8 data ask): the lowest-return sector index may still earn a place on the frontier because it zigs when the others zag. Strongest opener because the "wrong" answer is the one every fresher gives.

**Hook B — the story with stakes: Markowitz's 1952 observation.** Markowitz (Hull fn. 6, p. 352: *"Harry Markowitz was one of the first researchers to study the benefits of diversification… awarded a Nobel prize for this research in 1990"*) noticed that the then-standard advice — put everything in the highest-expected-return stock — would be followed by nobody, so the advice must be missing something, and the something was variance. The story is short and true; two sentences in the standfirst.

**Hook C — the everyday object: the three ways a rope moves.** Hold a skipping rope by both ends: you can raise it (level), tilt it (slope), or make it bow in the middle (curvature); every other wobble is small and dies fast. A yield curve is that rope, and PCA is the machine that finds the rope's three motions from the data without being told they exist — Litterman and Scheinkman found that for US Treasuries the first motion alone is about ninety percent of everything that happens, and three motions are over 95 %. Best as Figure 3's opening image, because the reader can see the three shapes before meeting a single eigenvector.

**Recommendation: A opens the frontier half, C opens the PCA half, B is a line.** The chapter has two halves joined by one object — the covariance matrix — and the storyboard should make the join explicit: the frontier asks the matrix "what mix has least wobble?"; PCA asks it "which directions carry the wobble?".

### Best beginner explanations found on the web

- **Jun Pan, "Factors Influencing the Yield Curve" (SAIF lecture slides, en.saif.sjtu.edu.cn/junpan/slides/Slides3_Class2.pdf)**: the Litterman–Scheinkman result stated for a class — “most of the variation in returns on all fixed-income securities can be explained in terms of three 'factors,' or attributes of the yield curve: level, steepness, and curvature” — and the right teaching order: “To simplify our analysis, let's start with zero-coupon bonds. Once we understand the common factors in zero rates of various maturities, we can readily apply this knowledge to coupon bonds, since they are weighted sums of the zero-coupon bond prices.” Works because it says what to simplify first.
- **Bliss, "Movements in the Term Structure of Interest Rates" (Atlanta Fed Economic Review 1997 Q4, atlantafed.org)**: the number freshers should carry — “Of Litterman and Scheinkman's three factors, the first one accounted for an average of 89.5 percent of the observed variation in yield changes across maturities” — and the caution that the follow-up study on money-market rates “found, surprisingly, that four factors are important”, explained by the mix of instruments. Works because it gives the headline number and its exception.
- **Pérignon, Smith & Villa, "Why common factors in international bond returns are not so common" (JIMF 2007, via math.tecnico.ulisboa.pt)**: the misconception-killer — PCA “tends to pick too many factors” when you pool countries, because “the goal of PCA is to extract the factors that maximize the explained variance, but not necessarily factors that are common”. One sentence in the close: PCA finds directions of variance, not causes.
- **CCIL, "ZCYC Parameters" (ccilindia.com/zcyc-parameters)**: the Indian data source — CCIL fits a Nelson–Siegel–Svensson zero curve daily (“Spot Rate = ß0 + (ß1+ß2) *(1-e(-m/t1))/(m/t1) – ß2*e(-m/t1) + ß3*(1-e(-m/t2))/(m/t2) - ß3*e(-m/t2)”) and publishes the six parameters per day (e.g. 2 Jan 2026: β₀ 7.5358, β₁ −2.1464, β₂ −15.2132, β₃ 17.6262, τ₁ 9.5722, τ₂ 9.8044); CEIC's listing of CCIL tenor yields shows daily 2 y/5 y/10 y/15 y/20 y series “from Aug 2008… with 4190 observations”. Works as the route to a real Indian curve for Figure 3 (§8).
- **Cbonds, "India Government Bond Zero-Coupon Yield Curve"**: FBIL's curve “includes 210 tenors ranging from 1 week to 50 years… constructs the curve using government bond transactions executed through the Reserve Bank of India platform”, published “with a 7-day reporting lag” — a second source if CCIL's archive is awkward.

### Common misconceptions

1. **"Diversification means more stocks."** Thirty stocks that all move with the index are one bet; the frontier is about correlation, not count (Wilmott's O(N^{−1/2}) holds only for *uncorrelated* assets).
2. **"The efficient frontier tells you what to buy."** It tells you the menu; where you sit on it is yours (Wilmott §18.4: *"the efficient frontier is objective, given the data, but the 'best' position on it is subjective"*). And the menu is drawn from estimated inputs — *"do they even exist?"* — so the frontier moves when the window moves; a still shows the same five assets' frontier on two different five-year windows.
3. **"Negative weights are a bug."** They are short sales; Wilmott's 30 % target needs −146 % of A. The page should show the frontier with and without a no-short constraint (the constrained one is inside the other).
4. **"PCA finds causes."** It finds directions of variance; "level, slope, curvature" are names people gave to shapes (Pérignon et al.). The components are uncorrelated by construction, not independent in any deeper sense.
5. **"The first component is the average."** For a yield curve PC1 is *nearly* flat but not exactly (Frye: 0.21 at 3 m, 0.36 at 3–5 y, 0.25 at 30 y) — the curve's middle moves most; a figure that overlays "equal weights" on PC1 shows the difference.
6. **"Three factors always."** Bliss's money-market exception; and PCA on *levels* instead of *changes* gives nonsense (Hull: "carried out on percentage changes… rather than on their actual changes"; for yields, on daily changes in bp).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three DIFFERENT modes

### Concept 1 — "Mix two, then five" — the frontier as a drawn curve (explorable with one dial, then a bet)

1. Two assets from Wilmott's Figure 18.2 (A: 15 %/20 %; E: 20 %/35 %) with one dial, **the weight of A** (−50 % → 150 %): a dot moves along the hyperbola; its bold part is the efficient half; past 100 % the dot leaves the segment ("you have sold E to buy more A").
2. A second dial, **ρ** (−1 → +1): the hyperbola fattens into a straight line at ρ = 1 and bends to touch the vertical axis at ρ = −1 — the whole idea of diversification in one sweep (A2's shear becoming a shape in risk–reward space). Readouts: return, risk, Sharpe-type slope.
3. The bet: "add the five assets of Figure 18.1 — can a portfolio beat A's 15 % at less than A's 20 % risk?" Most say no (A dominated everyone). Reveal: the five-asset frontier passes above A, and the portfolio that does it holds the 'ruled-out' D. One line of why: D's correlations are the lowest in the table.
4. The frontier with a risk-free rate: the tangent line, the market portfolio dot, "lend or borrow along the line" — one caption, no new control.
5. Real version if the data arrive (§8): five NSE indices 2016–2026, the realised frontier and the same bet with real sector names.

Makes visible: the hyperbola is a *mixture*, the frontier is its envelope, and the bad asset's place on it. Cost: low (a closed-form two-asset curve; the N-asset frontier is a small quadratic solve — Wilmott's Lagrange solution reproduces his spreadsheet). Risk: the fresher reads the frontier as advice — the standfirst and misconception 2 answer it.

### Concept 2 — "The cloud's own axes" — covariance to PCA in two dimensions (scrubbable animation)

1. A2's tilted cloud of two daily series (if real: NIFTY vs Nifty Bank daily returns; else Tsay's IBM/HPQ pair), ρ shown.
2. Scrub **a direction** around the clock (0° → 180°): project every dot onto that line, a histogram of the projections grows beside it, its width read out. The width is largest along the cloud's long axis and smallest across it — those two are the principal components, found by *looking*, not by algebra.
3. A second panel: "how much of the total wobble each axis carries" — two bars, e.g. 85 % / 15 % — and the identity that the bars sum to the two original variances (Tsay 9.13).
4. Scrub ρ (as in A2): the axes rotate toward 45° and the second bar shrinks to nothing as ρ → 1 — one series is the other; the cloud is one-dimensional.
5. Caption: the arrows that only stretch (3b1b's eigenvector picture) are these axes; the word arrives after the thing.

Makes visible: PCA is "turn the cloud until the wobble is all along one axis"; eigenvalues are widths; the dimension collapses when correlation does. Cost: low (projection + histogram; A2's cloud component). Risk: none serious; keep ρ as the only slider besides the direction.

### Concept 3 — "Shift, tilt, bend" — the yield curve's three motions (annotated real artefact + small multiples)

1. The rope: a yield curve (real CCIL/FBIL tenors 3 m … 30 y if supplied; Frye's ten US tenors from Hull as the labelled textbook fallback) with three ghost curves: shifted, tilted, bowed — Hook C.
2. The data: a strip of N daily curves (the "rope film"); scrub a day and the curve wobbles.
3. PCA on the daily *changes*: three small multiples — PC1 (all one sign, humped in the middle), PC2 (short end down, long end up), PC3 (ends up, middle down) — with their shares (Frye: 83.1 %, 10.0 %, 2.8 % — 93.1 % for two, 95.9 % for three; Litterman–Scheinkman ~89.5 % for the first).
4. One dial: **how many factors** (1 → 10): a chosen day's actual change is rebuilt from k factors; at k = 1 the rebuild is a parallel shift that misses the twist; at k = 3 it is almost the day. Readout: the share explained, the rebuild error in bp.
5. The bridge to E1 (Hull Table 16.5): a bond book's exposures per tenor collapse to two factor exposures (−0.08 and −4.40 $ m/bp) and one VaR (62.12); "using only one factor would significantly understate VaR" because this book is a *steepener* — its risk is all in PC2. A result card.

Makes visible: the three shapes are in the data, not imposed; most of the risk of a curve book is one or two numbers; which number depends on the book. Cost: medium (an eigen-solver for a 10 × 10 symmetric matrix — Jacobi, 40 lines; tested on Tsay 9.1 and Frye; CCIL/NSS curve reconstruction from six parameters is one formula). Risk: with the textbook fallback the figure is labelled textbook and the sponsor's data ask stands, as in D2/D3.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, with reference numbers

- **Portfolio moments.** μ_P = Σ Wᵢμᵢ; σ_P² = Σᵢⱼ WᵢWⱼρᵢⱼσᵢσⱼ (Wilmott 18.2–18.3; Hull 16.2). Two assets: σ² = W²σ_A² + 2W(1−W)ρσ_Aσ_E + (1−W)²σ_E² — a hyperbola in (σ, μ); straight at ρ = ±1; touches σ = 0 at ρ = −1 with W = σ_E/(σ_A + σ_E). Minimum-variance frontier for target m (short sales allowed): w = Σ⁻¹(λ1 + γμ) with λ = (C − Bm)/D, γ = (Am − B)/D, A = 1ᵀΣ⁻¹1, B = 1ᵀΣ⁻¹μ, C = μᵀΣ⁻¹μ, D = AC − B². Tangency (market) portfolio with rate r: w ∝ Σ⁻¹(μ − r1). No-short frontier by a small QP (or a grid over the simplex for ≤ 5 assets — exact enough for the picture, and the test compares to the unconstrained curve where no weight is negative).
- **Wilmott Figure 18.4 (test).** μ = (10, 15, 18, 20) %, σ = (20, 30, 25, 35) %, ρ₁₂ 0.2, ρ₁₃ 0.3, ρ₁₄ 0.1, ρ₂₃ 0.05, ρ₂₄ 0.1, ρ₃₄ 0.05; target 30 % → w = (−1.463705, 0.393636, 1.334432, 0.735636), σ_P = 45.644 % (book: −1.46371, 0.393638, 1.334433, 0.735637, 45.6 %).
- **Hull §16.3 (test, shared with E1).** $10 m at 2 %/day and $5 m at 1 %/day, ρ 0.3: σ_P = $220,227/day; benefit of diversification $219,369 on the 10-day 99 % figures.
- **PCA.** Σ = Σᵢ λᵢ eᵢeᵢᵀ, λ₁ ≥ … ≥ λ_k ≥ 0, eᵢᵀeᵢ = 1; PC scores yᵢ = eᵢᵀr, Var(yᵢ) = λᵢ, Cov(yᵢ,yⱼ) = 0; Σλᵢ = trace Σ (Tsay 9.13); share λᵢ/Σλ. On the correlation matrix the shares are λᵢ/k. Reconstruction with k factors: r̂ = Σᵢ≤k (eᵢᵀr) eᵢ; the residual variance is Σᵢ>k λᵢ. Apply to *changes* (bp per day), demeaned.
- **Tsay Example 9.1 (test).** Correlation matrix rows (1; .42 1; .30 .45 1; .18 .36 .26 1; .18 .38 .24 .79 1): eigenvalues 2.456, 1.146, 0.695, 0.495, 0.209 (shares 49.1, 22.9, 13.9, 9.9, 4.2 %); e₁ = (0.342, 0.475, 0.390, 0.502, 0.503); e₂ = (0.529, 0.311, 0.399, −0.481, −0.482) (book: 2.456 / 1.145; e₁ (0.342, 0.474, 0.387, 0.503, 0.505); e₂ (0.525, 0.314, 0.405, −0.481, −0.481) — agree to the rounding of a printed correlation matrix). Covariance-matrix version also printed in Tsay Table 9.3 (not reproduced here; the key can).
- **Frye / Hull §16.9 (test).** Factor-score sds (17.49, 6.05, 3.10, 2.17, 1.97, 1.69, 1.27, 1.24, 0.80, 0.79) bp → total variance 367.97; shares 83.13 %, 93.08 % (two), 95.9 % (three). PC1 loadings (0.21, 0.26, 0.32, 0.35, 0.36, 0.36, 0.36, 0.34, 0.31, 0.25); PC2 (−0.57, −0.49, −0.32, −0.10, 0.02, 0.14, 0.17, 0.27, 0.30, 0.33); PC3 (0.50, 0.23, −0.37, −0.38, −0.30, −0.12, −0.04, 0.15, 0.28, 0.46); one sd of PC1 moves the 3 m rate 0.21 × 17.49 = 3.67 bp and the 6 m rate 4.55 bp. ## 7. Where it connects

Back: A2 (the shear *is* the covariance matrix; its ellipse's axes are the PCs), A5 (σ estimates are the one input Wilmott trusts), E1 (the linear model's σ_P and Hull 16.9's factor VaR; this is where E1's "forty positions" book becomes three numbers), E2 (Cholesky and PCA are the two ways to factor Σ; PCA of the Brownian covariance gives the principal paths used for quasi-Monte Carlo). Forward: F (credit portfolios: one factor, Vasicek, is PCA with k = 1 by assumption), G (rates: a one-factor short-rate model moves only PC1, which is why swaption desks want two- and three-factor models), the user's own treasury work (a G-sec book's DV01 ladder → PC1/PC2 exposure is the first thing a rates risk report shows). The real-world moment: a risk report line "DV01 by tenor" next to "PC1/PC2/PC3 exposure", and the day the book is flat on DV01 and loses money because the curve twisted.
