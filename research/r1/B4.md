# B4 Itô's lemma

_R1 deep note, 2026-10-23. Options for discussion, not decisions. Assumes B3's "squares dial" (Σ(ΔW)² reads the clock) and 0.1's F2/F7 (Taylor; zoom until straight)._

## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling (one sentence, plain words — no symbols)

Itô's lemma is the Taylor expansion you already know with one extra term kept, and the term stays because a random wiggle, squared, does not shrink to nothing — it adds up to elapsed time — so a curved function of a random path picks up a steady drift from its curvature alone.

Secondary: the sign of that drift is the sign of the curvature (convex gains, concave loses — Mel's drag is concavity of the log); the three box rules are a bookkeeping summary of which terms survive a sum; the noise term of the function is just the ordinary chain rule.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**(a) The puzzle: is d(W²) = 2W dW?** Wilmott asks it in those words (p. 78: "If F = X² is it true that dF = 2X dX? No."). The fresher checks it in one line: B3 says E[W_T²] = T, but 2∫W dW is a sum of fair bets with expectation 0. Something worth exactly T is missing. Guess-then-reveal: "where did T go?" — then the ledger of (ΔW)² rows. Shreve, p. 137: "If the term −½t were not present, we would not have a martingale." Two lines, checkable by simulation, and the answer is the chapter.

**(b) The story with stakes: the simulation that overprices by 8 %.** A1 found Mel's median at 98.76 by Taylor-expanding log(1 + r) to the −½r² term. A desk that writes S_T = S₀ e^{μT + σW_T} — forgetting that term — overstates the average price by e^{½σ²T}: 2 % at σ = 20 %, 8.3 % at σ = 40 % over a year. Shreve, p. 148: "One can incorrectly argue from this formula that since Brownian motion is a martingale … the mean rate of return for S(t) must be α − ½σ². The error in this argument is that although W(t) is a martingale, S(0)e^{σW(t)} is not. The convexity of the function e^{σx} imparts an upward drift." riskhub's "omit the drift correction" lab (R0 audit) turns this into a counter that moves away from 1 as σ grows. The stake is a real bug a fresher can write in week two.

**(c) The everyday object: a square whose side jitters.** 3Blue1Brown's nudged square (`_2017/eoc/chapter3.py`, `NudgeSideLengthOfSquare`): two thin strips plus a corner square that is "Safe to ignore". Now let the side move by a coin flip of size ±√Δt instead of a fixed dx: the strips flip sign and cancel on average; the corner square is always positive and its area is Δt on average — the sticker gets crossed out. A picture with a place for every term, and the extra one has a home. Hellestal's lay version (Straight Dope): "Any concavity or convexity in the function is a big deal"; a run of good flips is "not necessarily as helpful to the function as a random series of 'bad' flips can be hurtful."

**Strongest: (a)** as the opener — it is short, it is a guess the fresher gets wrong, and it needs nothing but B3. **(b)** is the stakes section with the break-it button; **(c)** is the main figure (§5).

## 3. The sources, actually reopened

### Books

- **Hull OFOD 5e §11.6–11.7, pp. 226–228, and Appendix 11A, pp. 232–233 (PDF 241–243, 247–248).** States the lemma for dx = a dt + b dz as dG = (G_x a + G_t + ½G_xx b²)dt + G_x b dz (11.12), and for GBM (11.14); "A completely rigorous proof of Itô's lemma is beyond the scope of this book" (p. 226). Two worked examples: the forward price F = Se^{r(T−t)} (11.15) → dF = (μ − r)F dt + σF dz (11.16, p. 227) — "The growth rate in F is the excess return of S over the risk-free rate" — and G = ln S with G_S = 1/S, G_SS = −1/S², G_t = 0 → dG = (μ − σ²/2)dt + σ dz (11.17, p. 227), hence ln S_T ~ φ(ln S₀ + (μ − σ²/2)T, σ√T) (11.19, p. 228). Hull's point to keep: "both S and G are affected by the same underlying source of uncertainty, dz" (p. 226). **Appendix 11A** is the Taylor-expansion derivation: the two-variable expansion (11A.6), the discretised δx = a δt + b ε√δt (11A.7), then "δx² = b²ε²δt + terms of higher order in δt (11A.8). This shows that the term involving δx² in equation (11A.6) has a component that is of order δt and cannot be ignored"; E(ε²) = 1 so "the expected value of ε²δt is therefore δt. It can be shown that the variance of ε²δt is of order δt² and that, as a result of this, we can treat ε²δt as nonstochastic and equal to its expected value of δt as δt tends to zero" (p. 233), giving (11A.9). Good for a beginner: short, honest, exactly "Taylor plus one surviving term". Bad: the "can be shown" hides the sum-vs-term subtlety that Shreve's Remark 3.4.4 makes explicit.
- **Shreve SCF II §4.4, pp. 137–153 (PDF 153–169).** §4.4.1: "Because W has nonzero quadratic variation, the correct formula has an extra term" (4.4.1, p. 137); the sketch of proof for f(x) = ½x², where "Taylor's formula to second order is exact" (p. 138), summing over a partition (4.4.5–4.4.7, p. 139) with the sum of cubes dying (Exercise 3.4); the time-dependent expansion (4.4.8–4.4.9, p. 140) and the bounds showing the dt·dW and dt² sums vanish (4.4.10–4.4.11, p. 141); **Remark 4.4.2 is the box table**: dW dW = dt, dt dW = dW dt = 0, dt dt = 0 (4.4.12, p. 141). **Figure 4.4.1 (p. 142)** draws the first-order error as the gap between the tangent line and the curve at W(t_{j+1}), "due to the convexity of the function f"; and the paragraph beneath is the clearest statement anywhere of *why* the second-order term is needed: "before we let ‖Π‖ → 0, we must first sum these equations over j, and the smaller we make ‖Π‖, the more terms there are in the sum. When we sum both sides of (4.4.14), the errors accumulate, and although the error in each summand approaches zero as ‖Π‖ → 0, the sum of the errors does not. When we use the more accurate approximation (4.4.15), this does not happen" (pp. 142–143). ½W²(T) = ∫W dW + ½T (p. 143; 4.3.8 p. 137). **Remark 4.4.7 (p. 147)** gives the rule a fresher can carry: write the Taylor expansion "out to first order for every argument that has zero quadratic variation … and … out to second order for every argument that has nonzero quadratic variation"; "Itô calculus is little more than repeated use of this formula." Example 4.4.8 GBM (4.4.26–4.4.28, pp. 147–148) with the "incorrectly argue" paragraph quoted in §2. Example 4.4.10 Vasicek: f_xx = 0, so no correction (p. 151) — a useful "when the extra term is zero" case. Bad for a beginner: the measure-theoretic surround; good: every sentence about *why* is quotable.
- **Wilmott on Quantitative Finance §4.7–4.14, pp. 75–89 (PDF 96–110).** §4.7: the integrand "is evaluated in the summation at the left-hand point … non-anticipatory. In financial terms … we take some action such as choosing a portfolio and only then does the stock price move" (p. 76). §4.8: "d Something = Deterministic dt + Random dX" (p. 76). §4.10: Figure 4.3 (a Brownian path and its square on one plot), "My derivation is more heuristic than rigorous, but at least it is transparent"; the timescale h = δt/n, Taylor at each small step, the second-order sum "in the mean square sense" → ½F″δt, boxed result (4.5) dF = F′dX + ½F″dt; F = X²: dF = 2X dX + dt and ∫X dX = ½X² − ½t (pp. 78–80). §4.11 Figure 4.4 (a stock path above, an option value below): "dV = ___ dt + ___ dX. The question is then: What are the underlined bits? And that is precisely what Itô tells us" (pp. 80–81). **§4.12 "Itô and Taylor" is the passage the brief calls "in a nutshell"** (no section with that title exists in this edition; §4.12–4.12.1 is the equivalent): "If we were to do a naive Taylor series expansion of F, completely disregarding the nature of X … This is very similar to (4.5) (and Taylor series *is* very similar to Itô), with the only difference being that there is a dX² instead of a dt. However, since in a sense ∫₀ᵗ(dX)² = t I could perhaps write dX² = dt. (4.6) Although this lacks any rigor (because it's wrong) it does give the correct result … you can, with little risk of error, use Taylor series with the 'rule of thumb' (4.6) and in practice you will get the right result" (p. 82). §4.12.1 *The Intuition*: add N normals of sd √(δt/N) and get sd √δt — "Notice how the N disappears"; add their N *squares* and get mean N(√(δt/N))² = δt with sd δt√(2/N), which "tends to zero as N gets larger … because the randomness as measured by the standard deviation disappears leaving us with just the mean, dt" (pp. 82–83). §4.13: the two-factor rules dX₁² = dt, dX₂² = dt, dX₁dX₂ = ρ dt (p. 84). §4.14.2: F = log S → dF = (μ − ½σ²)dt + σ dX, "log S can range between minus and plus infinity but cannot reach these limits in a finite time, therefore S cannot reach zero or infinity" (p. 85). Good: the warmest and most honest treatment, and the "N squares" paragraph is a storyboard. Bad: no figure of the mechanism itself.

### Common misconceptions

- "(dW)² = dt is true step by step." No: the ratio is χ²₁ (Shreve Remark 3.4.4; Wilmott "because it's wrong"; riskhub). True inside a sum/integral.
- "The second-order term is a small correction." It is the *whole* drift of ln S and the whole of gamma P&L (C4/D1); Hull 11A.8: "cannot be ignored".
- "W is a martingale, so e^{σW} has no drift." Shreve p. 148 — convexity imparts drift; the ½σ² exponent fixes it.
- "Itô changes the noise." The dW coefficient is the ordinary chain rule f′·b; the same dz drives S and G (Hull p. 226).
- "dt·dW matters too." Its sum is bounded by max|ΔW| · ∫|f_tx| → 0 (Shreve 4.4.10); so do (dW)³ sums (p. 139).
- "Where you evaluate the integrand doesn't matter." It does for ∫H dW: left endpoint = non-anticipating (Wilmott p. 76); a peeking weight leaves ≈ 0.484 (riskhub). (It does not matter for ∫H (dW)², riskhub §7.)
- "The rule of thumb is the theorem." Wilmott: it is a reliable heuristic; the theorem is the integral form (Shreve 4.4.2).
- "If f″ = 0 the lemma is different." It is the ordinary chain rule (Vasicek, Shreve p. 151).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three different modes

### C1 — Tiles that shrink at different speeds (symbol-into-picture derivation, step-through)

Mode: Back / Reset / Forward step-through (catalogue B4) in which every term of the expansion is a tile whose area is its size.

1. Reprise 0.1's F2: f(x + Δx) − f(x) = f′Δx + ½f″(Δx)² + …, as three tiles (a bar of width Δx, a square of side Δx, a sliver). Press "halve Δx" three times: the square shrinks four times faster than the bar. "Safe to ignore" — the 0.1 lesson.
2. Substitute Δx = aΔt + bΔW. Multiply out: tiles (Δt)², Δt·ΔW, (ΔW)². The ΔW tile is drawn with a coin on it: its side is ±√Δt.
3. "Halve Δt" four times with the tile areas printed (expected areas, with a "show one sample" toggle): the Δt bar halves; (Δt)² tile quarters; Δt·ΔW tile shrinks by 2.8; **the (ΔW)² tile halves — the same rate as the Δt bar beside it.** (Hull 11A.8; MIT: "this one survives because it's equal to dt".)
4. The kill: tiles that shrink faster than Δt fade with a "Safe to ignore" sticker; the b²(ΔW)² tile keeps its sticker-less place, labelled "≈ b²Δt *on average*".
5. "Now sum over N steps": the surviving tiles stack into columns; the (ΔW)² column is the B3 squares dial and reads T (Wilmott's "N squares": mean δt, sd δt√(2/N)).
6. The box rules appear as the *summary* of what survived: a 2×2 table dt·dt = 0, dt·dW = 0, dW·dW = dt (Shreve 4.4.12), each cell flown from its tile.
7. The formula assembles term by term, colour-locked to its tile: df = (f_t + a f_x + ½b²f_xx)dt + b f_x dW (Hull 11.12).
8. Worked example f = ln S, a = μS, b = σS: f′ = 1/S, f″ = −1/S² → the tile ½(−1/S²)(σS)² = −½σ² lights up red; result (μ − ½σ²)dt + σ dW (Hull 11.17; Wilmott p. 85). A1's 98.76 appears in the margin.

Makes visible: the *order* of every term as an area shrinking at its own speed; "negligible" as a race you watch; the box rules as a conclusion rather than an axiom. Cost: medium — the tile layout and a reconfigurable derivation (B83) that re-flows when f is changed (ln S, S², e^S, √S from Hull Problem 11.15). Risk: areas of *random* tiles confuse; mitigated by the "expected area" label, the one-sample toggle and the B3 dial reappearing at beat 5.

### C2 — The nudged square with a jittery side (physical picture, explorable)

Mode: physical picture with a coin button and a Δt dial; the 3b1b scene inverted.

1. The deterministic square, verbatim from `NudgeSideLengthOfSquare`: side 3, nudge 0.25 then 0.01; strips 2·3·0.01 = 0.06, corner 0.0001; "Safe to ignore" circled in red. (Thirty seconds; the fresher has seen this logic in 0.1.)
2. The side now moves by a coin: +√Δt or −√Δt. Flip once: heads adds two yellow strips and a yellow corner; tails *removes* two strips (drawn blue, negative) but still adds a yellow corner. The corner is positive either way.
3. Flip twenty times, fast. Two running totals on dials: strips (yellow minus blue) wander around 0 like a random walk; corners climb steadily at rate 1 per unit time — the B3 dial again.
4. Turn the Δt dial down. Each strip shrinks by √2 but there are twice as many: the strip total is as random as before. Each corner halves and there are twice as many: the corner total is unchanged, t. Cross out "Safe to ignore".
5. The equation written with the shapes as its terms (the 3b1b move): d(x²) = 2x dx + dt. Check panel: x_T² − 2Σx·dx = Σ(dx)², exact at every Δt by telescoping, and → T.
6. Generalise: a curved f instead of x². The nudge region under the slope line is a rectangle f′dx plus a triangle ½f″(dx)² (`SecondTermIntuition`); with a coin-flip dx the triangle's expected area is ½f″Δt and has the sign of f″. Convex (e^x): the triangle adds — Shreve's "convexity imparts an upward drift". Concave (ln x): it subtracts — Mel's drag.
7. Pay-off: f = ln S with S's jitter σS√Δt → triangle −½σ²Δt; S = 100, σ = 40 %: median 102.02 vs mean 110.52 at one year.
8. Break-it button (common random numbers, two fans): "simulate without the −½σ²" — the readout mean(S_T)/S₀e^{μT} drifts to 1.020 (σ = 20 %), 1.083 (σ = 40 %); with the term, it stays at 1.00.

Makes visible: where the extra term *lives* geometrically; why its sign is the sign of the curvature; that the strips (first order) carry the randomness and the corners (second order) carry the drift. Cost: low–medium — 2D shapes, a coin, two dials, one small simulation. Risk: a fresher may think the result is special to squares; beat 6 is mandatory. Negative strips need a clear colour convention (blue = removed).

### C3 — The Sceptic and the Accountant (drawn-character dialogue; illustrated text)

Mode: a short comic dialogue between two characters, with static ledgers and one tiny simulation.

1. The Sceptic writes the chain rule for W² → 2W dW: "I did second-order terms in 0.1; they vanish."
2. The Accountant: "Then E[W_T²] = 0. B3 says it is T. Where did T go?"
3. The Sceptic: "(ΔW)² is tiny — ignore it." The Accountant opens a ledger: N rows of (ΔW)²; the column sum ≈ T whatever N is.
4. The Sceptic: "But each row is nowhere near Δt!" The Accountant: "68 % of rows are below their mean; the total still lands within √(2/N). It's an insurer's book, not a ruler." (Shreve Remark 3.4.4; riskhub.)
5. The Sceptic: "So just write dW² = dt and move on?" The Accountant: "Inside a sum, yes. Outside one it's false." (Wilmott p. 82.)
6. The Sceptic tries to cheat: counts a row only after seeing it. Persistent error 0.484. "No peeking" = non-anticipating (Wilmott p. 76).
7. Together they write the box table.
8. Epilogue: the Sceptic re-derives ln S, finds −½σ², and recognises Mel's 98.76.

Makes visible: the objections in the fresher's own head, answered in the order they arise; the sum-not-term subtlety, which no single picture carries. Cost: low — illustrated text with two drawn characters and static ledgers; one 20-line simulation panel. Risk: dialogue can patronise; the Sceptic must be right about everything except the sum, and the whole piece must fit on one screen.

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right, and reference numbers for tests

Statements (with source):
- Box rules: dW dW = dt, dt dW = dW dt = 0, dt dt = 0 (Shreve 4.4.12, p. 141; 3.4.10–3.4.14, pp. 105–106). Two factors: dX₁dX₂ = ρ dt (Wilmott p. 84).
- Itô-Doeblin for Brownian motion: df(t, W) = f_t dt + f_x dW + ½f_xx dt (Shreve 4.4.13, p. 141; Wilmott 4.5, p. 80). For an Itô process dX = Θ dt + Δ dW: df = f_t dt + f_x dX + ½f_xx (dX)² with (dX)² = Δ² dt (Shreve 4.4.23–4.4.24, p. 147). Hull's form for dx = a dt + b dz: dG = (G_x a + G_t + ½G_xx b²)dt + G_x b dz (11.12, p. 226). Integral form is the meaningful one (Shreve 4.4.2, p. 137).
- Derivation: Taylor (Hull 11A.6, p. 232; Shreve 4.4.8–4.4.9, p. 140; Wilmott p. 79); δx² = b²ε²δt + o(δt) (Hull 11A.8); E[ε²] = 1, Var[ε²δt] = 2δt² (Hull p. 233; Shreve 3.4.7); the sums of dt·dW, dt² and |dW|³ terms vanish (Shreve 4.4.10–4.4.11, p. 141; p. 139).
- Worked examples: f = W² → d(W²) = 2W dW + dt, ∫₀ᵗ W dW = ½W² − ½t (Wilmott p. 80; Shreve 4.3.8, p. 137, and p. 143). f = ln S under dS = μS dt + σS dz → d ln S = (μ − ½σ²)dt + σ dz, ln S_T ~ φ(ln S₀ + (μ − ½σ²)T, σ√T) (Hull 11.17–11.19, pp. 227–228; Wilmott p. 85).
