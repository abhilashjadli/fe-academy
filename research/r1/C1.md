## 1. What a fresher must walk away feeling

An option is a paid-for choice that you only use when it helps you, so what it is worth at the end is a bent line — and two bundles that will be worth the same whatever happens must cost the same today.

## 2. Why a fresher should care (three hook candidates)

**A puzzle: the pattern hiding in a price sheet.** Natenberg prints a real-looking option table (p. 13): underlying at 99.00, two months left, strikes 85 … 115, calls 14.05 / 9.35 / 5.50 / 2.70 / 1.15 / .45 / .20, puts .10 / .45 / 1.55 / 3.70 / 7.10 / 11.35 / 16.10. Ask the fresher: subtract each put from its call. They get 13.95, 8.90, 3.95, −1.00, −5.95, −10.90, −15.90 — and beside it 99 − K is 14, 9, 4, −1, −6, −11, −16. Every row agrees to within ten cents. *Why?* Nobody told the market to do this; the chapter ends by explaining that it had no choice. A pattern the learner finds with their own subtraction is the strongest kind of hook for an engineer.

**A story with stakes: the baker who wanted to walk away.** Wilmott opens his derivatives chapter with the sentence the whole topic turns on: "The holder of future or forward contracts is obliged to trade at the maturity of the contract … *Wouldn't it be nice if we only had to take possession of the asset if it had risen in value?*" (p. 25). A farmer and a baker shake hands on 100 a sack for next spring — both are on the hook. Then the baker asks for something new: "I'll pay you 8 today for the *right* to buy at 100; if wheat is cheaper I'll just go to market." Now only the farmer is on the hook, and he wants the 8 up front. The CIBC transcript does the same with a house: a friend pays for the right to buy your $750,000 house at $800,000 within 12 months; at $900,000 "you are obligated to sell your house to her for the agreed upon $800,000" and "payment for the right is called a premium." The story makes the asymmetry a *person*, which is exactly what a fresher does not get from max(S − K, 0).

**An everyday object: an insurance policy.** Hull himself says it: "A call option, when held instead of the stock itself, in effect insures the holder against the stock price falling below the exercise price" (p. 177); for puts, "a put option, when held in conjunction with the stock, insures the holder against the stock price falling below a certain level." The bookdown.org note maps the two side by side — insured asset ↔ underlying, insured period ↔ time to expiry, value insured ↔ strike, *deductible ↔ how far out-of-the-money*, insurance premium ↔ option premium. The deductible-as-moneyness mapping is the one that makes "cheap far-OTM puts" click instantly. Natenberg (p. 38) even defends the gambling analogy by retreating to this one: "think of them as insurance policies which require the payment of a premium."

**Strongest: the story, used first and short (≤ 90 s), with insurance as the running metaphor for puts and the price-sheet puzzle as the mid-chapter reveal.** The story answers *why this contract exists* (take the obligation away from one side) before any payoff picture, which is the part Hull, OIC and Investopedia all skip; the puzzle then gives the parity section a reason to exist.

### Common misconceptions (with the source that corrects them)

- *"A call is a bet the stock goes up; if it does, I win."* Natenberg p. 36: "not only must he be right about market direction, he must also be right about market speed." Break-even is strike + premium (Natenberg Fig. 2-2: 102.70), and the stock can rise and the call still lose.
- *"Never exercise if you'd lose money overall."* Hull p. 7: exercising at 62 loses 300, not exercising loses 500.
- *"The strike is the price of the option."* Lightspeed's definitions; Natenberg's Figure 1-1 separates the four fields.
- *"Price = chance of profit"* or *"time value is the value of waiting".* Natenberg p. 37: value = expected payoff less carrying cost; an OTM option's price "consists solely of time value" (p. 7) — time value is the price of *uncertainty*, not of time.
- *"The payoff diagram is my P&L today."* Wilmott p. 36: it "only tell[s] you about what happens at expiry"; the curve before expiry sits above it (Hull Fig. 8.3) and "must converge to the payoff function" (Wilmott p. 30).
- *"Covered calls are free income."* Hull p. 187: by parity a covered call *is* a short put plus cash.
- *"Parity holds for American options."* Hull p. 175: only European; American gets the two-sided bound (8.4).
- *"An option could be worth more than the stock / a put more than its strike."* The ceilings c ≤ S₀, p ≤ K (Hull p. 171).
- *"Forward and option are the same thing."* The story's whole point: one side keeps the obligation, the other buys out of it (Natenberg p. 1; Wilmott p. 25).

## 4. Three illustration concepts, in three different modes

### Concept A — Drawn-character story: "The baker who wanted to walk away" (≤ 90 s, 8 panels, autoplay with a scrubber)

1. A baker needs wheat next spring; a farmer fears a glut. They shake hands on 100 a sack. Caption: *a forward: both are bound.*
2. Two springs side by side: wheat at 130 (baker grins, farmer winces) and at 70 (reversed). Under them the forward's payoff draws itself: a straight 45° line through 100 (Natenberg Fig. 2-1).
3. The baker: "What if I pay you 8 *now* for the right to buy at 100 — and if wheat is cheaper I go to market?" The farmer: "Then I alone am bound. Eight up front, please."
4. The right half of the line stays; at 100 the left half bends flat. At the kink both arrows appear — *exercise* / *walk away* — and the worse one dims (the GaltonBoard move). Caption: *the bend is a decision.*
5. Lower the line by 8: the profit picture; break-even 108. Caption: *the premium is the price of the choice.*
6. Flip: the farmer's picture is the mirror image (flat then falling) — he collected 8 and is on the hook. Wilmott p. 37 quoted.
7. The farmer buys the *other* right — to sell at 100 — from the miller. The insurance table slides in: premium / deductible / cover ↔ premium / moneyness / strike.
8. Hand-over card: *call, put, strike, expiry, premium, writer* in one row, each word pointing at a panel.

*Makes visible:* why the contract exists, who is obligated, and that the kink is a choice. *Costs:* eight character panels plus simple line morphs; low engineering, medium illustration effort. *Risk:* a fresher may think options are private deals; panel 8 must cut straight to the exchange-traded chain of Concept B. Physical delivery of wheat is fine as a story but must not be the chapter's mental model of settlement.

### Concept B — Annotated real artefact: the option chain you can interrogate

A chain drawn in our visual system from Natenberg's p. 13 numbers (underlying 99.00, two months; strikes 85–115), laid out as Lightspeed describes (calls left, strikes centre, puts right, ITM half shaded). Every number is hoverable; a side panel explains it in words and draws its tiny payoff.

1. Hover 99.00: *the underlying — the only number here that is a thing, not a right.*
2. Hover the 100 call, 2.70: *the price today of the right to buy at 100. Intrinsic 0, time 2.70 — all of it is uncertainty* (Natenberg p. 7). Hover the 90 call, 9.35: *intrinsic 9, time 0.35.* The shading explained as geography: ITM calls left of the price, ITM puts right (Natenberg Fig. 1-7).
3. Drag a marker down the strike column: a small plot of call price vs strike falls, put price rises (Wilmott p. 29). Two bounds appear as thin lines on that plot: *the floor* S − K and *the ceiling* S (Hull p. 171–172).
4. Button *subtract*: a new column C − P appears: 13.95, 8.90, 3.95, −1.00, −5.95, −10.90, −15.90; beside it 99 − K. Caption: *why?* (predict-then-run: the learner types a guess before the next beat).
5. Stack the legs at one strike: the long-call picture and the short-put picture stack tip-to-tail (the Fourier move) into a straight line through K — the forward from Concept A. Caption: *a call minus a put is a forward* (Natenberg p. 220).
6. The stock version with the small correction: c − p = S − Ke^{−rT} (Hull eq. 8.3); a toggle *interest on* shows the ten-cent residuals in the table closing. Then the "two baskets" card: call + cash K vs put + share, three columns (S_T above / below / at K), both worth max(S_T, K) (Hull p. 174; quantt table).
7. **Break it:** type the 100 put at 3.95 instead of 3.70. The conversion table fills itself (Natenberg p. 217 style): buy underlying −99.00, sell call +2.70, buy put −3.95, exercise/assignment at 100 → +100.00; total +0.25 *whatever happens*; a "whatever happens" strip shows the total at S_T = 80, 99, 120. Hull's stock version (S = 31, K = 30, 3 months, 10 %: profit 1.02) as the second preset.
8. Closing card: the chain re-shaded by moneyness, with Figure 8.3's curve above the kink named *time value*, and a pointer to C2 ("where 2.70 comes from").

*Makes visible:* that parity sits in every real price sheet; moneyness as a map; bounds as floor and ceiling lines on real numbers. *Costs:* medium — a table, a hover panel, a mini payoff plot, one stacking animation, one cash-flow table; data static. *Risk:* tables bore freshers if the C − P reveal comes late — it must be beat 4, not beat 8; and the futures-style chain (no discounting) must be labelled honestly before the e^{−rT} appears.

### Concept C — Game: "Build the payoff" (stack legs to match a target)

A target payoff (grey) and a shelf of legs: long/short call/put at 90, 95, 100, 105, 110, the underlying, cash. Legs drawn dashed, the running total solid (Hull's convention). Score = shaded area between total and target; Natenberg's three rules appear as hints.

1. Level 1 — match a long 100 call (one leg; learn the shelf).
2. Level 2 — match a straight line through 100 with no underlying on the shelf: the learner discovers long call + short put. Reveal: *that is put–call parity, built by hand.*
3. Level 3 — target is a covered call (stock + short call); the shelf has no stock: short put + cash matches it (Hull p. 187).
4. Level 4 — the V of a straddle (Hull p. 194 numbers: cost 7, break-evens 63 and 77).
5. Level 5 — the butterfly spike (Hull Fig. 9.13); then *free draw*: paint any payoff and watch it decompose into a row of spikes ("any payoff function can be approximated", Hull p. 197).
6. Each level ends with the rule that would have solved it ("if the graph bends, it bends at a strike").

*Makes visible:* payoffs add pointwise; strategies are sums; parity is a construction, not a formula. *Costs:* medium-high — drag-and-drop, live sum, a small decomposition solver for level 5. *Risk:* scoring invites trial-and-error without understanding; mitigate with "say which legs before you place them" prompts and a four-leg cap. Existing builders (wallstreetuni, OptionAlpha, drwong) prove the form but none narrates — the narration is the only reason to build another.

(A fourth, folded in: a two-panel **comic strip** for parity — "two shopping baskets" — is Concept B beat 6 drawn as characters; it would use the Pythagorean dissection move. Not a separate build.)

## 5. My recommendation

**A then B, with C's levels 1–4 embedded inside B as an un-scored "stack the legs" widget at beat 5.** Judged in the sponsor's order: *easy* — a 90-second story and a labelled price sheet put no formula on screen until beat 6; *interesting* — the learner subtracts two columns and finds a law; *interactive* — hover, drag, stack, break. The game as the primary form loses on "easy": a fresher who does not yet know what a call is cannot be asked to build one, and scores reward guessing. Bounds get one illustrated-text panel with Hull's two arbitrage tables (p. 172–173) as a break-it preset; early exercise gets one panel (Hull p. 177's "two reasons") and defers the American put to C2. Natenberg's roulette-and-five-prices panel (pp. 37–41) closes the chapter as the bridge: *the premium is an average of the bent line over where the price might land, less carrying cost — C2 shows which average.*

## 6. Mathematics the chapter must get right

Notation: S₀ spot, S_T price at expiry, K strike, T years, r continuously compounded, c/p European, C/P American; no dividends unless stated.

- Payoffs: long call max(S_T − K, 0); short call min(K − S_T, 0); long put max(K − S_T, 0); short put min(S_T − K, 0) (Hull pp. 9–10). Profit = payoff − premium, ignoring the time value of money (Hull p. 185, stated assumption). Break-even: K + c for a call, K − p for a put.
- Intrinsic value max(S₀ − K, 0) / max(K − S₀, 0); time value = price − intrinsic (Natenberg pp. 6–7). Moneyness: ITM / ATM / OTM.
- Bounds (Hull §8.3): c ≤ S₀, C ≤ S₀; p ≤ K, P ≤ K, p ≤ Ke^{−rT}; c ≥ max(S₀ − Ke^{−rT}, 0) (8.1); p ≥ max(Ke^{−rT} − S₀, 0) (8.2). American: S₀ − K ≤ C − P ≤ S₀ − Ke^{−rT} (8.4, p. 175). Assumptions (§8.2): no transaction costs, one tax rate, borrow and lend at r, r > 0, no arbitrage.
- Put–call parity: c + Ke^{−rT} = p + S₀ (Hull 8.3, p. 174); with dividends c + Ke^{−rT} + D = p + S₀ (9.1, p. 186); futures / no-interest form C − P = F − K (Natenberg p. 220; CME). Holds for European options only.
- Forward price F = S₀e^{rT} (Natenberg p. 42 in words: 100 with 4 of carry → 104). Expected-value model: price = Σ pᵢ·payoffᵢ, discounted (Natenberg p. 41).
- Six factors and signs (Hull Table 8.1, p. 168): S₀ +/−, K −/+, T ?/? (European) +/+ (American), σ +/+, r +/−, dividends −/+.
